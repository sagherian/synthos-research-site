The Overview

Airbnb is a website where people who own a spare room, flat or house rent it to travellers. Airbnb owns none of the property. It takes a fee from both sides and does almost nothing else — 8,200 staff produce $12.2 billion of revenue, and the company spends about $8 million a quarter on equipment, which for a company this size is essentially nothing.

The economics are therefore superb. Eighty-three cents of every revenue dollar is gross profit. There is $8.7 billion of net cash on the balance sheet and no meaningful debt.

Two things are happening at once and they point in opposite directions.

The good one: growth has picked up sharply. Revenue rose 17.9% in the March quarter, against 10.3% for the whole of last year, and the value of everything booked on the platform rose 19%. That is the fastest the business has grown since travel reopened.

The less good one: none of that extra revenue reached profit last year. Airbnb's operating profit was $2.54 billion in 2025 and $2.55 billion in 2024 — flat — while revenue grew 10%. The money went into engineering and marketing, partly to build a new "Services and Experiences" business. And the company has now come in below what analysts expected three quarters in a row.

Two other facts you should know before deciding anything.

Airbnb reports results in two days.

And eight days ago, Joseph Gebbia — one of the three people who founded the company, still a director and a declared 10% owner — sold about 1.7 million shares for roughly $257 million, cutting the holding the filings show him having by 82%. That is not proof of anything; founders sell for many reasons and the other two remain in place. But it is a very large sale, very close to a results date.

You pay about 25 times what analysts expect Airbnb to earn in 2027 — about 22 times once you subtract the cash. For a business growing profits 18% a year with these margins, that is a fair price. We think it is worth about $162 against $150.

Eight per cent of upside, with a print in two days and three misses behind it, is not a reason to act.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.
