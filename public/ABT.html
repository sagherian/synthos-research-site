The Overview

Abbott makes four different kinds of thing: medical devices (heart valves, defibrillators, and — most importantly — the FreeStyle Libre glucose sensor that diabetics wear on their arm), diagnostic tests, infant and adult nutrition drinks, and generic medicines sold mainly in emerging markets.

The device business is doing very well. It has grown from $14.7 billion to $21.4 billion of sales in three years. The diagnostics business is doing badly, for an understandable reason: during the pandemic Abbott sold enormous quantities of COVID tests, and that revenue has gone. Diagnostics sales fell from $16.6 billion in 2022 to $8.9 billion last year.

In March, Abbott responded by spending about $20.6 billion buying Exact Sciences, which makes cancer-screening tests including Cologuard. It paid for the whole thing with borrowed money — $20 billion of new bonds issued in the same month, with the longest maturing in 2066.

That is the most important fact about this company right now, and it does not appear in the standard financial data. The commonly-available balance sheet is from last December and shows net debt of $6.5 billion. The company's own filing from three weeks ago shows $27.0 billion. Leverage went from very low to moderate in one transaction.

Meanwhile, reported profits are falling. In the first six months of this year Abbott earned $1.15 a share on a statutory basis, down from $1.77, even though sales rose 10.5%. The difference is interest on the new debt and the accounting cost of the acquisition.

The shares are $105.58. They are 23% below their high of a year ago, and they have bounced 21% in the last three months — hard enough that a standard momentum indicator reads 79 out of 100, which means "very overbought". They are also still below their 200-day average price.

Our estimate of value is $115. The average analyst says $123. The business is fine and the entry point is poor.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Was 4 six months ago. A $20 billion debt-funded acquisition changed that.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. The device business is genuinely good. The diagnostics recovery has been bought rather than earned.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. Glucose sensors and cancer screening are real adoption curves inside a half-company that has none.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $115 against a current price of $105.58 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Correction 1 — the balance sheet predates the largest financing event in Abbott's history, and the understatement is 313%. The vendor's most recent balance sheet is 2025-12-31, showing net debt of $6,545M. Between that date and 2026-06-30, Abbott issued $20.0 billion of senior notes and closed a $20.6 billion acquisition. The vendor reports net debt of $6.545B; the 10-Q supports $27.005B; we use $27.005B. Consequential: net debt/EBITDA rises from 0.66x to 2.72x; goodwill and intangibles rise from $29.561B to $52.455B — 48.0% of total assets; and total assets rise 25.9%. This is the CRM defect class (a stale balance sheet missing the largest financing event) in its largest absolute form in this batch.

Correction 2 — the vendor's own file is internally inconsistent by roughly $21 billion, which is worth stating precisely. bal_a[0].netDebt is $6,545M. But km_ttm.enterpriseValueTTM is $210.198B against a market capitalisation of $182.694B, implying net debt plus minority interests of $27.504B — i.e. the derived enterprise value DOES reflect the acquisition financing. netDebtToEBITDATTM of 2.766x likewise implies roughly $27.5B. So two parts of the same payload disagree by $21 billion: the balance-sheet block is stale and the trailing-metric block is current. A reader screening on the balance sheet would put Abbott at 0.66x leverage; a reader screening on the ratio block would put it at 2.77x. The ratio block is right. We state this because the balance sheet is the field most readers would trust.
