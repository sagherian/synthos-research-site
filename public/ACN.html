The Overview

Accenture is the company large organisations hire when they want to change how they work and cannot do it themselves. It employs about 799,000 people — roughly the population of a mid-sized city — and rents them out to build software, run computer systems, redesign supply chains, move companies onto cloud computing and, increasingly, install artificial-intelligence tools. Half its revenue comes from advice-and-build projects it calls consulting; the other half comes from running things for clients on multi-year contracts it calls managed services.

The way it makes money is simple and old. It hires a person, pays them a salary, and bills a client more than that salary. Multiply by 799,000 and keep 93% of those people busy on paying work, and you get $69.7 billion of revenue and about $12.6 billion of genuinely spare cash a year.

That model is now the subject of an argument, and the argument is why the shares have fallen by a third in a year while the stock market rose by a quarter. One side says artificial intelligence is the best thing that ever happened to Accenture: every large company on earth now has to rebuild itself around new technology, almost none of them can do it alone, and Accenture is the biggest firm that can. The other side says the opposite: if software can now do work that used to require a room full of junior consultants, then a business whose entire cost structure is a pyramid of junior consultants is exactly what gets squeezed.

Here is what the actual filings show, which is less dramatic than either story. Revenue per employee is going up, not down — about $91,500 over the last twelve months against about $86,600 the year before. Staff turnover has fallen. The proportion of staff on billable work is at 93%, the highest disclosed. The pile of already-signed, non-cancellable future work has grown from $34 billion to $38 billion in nine months. None of that is a business being eaten.

But there is one number going the wrong way, and it is the important one. New bookings — the value of contracts signed in the period, which tells you what revenue will look like in a year or two — fell 1% last fiscal year and fell 3% in the three months to May 2026, stripping out currency effects. Revenue is still growing because Accenture is working through business it won earlier. New business is not growing. In Accenture's own annual report, the company writes that some of the services its people perform "have been and will continue to be replaced by automation."

So what do you pay for that? About 11.6 times next year's expected earnings, which is roughly half what the shares fetched a year ago. The company has more cash than debt. It pays a dividend of 3.8% and is buying back $7.5 billion of its own shares this year — around 7% of the whole company. Add those up and you earn roughly 10% a year even if nothing improves and nobody changes their opinion of it.

The catch is timing. The shares have already jumped 37% from their low six weeks ago and are technically stretched, and the next real news is 51 days away. So the sensible thing is to buy part of a position now and keep the rest for either a pullback or for evidence, on 24 September, that new bookings have stopped shrinking. Our estimate of fair value is $190 against a price of $170.54.

1. What the business is, and how it actually reports

Identity. Accenture plc is incorporated in Ireland, headquartered at 1 Grand Canal Square, Dublin, and listed on the New York Stock Exchange. Chair and Chief Executive is Julie Spellman Sweet. The workforce was approximately 799,000 as of 31 May 2026 per the FQ3'26 10-Q, against 779,000 at 31 August 2025 and 791,000 at 31 May 2025 — the payload's fullTimeEmployees of 799,000 matches the filing exactly. The fiscal year ends 31 August, so "FY2025" means the twelve months to 31 August 2025 and the most recent reported quarter, FQ3'26, ended 31 May 2026.

Scale and client base. The 10-K discloses approximately 9,000 clients, of which 195 of the top 200 have been clients for ten or more years and 305 are "Diamond" relationships, the largest tier. The United States represented 45% of consolidated revenues in each of fiscal 2025, 2024 and 2023, and no other country reached 10%. Ireland, the domicile, is about 1%.

Three ways the company cuts its revenue — and the vendor confuses them. This matters, so it is set out precisely.

1. By geographic market — this is the reportable-segment basis. 10-K Note 16 states: "Our three reportable segments are our geographic markets: Americas, EMEA (Europe, Middle East and Africa) and Asia Pacific." The chief operating decision makers are the Chief Executive and Chief Financial Officer, who evaluate the segments on revenue and operating income. In the first quarter of fiscal 2025 the Latin America market unit moved from Growth Markets to North America; North America became "the Americas" and Growth Markets became "Asia Pacific," with prior periods reclassified.

2. By industry group — Health & Public Service; Communications, Media & Technology; Financial Services; Products; Resources. Used for commentary, not for segment reporting.

3. By type of work — consulting and managed services. Note that "outsourcing" is a retired label; every current filing says managed services.

The vendor's seg_geo is stale by two fiscal years and uses the retired names. Its most recent row is FY2023, showing Europe $21.285B, North America $30.296B and Growth Markets $12.531B. The vendor reports Europe / North America / Growth Markets through FY2023; the 10-K shows Americas / EMEA / Asia Pacific from FY2025 with prior periods restated; we use the 10-K basis. The FY2025 dollar split by geographic market cannot be reproduced here because the segment tables did not survive the filing text extraction (Section 8, item 10) — that is stated as a gap rather than estimated around.

The vendor's seg_prod mixes two different disaggregations across years. FY2025, FY2023, FY2022, FY2021 and FY2020 are type of work; FY2024, FY2019, FY2018 and FY2017 are industry group. Each year sums correctly to that year's revenue — FY2025's $35.107B consulting plus $34.566B managed services equals $69.673B exactly, and FY2024's five industry groups sum to $64.896B exactly — so there is no double-count. But it is not a time series and must not be read as one.

2. The most important thing to understand about these numbers — bookings versus revenue, and the currency wedge between them

Accenture's reported dollar revenue growth is the least informative number the company publishes, and the number that matters most is going the other way.

Three facts have to be held together.

First, roughly 2.5 points of fiscal-2026 reported growth is the dollar, not the business. The FQ3'26 10-Q states it plainly: "The U.S. dollar weakened against various currencies during the three and nine months ended May 31, 2026 ... resulting in favorable currency translation and U.S. dollar revenue growth that was approximately 2.5% and 2.7% higher, respectively, than our revenue growth in local currency." The company further estimates that full fiscal-2026 dollar growth will be about 2 points above local-currency growth. That is management framing about a future period and is half-weighted accordingly, but the 2.5 and 2.7 point figures for periods already reported are audited-period disclosures, not guidance.

Second, new bookings — the leading indicator — are declining. Accenture defines new bookings as new contracts including those acquired, plus renewals, extensions and changes to existing contracts, and warns explicitly that there are no third-party standards governing the calculation and that bookings are not comparable to revenue. With that caveat stated, this is the single most important disclosure the company makes, because managed-services bookings convert to revenue over years and therefore tell you what the revenue line looks like after the current backlog is worked off.

Third, the counterweight is equally documented. Remaining performance obligations — the non-cancellable portion of signed contracts, which the company is careful to say is only a fraction of what it counts as bookings — went $30B (31 Aug 2024) → $34B (31 Aug 2025) → $38B (31 May 2026). That is roughly 13% growth in FY2025 and a further 12% in nine months, and it is rising faster than revenue. Bookings falling while RPO rises is exactly what a mix shift from short cancellable consulting work toward long non-cancellable managed-services work looks like. It does not resolve the argument. It does mean the argument is not yet a rout.

Therefore: this dive treats local-currency bookings, not dollar revenue, as the primary trend indicator, and treats the 2026-09-24 print as the decisive scheduled observation.

11. Verdict, kill criteria and flip conditions

Buy — Tactical.

The case in one paragraph. Accenture generates about $12.58B of free cash flow a year on $600M of capital expenditure, earns a 25.0% return on equity, holds roughly $5.1B of net cash once the vendor's misclassified office leases are removed, and is priced at 11.62x consensus FY2027 adjusted earnings with a 12.06% free-cash-flow yield and a 3.82% dividend. It has committed to repurchasing $7.5B of stock in fiscal 2026 — 7.2% of the market value — and the collapse in the share price has roughly quadrupled the rate at which those dollars retire shares, with the diluted count down 1.7% in two quarters against 0.55% for the whole of FY2025. At a constant forward multiple that combination returns roughly 10% a year with no change of view required from anybody. The lagging operational measures are all pointing the right way: revenue per employee up 5.7%, utilisation at 93%, attrition down to 14%, remaining performance obligations up from $34B to $38B in nine months, adjusted operating margin up 20 basis points in the most recent quarter.

What you are accepting, stated without softening. New bookings — the leading indicator, and the disclosure Accenture itself describes as the best available trend information on new business — fell 1% in fiscal 2025 and 3% in local currency in the quarter ended 31 May 2026. Book-to-bill has gone from a derived 1.25x to 1.16x to 1.03x. Business optimisation charges have run three consecutive years. Research and development spend has fallen 38% in two years. Goodwill of $22.536B is 34.5% of total assets and tangible book is $8.48 a share. The U.S. federal business is under disclosed pressure. And the company's own 10-K risk factors state that it faces competition "including from our clients and ecosystem partners, who may develop their own internal AI-related capabilities, as well as new AI-native companies," and that "some services and tasks currently performed by our people have been and will continue to be replaced by automation." Our knowledge base is thin, split, and its only specific bullish call was made at the top and has been wrong by 40%.

And the entry is stretched. RSI 74.76 after a 37.0% rally off the low, 18.9% below a falling 200-day average, with the decisive disclosure 51 days away. Hence Tactical, hence staged: a third to a half now, the rest on a retest of the $150-158 zone or on evidence at the 24 September print.

Pre-registered KILL criteria — what would break the thesis:

  • Local-currency new bookings declining again in FQ4'26 (2026-09-24) and in FQ1'27, taking trailing book-to-bill below 1.0x. This is the primary criterion and it dominates the others.
  • Fiscal-2027 revenue guidance implying 2% or less local-currency growth, given the company's own estimate of roughly 2 points of currency benefit.
  • Revenue per employee falling year-on-year in local currency for two consecutive quarters — the direct mechanical signature of automation compressing the pyramid.
  • Utilisation falling below approximately 90% while headcount grows. That combination is bench-building and it precedes margin damage by about two quarters.
  • Adjusted operating margin below 15%, against 15.9% for the nine months to May 2026 and 15.6% for FY2025.
  • A goodwill impairment of any material size against the $22.536B balance. The FY2025 write-down of $271M on two divested acquisitions is the small-scale precedent.
  • A fourth consecutive year of business optimisation charges appearing in FY2027, at which point they are structural cost, not restructuring.
  • The $4.979B of July note proceeds funding a large transformational acquisition rather than repurchase or tuck-ins. That would change the risk profile of a company we are buying partly for its balance sheet.

Pre-registered FLIP TO CORE (upsize):

  • Local-currency bookings growth above 5% for two consecutive quarters and trailing book-to-bill back above 1.15x.
  • Fiscal-2027 guidance of 5% or better local-currency revenue growth on 24 September.
  • Disclosure that the July note proceeds were deployed into repurchase below approximately $180, which would be management acting on the same valuation view this dive takes.
  • Revenue per employee accelerating above 4% in local currency, which would mean the automation is accruing to Accenture rather than to its clients.
  • A price below approximately $140 (about 9.5x FY2027E) in the absence of a fundamental break — at which point the position should be completed rather than reconsidered.

Where ACN fits in the Synthos Framework Portfolio. The cash-return / de-rated quality sleeve, as a 1.5-2% starter position built to 3% on the triggers above. The sizing logic is explicit: the business quality, the balance sheet and the yield justify a full-sized holding, but the conviction lane is Low, the leading indicator is negative, and the entry is technically stretched — three independent reasons to leave room. On overlap within this batch: Accenture, Adobe and Salesforce are three expressions of the same wager, that the market has over-discounted incumbents whose revenue models new capability might displace. Accenture is the cheapest of the three on forward earnings, the only one paying a substantial dividend (3.82%), the only one whose cost base is people rather than code — which cuts both ways, since people can be reduced but code cannot be un-commoditised — and the one whose leading indicator is most clearly published and therefore most cleanly falsifiable. Logged as a tracked Synthos call (Buy — Tactical) as of 2026-08-04 at $170.54.

Single biggest risk: a sustained decline in local-currency new bookings. Everything else in the bear case is a symptom; this is the mechanism. Accenture's revenue in any quarter is the conversion of business won in prior quarters, so a shrinking order book shows up in revenue with a lag of roughly four to eight quarters, and by the time it is visible in the revenue line the multiple will have moved first. Book-to-bill at 1.03x in FQ3'26, against a derived 1.25x two years earlier, is the warning. It is not yet a verdict — bookings are lumpy by the company's own admission, dependent on the timing of a small number of large managed-services signings, and remaining performance obligations rose 12% over the same nine months — but it is the number that decides this investment, and it prints on 24 September.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that Accenture captures the surplus from automating its own delivery rather than surrendering it to clients in price. The whole bull case reduces to one ratio — revenue per employee growing faster than cost per employee, in local currency, for years. It is currently doing so (about $91,489 against about $86,577 twelve months earlier). But the 10-K names the exact failure mode: "If we are unable to introduce or if our clients do not accept new pricing or commercial models that reflect the value of these AI-enabled solutions, our results of operations may be adversely affected." A firm that automates its own work and continues to bill by the hour has cut its own revenue. A firm that automates its own work and successfully re-prices to outcomes has expanded its margin. Which of those two Accenture becomes is not knowable from this file, is not knowable on 24 September, and is the reason the exponential score is 2 and the position is staged.
