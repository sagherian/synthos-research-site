The Overview

Adobe sells the software that professionals use to make and manage visual work — Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere, Acrobat for documents, and a separate set of tools that big companies use to run their marketing. It stopped selling boxed software years ago and now rents everything by subscription, so about 96 cents of every revenue dollar arrives as a recurring payment. That makes the business unusually predictable, and unusually profitable: of every dollar of revenue, roughly 89 cents is left after the direct cost of delivering the service, and roughly 42 cents ends up as spare cash.

The company is not in trouble in any way you can see in its accounts. Revenue in its most recent quarter was 13% higher than a year before. The amount of business it has already signed and not yet delivered — the closest thing a subscription company has to an order book — is $22.27 billion, also up 13%. It has beaten what analysts expected on revenue in each of its last five quarters, and the margin of the beat has got bigger each time.

And yet the shares have fallen by a quarter in a year in which the wider market rose by a quarter, and are down about 62% from their high. The reason is a single argument: that new tools which generate images, video and text on demand will make Adobe's software less necessary, so that even though it earns record cash today, the money it earns a decade from now is unknowable. Several of the experts we track make that case forcefully and have been right about the share price so far.

Here is what makes the situation interesting. If you take today's price and work backwards, you find the market is already assuming Adobe's cash flow shrinks slightly, every year, forever. Not that it grows slowly — that it shrinks. That is a hard thing to believe about a company whose signed order book just grew 13%. So you are not being asked to pay for growth. You are being paid to take the risk that the pessimists are right.

Two things stop us calling it a buy today. First, Adobe does not tell anyone how much money it makes from its new generative features — not a number, not a percentage, nothing, in either quarterly report or the annual one. The most important question about the company is one it declines to answer. Second, its finance chief resigned in June and has been replaced on an interim basis, and a legal document filed in July reveals that a new chief executive is being hired but has not been announced.

The company reports again on 10 September, 37 days away, and that report will contain the order-book number. Our estimate of fair value is about $296 against today's $257.64, with a realistic downside near $190.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

2. The single most important thing to understand — the order book says one thing, the price says another

For a subscription business, the honest forward indicator is not revenue, which is a backward-looking recognition schedule, and not ARR, which the company defines itself. It is remaining performance obligations: contracted revenue not yet recognised, disclosed under a standard, audited annually. Adobe publishes it every quarter, and the series is the most important table in this dive.

Read it correctly and it is unambiguous. The 10-Q states plainly: "Remaining performance obligations of $22.27 billion as of May 29, 2026 increased by 13% from $19.69 billion as of May 30, 2025." The 10-K states the same for the year-end: "$22.52 billion as of November 28, 2025 increased by 13% from $19.96 billion as of November 29, 2024." Thirteen percent growth at both the half and the year-end.

Read it carelessly and you get the opposite answer, which is why it is worth spelling out. The book has fallen $250M since the fiscal year-end, from $22.52B to $22.27B. That looks like deceleration. It is not: the prior year shows the identical shape, $19.96B at year-end falling to $19.69B at the half — a 1.4% dip against this year's 1.1% dip. Adobe's fiscal fourth quarter is its enterprise renewal quarter, so the book peaks in November and drifts through the first half. The correct comparison is year on year, and year on year the book is compounding at 13%. This year's seasonal dip is slightly shallower than last year's.

Approximately 67% of the book converts to revenue within twelve months, with the remainder thereafter — so roughly $14.9B of the next twelve months' revenue is already contracted, against a consensus FY2026 revenue estimate of $26.524B.

Now put that against the price. At $257.64 the market capitalisation is $102.41B and the corrected enterprise value is approximately $103.9B (Section 4) against trailing free cash flow of $10.63B. Solve the perpetuity — enterprise value equals free cash flow times (1 + g) divided by (r − g) — at a 9% cost of capital:

> $103.9B = $10.63B × (1 + g) ÷ (0.09 − g) → g = −1.1%

Today's price implies Adobe's free cash flow declines about 1.1% every year, forever. At a 10% discount rate the implied rate is −0.2%; at 8% it is −2.1%. Under no reasonable discount rate does the current price imply growth. This is our arithmetic, labelled as such, and it is the central fact of the dive.

A contracted book growing 13% and a price implying perpetual decline cannot both be right. One resolves within about eight quarters. That is the trade, and it is why the September print is the catalyst rather than a routine update.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch.

What is true and good. An 89.1% gross margin. A 42.2% free-cash-flow margin producing $10.63B of trailing free cash flow on $179M of annual capital expenditure. Effectively zero net debt once the vendor's $1.164B short-term-investment omission is corrected. 97% subscription revenue. A contracted forward book of $22.27B, up 13% year on year at both the half and the fiscal year-end. Five consecutive revenue beats with the beat widening from +1.3% to +2.5%. A share count falling 7.2% a year against $26.78B of remaining repurchase authority. And a price of 9.37x FY2027 consensus non-GAAP earnings which, inverted, assumes free cash flow declines 1.1% a year in perpetuity.

What is true and bad. The knowledge base is net negative — 24 bearish against 14 bullish — and the negative lane has been right about the price, with a pre-registered technical test that is currently passing. GAAP net income grew 1.2% last quarter on 13% revenue growth, with operating expenses up 17% and operating margin down 400 basis points sequentially. Adobe collapsed three reporting segments into one and stopped publishing Digital Media ARR, reducing disclosure exactly when scrutiny rose. It materially expanded its competition risk factor in the Q1 FY2026 10-Q. It publishes no generative-monetisation metric of any kind. Its chief financial officer resigned in June and an unnamed chief executive is being hired. And the street, while 49% buy-rated, has a median price target 4.9% below the current price.

Why Watch and not Buy — Tactical. The asymmetry (1.9:1) and the valuation would ordinarily support a staged position. Three things override it. One: the central question — whether generative tooling expands Adobe's market or collapses its moat — cannot be answered from Adobe's own disclosure, and buying a name whose decisive variable is undisclosed is speculation dressed as analysis. Two: the resolving event is 37 days away and carries the exact number that matters. Three: the stock has already run 33% off its low into a still-declining 200-day average, so the timing premium for waiting is small and the timing risk of being early is a 25% retest. Waiting 37 days for a number that settles the thesis is not indecision; it is the correct use of a known catalyst.

Pre-registered FLIP TO BUY — TACTICAL (the specific trigger):

  • Remaining performance obligations at the 2026-09-10 print growing 11% or better year on year (the last two readings were both +13%). This is the single trigger. It says the contracted book has not cracked, and at 9.4x forward earnings that is enough.
  • AND gross margin at or above 88% in the same print, confirming inference costs are not eating the model.
  • Either of these alone is insufficient; together they convert the position. Add: a named permanent chief executive would allow a larger initial size.

Pre-registered FLIP TO BUY — CORE (upsize):

  • First disclosure of a consumption- or credit-based generative revenue metric showing meaningful scale. This would convert the unmeasurable to the measurable and is the highest-value single event available.
  • An accelerated repurchase materially above the ~$2.3B quarterly run rate at these prices, signalling management agrees with the arithmetic.
  • Reclaim of the 200-day moving average at $275.66 on the print, retiring the knowledge base's own technical test.
  • A price below ~$200 (near the $193.41 low) with the forward book still compounding — at which point the bear case is fully paid for and the position should be built rather than reconsidered.

Pre-registered KILL criteria — what removes this from the watchlist entirely:

  • Remaining-performance-obligation growth below 8% at the September print, or any sequential decline that exceeds the ~1.1-1.4% seasonal pattern established in the last two years. This is the primary kill.
  • Gross margin below 87% for two consecutive quarters, indicating inference costs are structurally impairing the model — the most fragile assumption in the price (Section 6a).
  • Organic ARR growth (ex-Semrush) falling below 8%.
  • A downward revision cycle taking FY2028 consensus EPS below ~$27, which would put the current price at 9.5x FY2028 rather than 8.2x and remove the valuation support.
  • The repurchase pace falling below ~$2B a quarter while the stock sits under 12x forward earnings, which would signal either that management does not share the valuation view or that cash is needed elsewhere.
  • A permanent chief executive appointed from outside with a mandate to restructure, which would introduce a multi-year transition of a kind this valuation does not price.

Single biggest risk: an unmeasurable terminal value. Not that Adobe is being disrupted — nothing in the filings shows that — but that the question cannot be answered from what the company publishes. Adobe discloses total ARR, subscription revenue across two broad customer groups, and remaining performance obligations, and nothing at all about how much revenue its generative capabilities produce, how consumption is trending, or how pricing is being restructured around them. Every bull argument in our knowledge base and every bear argument rests on an assertion about a number nobody outside the company has. That is why the risk score is 5 rather than 3, and why this is a Watch.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that gross margin holds near 89% while inference costs scale with usage. It has held so far — 89.2% in the latest quarter against 89.1% a year earlier — and the 10-Q confirms those costs are already inside cost of subscription revenue. But the same filing warns that deploying AI models "may continue to increase the cost of our solutions" and that the costs "could adversely impact our margins." Adobe's entire valuation support is a 42% free-cash-flow margin. Take gross margin from 89% to 84% and the free-cash-flow yield goes from 10.4% to roughly 8%, and the whole valuation argument in this dive becomes ordinary. It would not arrive as a headline; it would arrive as four quarters of quiet erosion.
