The Overview

The world is analog and computers are digital. Temperature, pressure, sound, light, voltage, motion — all of these are continuous physical quantities, and something has to convert them into numbers a processor can use, and convert the numbers back into things that move. Analog Devices makes those converters, along with the amplifiers that clean up weak signals and the circuits that manage electrical power.

This is an unglamorous but very good business. The parts are usually a small fraction of the cost of the machine they go into, they are designed in for a decade or more, and they are hard to swap because the performance lives in the circuit design rather than in a specification anyone can copy. ADI employs about 24,500 people, of whom roughly 13,000 are engineers.

It is also cyclical, and the swings are large. In the year to November 2024 revenue fell 23%. Since then it has come roaring back: last quarter sales were up 37% on the year before, and — this is the important part — the profit on each dollar of sales went from 61 cents to 67 cents. That happens because ADI owns its own factories. When they run near full, the fixed costs are spread over more chips and the margin jumps. When they run empty, the same thing happens in reverse.

So the results are excellent. The problem is the price. The shares are $380.80, having risen 72% in the last twelve months against 24% for the market, and 73% from their low of $220.68. That is about 31 times what analysts expect the company to earn this year, and 57 times what it actually reported on a statutory basis over the last twelve months.

There is also a timing issue. The company reports results on 19 August, fifteen days from now. Buying a semiconductor stock at 31 times forward earnings, two weeks before a report, on the day it rose 5%, is a bet on a specific event rather than an investment.

Our estimate of value is $400. The average analyst says $455. We are lower, and we are lower because we think a good part of the 630-basis-point margin improvement is factories running full rather than a permanent change.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Modest debt, big margins, good cash generation — attached to a business that fell 23% two years ago and a share price that has tripled off its low.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. Outstanding right now. Measured across the whole cycle, about 6% a year.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. More sensors and more power circuits in every car, factory and robot. Real, well-evidenced, and slow.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $400 against a current price of $380.80 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

The 630 basis points now being added were, in large part, the 690 basis points lost then. The honest question is not whether margin expanded — it plainly did — but whether 67.3% is the new normal or the top of a utilisation curve.
