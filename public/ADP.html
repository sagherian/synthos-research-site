The Overview

Every two weeks, a large fraction of American companies send ADP the money for their employees' wages and payroll taxes. ADP holds that money for a few days, sends it where it needs to go, and — this is the part most people miss — earns interest on it in the meantime. Right now it is holding about $40 to $48 billion at any given moment and earning about 3.3%. That interest was $999 million of revenue in nine months, and because holding money costs almost nothing, nearly all of it drops to profit.

The rest of the business is software and service: running payroll, filing taxes in every state and city that levies them, and for a third of revenue, acting as the legal co-employer for small businesses so they can offer benefits they could not buy alone.

It is an unusually good business. Companies almost never switch payroll providers, because if payroll goes wrong people do not get paid and the government gets angry. ADP earns 70 cents of profit annually for every dollar of shareholder equity. It carries almost no net debt. It raised its dividend again. It bought back $2.1 billion of stock last year.

So why not buy it?

Because of the price. Twelve months ago the stock was near $300. It fell to $189. Over the last three months it has gone back up 28%, to $270.53. At that price you are paying about 22 times what analysts think ADP will earn in the fiscal year ending June 2027. The analysts who cover it — 36 of them — mostly say Hold, five say Sell, and their average price target of $267.67 is below where the stock trades today. Our own estimate of fair value is $272, which is about where it already is.

Two things also worth knowing. First, growth is slowing to the low end of what this company normally does: strip out currency and the interest on client money, and the underlying business is compounding at roughly 5%. The number that tells you how many people ADP's own clients employ grew 1%. Second, that interest income cuts both ways — if short-term interest rates fall, ADP loses that revenue almost dollar for dollar.

Nothing is wrong here. The stock is simply worth about what it costs.

  • Downside Risk 3/10. Recurring revenue, tiny net debt, 0.84 beta. But it fell 39% inside the last year, so "low risk" is not "low volatility."
  • Growth Quality 5/10. Real, durable, and slow — with the fastest-growing line being interest rates rather than anything ADP sells.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. A compounder. Nothing in this file suggests otherwise, and the company does not pretend otherwise.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $272 against a current price of $281.35 — a premium price for a business we still like.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Read that table and the shape of the business is clear. Employer Services — payroll, tax filing, HR software, time and attendance — is two-thirds of revenue at a 37.4% pre-tax margin and it is expanding. PEO Services, in which ADP becomes the co-employer of record for small and mid-sized clients' worksite employees, is a third of revenue at a 13.4% margin and it is contracting: margin down 110 basis points over nine months and 120 in the March quarter, with pre-tax earnings falling 2% on 6% revenue growth.

The PEO deterioration has a named cause and it is structural, not cyclical. From the 10-Q: nine-month operating expenses rose "due to an increase of $232.8 million of PEO Services zero-margin benefits pass-through costs to $3,427.2 million from $3,194.4 million", plus "$31.9 million due to an increase in costs related to workers' compensation coverage and state unemployment taxes for worksite employees." Zero-margin pass-throughs are revenue that carries no profit at all, so when they grow faster than the rest of the segment, reported revenue growth flatters and margin compresses mechanically. PEO's headline 6% growth therefore overstates its economic contribution, and this is the least-good line in the company.

Three things follow and only the first is usually said out loud.

It is nearly pure margin. ADP incurs essentially no incremental cost to earn it, so its contribution to pre-tax earnings is close to its full dollar value. $999.4 million against nine-month pre-tax earnings of $4,472.5 million is 22.3% of pre-tax profit from 6.1% of revenue.

It is a rate bet the income statement does not label as one. ADP invests the float in a laddered portfolio "out to five years (in the case of the extended portfolio) and out to ten years (in the case of the long portfolio)", which damps but does not remove the sensitivity. A 100-basis-point fall in the earned yield is roughly $400 million of annual revenue and close to $400 million of pre-tax earnings — about 7% of the fiscal-2026 pre-tax total of $5,730.3 million. The ladder means this arrives over years rather than quarters, which is a real mitigant and is why the score is 3 and not higher.

It grows with employment and wages. Balances rose 7.3% year on year, against pays per control of +1%, so most of the balance growth is wage inflation and new client wins rather than headcount. In a genuine employment downturn, the float shrinks at the same time the yield falls.
