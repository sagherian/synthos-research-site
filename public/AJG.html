The Overview

When a company needs insurance — for its buildings, its trucks, its employees, its liabilities — it usually does not go to an insurer directly. It goes to a broker, who knows the market, negotiates the terms, and takes a commission. Gallagher is the third-largest such broker in the world.

It is a good business for boring reasons. Policies renew every year, so the revenue comes back. The broker does not take the risk — if the insurer pays out a fortune after a hurricane, that is the insurer's problem, not Gallagher's. And it requires almost no equipment: Gallagher spent $145 million on capital projects last year against $13.9 billion of revenue.

Last year Gallagher did something very large. It bought a company called AssuredPartners, spending $15.7 billion and issuing 16% more shares to pay for it. Before the deal it had more cash than debt. After it, it owes about $11.3 billion net.

Two warnings about the numbers you will see quoted elsewhere.

The first is about debt. Standard data feeds show Gallagher with almost no net debt. That is wrong, and the reason is specific to brokers. When a client pays an insurance premium, the money sits in Gallagher's bank account for a while before it reaches the insurer. At the last count that was $7.1 billion of other people's money. It appears as cash — but Gallagher cannot spend it. Strip it out and the company is carrying about 2.9 times its annual earnings in debt, not almost none.

The second is about profit. Gallagher reported about $6.04 per share of profit under standard accounting rules over the last year, and tells investors the "adjusted" figure is about $12.01 — nearly double. The difference is an accounting charge for the value of the customer relationships it bought. That charge is not cash, so excluding it is normal and defensible. But it is worth saying out loud: the company spent $15.7 billion buying those relationships, and it excludes the cost of writing them down.

Something we can report as genuinely clean: we check every company's capital-spending figure against its filings because that number is wrong in four out of ten of the files we examine. Gallagher's is exactly right in all three years we could test, and the company even tells you next year's — about $227 million.

The shares cost $248.36, about 17 times what analysts expect Gallagher to earn in 2027. That is not expensive for this kind of business. Our fair value estimate is $268, about 8% above the price. Analysts say $278.89.

One more thing, and it is unusual enough to state plainly. We maintain a database of 52,021 opinions from independent analysts and commentators. Not one of them mentions this company, or insurance broking at all. So unlike every other company in this batch, we have no outside opinion to check ourselves against. We are relying entirely on the filings. That is why we are cautious.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Long term 2+ years

▲ Tailwind
Driver
"Long-run, insurance broking is one of the better permanent businesses in listed equity and Gallagher is the third-largest practitioner of it. Commissions recur on renewal; the client relationship is sticky because switching brokers means re-tendering an entire insurance programme; the capital intensity is trivial at 1.0% of revenue; and the industry remains fragmented enough that a disciplined acquirer can compound by consolidation for a very long time — Gallagher has done exactly that, taking revenue from $7,009 million in 2020 to $13,942 million in 2025. The economics do not depend on underwriting: the broker is paid whether or not the insurer makes money, which is why the beta is 0.508. Chief executive J. Patrick Gallagher Jr. runs a company bearing his family name, which historically has correlated with long-horizon capital allocation. Against that: the roll-up model requires ever-larger deals to move a $63.8 billion company, and AssuredPartners at $15,749 million of net spend was already large enough to leave the balance sheet at roughly 2.85x EBITDA with negative tangible book value. The mathematical limit of a consolidation strategy is the supply of targets, and the price paid for them rises as the acquirer's need grows."
