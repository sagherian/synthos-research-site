The Overview

Allstate sells car and home insurance. You pay a premium; if something goes wrong, Allstate pays the claim. The company makes money in two ways: it keeps the difference between premiums collected and claims paid, and it invests the money in between.

Insurers measure themselves with one number: the combined ratio. It is the share of every premium dollar that goes out again as claims and costs. Below 100 means the underwriting made money; above 100 means it lost money and the profits have to come from investments.

In the three months to March, Allstate's combined ratio was 82.0. A year earlier it was 97.4. That improvement turned $360 million of underwriting profit into $2,658 million. Profit for shareholders went from $566 million to $2,428 million. The company earned a 48.4% return on its shareholders' money over the past year, and the book value of each share rose 52.2%.

Those are remarkable numbers, and the shares have responded: up 31% in a year.

Here is the part that decides this report. The company's own filing breaks the improvement into its parts, and two of them are luck rather than skill.

Storms. Catastrophes — hurricanes, hail, wildfires — cost Allstate $1,240 million this quarter and $2,202 million in the same quarter last year. In combined-ratio terms that is 8.4 points versus 15.7. A quiet quarter for weather is worth 7.3 points, and nobody controls the weather.

Reserves. When an insurer sets money aside for claims that have happened but not yet been paid, and later decides it set aside too much, it releases the difference straight into profit. Allstate released $1,004 million this quarter, against $251 million a year earlier — worth another 5.0 points. That is a one-off adjustment to past estimates, not this quarter's business.

Put both back to last year's levels and the combined ratio is closer to 94 than to 82. Ninety-four is a perfectly good number. It is not an eighty-two.

Wall Street has noticed. Analysts expect Allstate to earn $31.34 per share this year and $26.36 next year — that is, they expect profits to fall 16%. And the average analyst price target is $258.46, which is below the current price of $262.59.

Two more facts about timing. Allstate reports its next results tomorrow, 5 August. And a brand-new chief financial officer started two days ago — Christian Lown, previously of CoStar Group and Freddie Mac.

Our estimate of fair value is $290, about 10% above the price. Against that, about 24% of downside in a bad scenario. That is not enough reward for the risk, so our conclusion is Hold: a fine company, fairly priced, at the best moment of its cycle.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is

The Allstate Corporation, headquartered in Illinois with 53,300 employees, run by chairman and chief executive Thomas Joseph Wilson, provides property, casualty and other insurance across the United States and Canada. The profile block describes four segments: Allstate Protection, Protection Services, Allstate Health and Benefits and Run-off Property-Liability — "standard private passenger auto and homeowners policies, as well as specialized vehicle coverage for motorcycles, trailers, motor homes, and off-road vehicles... renter, condominium" cover and more.

That description is now partly out of date, and the 10-Q says why:

> "The dispositions of the employer voluntary benefits ('EVB') and group health businesses in 2025 did not qualify for discontinued operations. The Allstate Health and Benefits segment is no longer a reportable segment, with results of this segment recast to reflect only the results of the EVB and group health businesses. The retained individual health business, previously included in the Allstate Health and Benefits segment, is a non-reportable segment with results included in all other for all periods presented."

Now the arithmetic that decides this dive, stated as plainly as it can be.

The combined ratio improved 15.4 points. The filing attributes 7.3 of those points to a lighter catastrophe load and 5.0 to larger prior-year reserve releases. That is 12.3 of 15.4 points — 80% of the improvement — from two items that are not a run rate.

On catastrophes. Allstate published $1,240 million of catastrophe losses in the quarter against $2,202 million a year earlier. The company issues a monthly press release specifically to disclose estimated catastrophe losses — two of the three 8-Ks in this archive, dated 2026-06-18 and 2026-07-16, exist for no other purpose. A business that reports a number monthly because it is too volatile to leave to quarters is not a business whose good quarter should be annualised.

On reserve development. A prior-year reserve reestimate is a revision to an earlier estimate of claims already incurred. A $1,004 million release means Allstate concluded it had over-reserved past accident years by that amount, and the entire sum flows to current-period income. It is real money and it is genuine evidence of conservative past reserving. It is not this quarter's underwriting, and a release of that size — 6.8 points of combined ratio, four times the prior year's — cannot repeat indefinitely, because reserves are finite.

The normalised picture, labelled as our arithmetic and not the company's. Restore the prior year's 15.7-point catastrophe load and the prior year's 1.8-point reserve benefit and the Q1 2026 combined ratio would be approximately 94.3. On $14,802 million of premiums earned, the difference between 82.0 and 94.3 is roughly $1,820 million of underwriting income — taking the quarter from $2,658 million to approximately $838 million. That is not a bear case; it is the same quarter with average weather and average reserve development.

We do not claim the true mid-cycle number is 94.3. Catastrophe loads vary, Allstate has been raising homeowners rates, and the expense ratio at 21.3 is genuinely good. What we claim is narrower and harder to argue with: the headline 82.0 contains roughly 12 points that a shareholder should not capitalise, and the consensus estimate row proves the sell side agrees.
