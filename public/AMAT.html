The Overview

Every computer chip is built by adding microscopically thin layers of material to a silicon wafer and then cutting patterns into them, hundreds of times over. Applied Materials makes the machines that do the adding and the cutting. It is the broadest supplier in that business — it sells more different kinds of chip-making machine than anyone else — and it also runs a large repair-and-maintenance operation for the tens of thousands of its machines already sitting in factories around the world. That maintenance business brings in about $6.4 billion a year and grows steadily whether or not anyone is buying new machines, which makes it the most dependable part of the company.

The business is in excellent health. It keeps about 49 cents of gross profit on every dollar of sales, up from 45 cents five years ago, and the company's own filings say part of that improvement came from charging more, not just selling more. It has more cash than debt. It earns very high returns on the money shareholders have put in. It has beaten analysts' profit forecasts five quarters in a row.

The difficulty is the share price. The stock has gone up 204% in the past year while the wider market rose 24%. At $546.62 you are paying about 44 times this year's expected profit and 32 times next year's. For comparison, the company generates about $6 billion of genuinely spare cash a year, which on a $434 billion company is a yield of 1.4% — you would earn more in a bank account.

There is a second problem: nobody agrees what Applied will earn in 2028. The published forecasts run from $14.55 a share to $27.02 a share. That is not a small disagreement. It is the difference between the stock being expensive and the stock being reasonable, and the "consensus" figure everyone quotes is just the average of those two views.

And there is a date. On 13 August, nine days from now, the company reports. Analysts expect sales of about $9.0 billion, which would be 23% more than the same quarter last year — a very large jump for a company whose sales were actually shrinking two quarters ago. If it happens, the shares can go higher. If it does not, there is a long way down.

Two other facts are worth knowing. Every single insider transaction on file is a sale, including the chief executive selling about $55 million of stock in late June at prices between $700 and $736 — well above today's $547. And the company itself, which spent $4.9 billion buying back its own shares last year, spent only $737 million in the most recent six months despite having $13.2 billion of permission to spend. Some of that is because it is building factories and paid a $253 million government fine, but the change of pace is striking.

Our estimate of fair value is $570 against a price of $546.62. That is 4% of upside, which is not enough to buy into an event.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

1. What the business is, and how the segments actually report

Applied Materials, founded 1967 and listed since 1980-03-17, employs 36,500 people under chief executive Gary E. Dickerson. It provides, in the 10-K's own words, "equipment, services and software to the semiconductor and related industries."

As of fiscal 2025 the company reports TWO segments, not three. This matters, because the vendor payload and most secondary descriptions still carry the old three-segment structure. The 10-K filed 2025-12-12 states it directly:

> "We operate in two reportable segments: Semiconductor Systems and Applied Global Services (AGS). As of October 26, 2025, management no longer considers Display a significant operating segment for separate reporting purposes... Prior-year Corporate and Other balances have been recast to include Display financial results."

Semiconductor Systems is the capital-equipment business, comprising, per Note 15, "etch, rapid thermal processing, deposition, chemical mechanical planarization, metrology and inspection, wafer packaging, and ion implantation." It sells into three markets the company names explicitly: foundry, logic and other (split between leading edge, defined as 7 nanometres and below, and non-leading edge serving internet-of-things, communications, automotive, power and sensors); DRAM; and NAND flash.

Applied Global Services provides "services, spares and factory automation software" to the installed base, plus — through fiscal 2025 only — 200-millimetre and other equipment for non-leading-edge markets. The strategic intent is stated plainly: "to continue to shift the AGS service and spares business to a subscription agreement model... providing us a more predictable revenue stream."

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over 12-24 months ≈ earnings growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield.

Earnings growth on consensus is very large: plus 38% (FY26), plus 29% (FY27), plus 20% (FY28). Shareholder yield is small: a 0.35% dividend plus a buyback currently running at roughly 0.35% annualised, so call it 0.7%. Everything therefore depends on multiple drift, and our base case assumes COMPRESSION.

The reason is mechanical and is set out in Section 2. The reader today pays 25.1x fiscal-2028 earnings. Over a twelve-month horizon the market's anchor rolls forward one fiscal year, from 2028 to 2029. If the multiple simply holds at 25.1x and the anchor rolls, the price becomes 25.1 × $22.68 = $569 — because consensus fiscal-2029 earnings are only 4.3% above fiscal 2028. That is our base case, and it is a no-re-rating construction: the entire 4.3% of implied upside is the one-year roll, and it is small precisely because the consensus curve flattens.

Contrast the same construction on the nearer anchor: if the market keeps paying today's 32.3x forward multiple and the forward year rolls from 2027 to 2028, the price becomes 32.3 × $21.75 = $702. That is the bull mechanism — and note what it requires: that the market is still paying 32x for a company whose consensus growth in the following year is 4.3%. We do not think that survives contact with 2028.

Stated plainly: our fair value assumes the multiple compresses from 32.3x forward to roughly 25x forward over the horizon, driven by growth maturation that consensus itself forecasts. If the multiple instead holds, the stock is worth $700. The fragile leg of the bull case is not the earnings — it is the terminal multiple.
