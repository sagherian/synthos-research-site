The Overview

Amgen is one of the oldest and largest biotechnology companies in the world. It sells about fifteen important medicines — drugs for osteoporosis, high cholesterol, arthritis, psoriasis, several cancers, thyroid eye disease and gout. Last year it sold about $36.7 billion of them and turned about $8.1 billion of that into spare cash after paying for its factories.

Two things are happening to it at once, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first is good. Amgen is selling more medicine every year. In 2025 the actual number of doses going out the door rose 13%. That is genuine demand growth, and some individual drugs are growing very fast — Repatha, its cholesterol drug, was up 36%.

The second is bad, and it is the bigger of the two. Amgen is being paid less for each dose. Across the whole company the average price fell 3% last year. And two of its biggest products, Prolia and XGEVA — together about $6.5 billion, roughly one dollar in every six the company earns — lost their patent protection in early 2025. Copies are now on the market. In the first three months of 2026 the number of Prolia doses sold fell 17% and the price fell another 10%. Amgen's own words in its filing are "accelerated sales erosion". Another big product, ENBREL, has already fallen by a third and is now subject to a government-set Medicare price.

Put the two together and the analysts who follow the company expect sales to grow about 2.6% a year for the next three years, and profits about 4-5% a year. That is not a fast-growing company. That is a solid, slow one.

There is also a large debt. Amgen borrowed heavily to buy a company called Horizon in 2023, and after paying some of it back it still owes about $45 billion more than it holds in cash. That is about 2.7 times a year's earnings before interest, tax and depreciation — manageable, but not comfortable. And there are two things a shareholder should know that do not show up in any stock screener. One: Amgen is in a long-running court fight with the U.S. tax authorities over how it allocated profits to Puerto Rico, and the amounts at stake are roughly $10.7 billion before offsets. A judge's decision is expected in the second half of this year. Two: on 31 July 2026 — six days ago — Amgen told the SEC it had suffered a material computer break-in in which company data and patients' health information were stolen. It says it does not currently expect a big financial impact, but the investigation is still open.

Against all that, there is one genuine lottery ticket. Amgen has an experimental obesity drug called MariTide, and it now has six large final-stage trials running. Its stand-out feature, according to the company, is that patients may be able to take it monthly or even every three months rather than weekly. If that works it is worth a great deal. If it does not, Amgen is a slow-growing, indebted, dividend-paying pharmaceutical company.

The shares are $390.02. That is within 0.8% of the highest price they have closed at in a year, and they have risen 20% in three months. Our estimate of what they are worth is $375 — less than the price. The average analyst's target is $365 — also less than the price. And the company reports its quarterly results tonight.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Low share-price volatility and lots of cash, attached to a big debt, a shrinking patent estate, a large unresolved tax case and a fresh data breach.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. More doses sold, less money per dose, netting to about 2.6% a year.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. One real shot — six late-stage obesity trials — bolted to a large, mature, slow business.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

Amgen describes itself in the 10-K as a company that "discovers, develops, manufactures and delivers innovative medicines to fight some of the world's toughest diseases," which "helped launch the biotechnology industry more than 45 years ago." Incorporated in California in 1980, reincorporated in Delaware in 1987, headquartered in Thousand Oaks, California. Chairman and chief executive Robert A. Bradway. The vendor profile lists 31,500 full-time employees; the extracted 10-K text does not carry a headcount figure, so that number is vendor-sourced and labelled as such.

The single most important disclosure-structure fact: the 10-K states plainly, "Amgen operates in one operating segment: human therapeutics. Therefore, our results of operations are discussed on a consolidated basis." There are no reportable segments. Anything in a vendor file labelled seg_prod for Amgen is therefore not a segment table — it is the worldwide product sales table from the MD&A, plus one line of non-product revenue. That distinction matters and Section 8 records what the vendor got wrong in it.

2. The single most important thing to understand about these numbers: volume up, price down, and the cliff has already started

Every headline number Amgen reports is the net of two very large opposing forces, and the filing quantifies both.

> 10-K, 2026-02-13: "Total product sales increased 10% in 2025, driven by volume growth of 13%, partially offset by declines in net selling price of 3%. U.S. volume grew 13% and ROW volume grew 14%."

> 10-Q, 2026-05-01: "Total product sales increased 4% for the three months ended March 31, 2026, driven by volume growth of 9%, partially offset by declines in net selling price of 2% and 2% from lower wholesaler and end user inventory."

Read those two sentences next to each other and the trajectory is unambiguous: volume growth decelerated from 13% to 9%, and total product sales growth decelerated from 10% to 4%, in one year. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+4.7%, from 2026E $22.356 to 2027E $23.416) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 16.66x to 16.0x on the respective forward year, −4.0%) + shareholder yield (+2.58%, entirely dividend) ≈ +3.3%.

Our base case assumes mild multiple COMPRESSION and we should be explicit about why. Today the market pays 16.66x on the next calendar year's non-GAAP estimate. If in twelve months it pays 16.0x on the then-next year's estimate, that is a genuine de-rating of 4%, not the mechanical rolldown of the calendar. We assume it for two reasons. First, the loss-of-exclusivity profile gets worse before it gets better: Otezla enters Medicare price setting in 2027, the Colorado ENBREL upper payment limit bites no earlier than 1 January 2027, and Prolia/XGEVA erosion is explicitly guided to accelerate. A multiple appropriate to a company adding revenue is not appropriate to a company defending it. Second, the entry point is a 52-week high with RSI at 71.03 — the multiple has already expanded 20.4% over three months without an equivalent estimate revision.

The dominant term in the return is not earnings growth and it is not the multiple — it is the dividend. At a 4.7% EPS growth rate and a 2.58% yield, 44% of the expected total return is the dividend cheque. That is a materially different equity from the growth compounders elsewhere in this batch, and it should be underwritten as such: if the multiple simply HELD at 16.66x on 2027E the price would be $390 — exactly today's price, plus the 2.58% dividend; if it compressed to 15x the price is $351 (−10.0%); if it expanded to 18x the price is $421 (+8.1%). The entire spread between a good and a bad twelve months lives in three multiple points we do not control.

The bull case at $461 requires multiple EXPANSION and an earnings beat together — 19x on the 2027 consensus high of $24.250. The bear case at $287 is a de-rating to 13x on the 2027 consensus LOW of $22.100 — which is roughly what a market prices when it decides a company's revenue base is shrinking rather than plateauing.
