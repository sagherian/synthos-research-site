The Overview

Amazon does four things. It sells you things, and lets other people sell you things through its site and takes a cut. It rents out computers by the hour to almost every large company in the world — that business is called AWS. It sells advertising space on its own shopping site, which turns out to be an enormous and very profitable business almost nobody talks about. And it charges people a subscription for faster delivery and video.

Five days ago it announced the biggest quarterly profit any company has ever reported: about $62.6 billion in three months. That headline is misleading, and understanding why is the whole point of this document.

Amazon owns a stake in a private artificial-intelligence company. It does not sell that stake; it simply revalues it each quarter based on what other investors most recently paid. This quarter that revaluation added about $53.4 billion to reported profit. No cash moved. If you take the revaluation out, Amazon earned roughly $1.95 a share, against the roughly $1.82 that analysts expected. A good quarter. Not a miraculous one.

Now the part that does involve cash. Amazon is building data centres at an extraordinary rate. Over the last twelve months, the cash its business generated was about $161 billion. The cash it spent on buildings and computers was about $172 billion. So Amazon spent about $11 billion more than it made. To fund that it has been borrowing heavily: its bonds outstanding roughly doubled in six months, and it borrowed another $25 billion just after the quarter ended, some of it at over 6% interest on debt that does not mature until 2066. It pays no dividend and has bought back no shares in three years.

Here is the tension. Everything Amazon buys has to be written off over its useful life — computers over about five years, buildings over much longer. That write-off is a charge against future profits. Spending $200 billion a year eventually means roughly $40 billion a year of extra charges for each year of spending. Those charges arrive on a fixed schedule. The revenue that is meant to justify them arrives only if customers actually use the capacity.

Amazon's answer is that the customers have already signed. It disclosed that it has about $496 billion of committed customer contracts stretching out over an average of about six and a half years. That is a genuinely large number and a genuinely good answer. The uncomfortable detail is that Amazon has also invested roughly $60 billion of its own money into two of those customers, and the profit windfall this quarter came from marking up one of those very same investments.

Today the stock market started to take a view. Amazon fell 2.3% from its highest price of the year. On the same day, every company that sells Amazon the equipment it is buying went up between 4% and 13%. Investors bought the shovel-sellers and sold the digger.

We think the business is excellent and the price is full. Our estimate of fair value is $300 against a price of $277, which is not enough reward for the uncertainty. Analysts on average say $323. We are below them, which is unusual and deliberate.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Fortress franchises and $123 billion of liquid assets, but negative free cash flow, doubled debt, and no dividend or buyback to cushion a fall.
  • Growth Quality 8/10. Revenue growth accelerating for four straight quarters; cloud up 37%; advertising up 22%. Increasingly bought with capital rather than earned.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. Cloud re-acceleration, own-design chips, warehouse robotics against a $90 billion cost base. Constrained only by already being a $3 trillion company.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $300 against a current price of $277.42 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business actually is — and why the vendor has it filed wrong

The vendor's profile classifies Amazon as Consumer Cyclical / Specialty Retail, with 1,576,000 employees and Andrew R. Jassy as chief executive. The classification drives a peer set of Etsy, GameStop, Casey's General Stores, Tandy Leather Factory, LightInTheBox and Revolve. That peer set is rejected in full (Section 8): the largest of them outside Alibaba is a $31B convenience-store chain, and no multiple in this dive is drawn from it.

The 10-K and 10-Q both state the actual structure: three reportable segments — North America, International, and AWS, with the chief operating decision maker being the chief executive.

Three things follow from these tables that most coverage of Amazon misses.

First, advertising is the fastest-growing line in the company and it is not a reportable segment. At $68.635B growing 22.1%, Amazon's advertising business is larger than all but a handful of listed companies in the world, and it is buried inside North America and International. There is no disclosed advertising operating margin anywhere in the filings or the payload. Anyone modelling Amazon's margin structure without an explicit advertising assumption is guessing, and so are we — we say so rather than fabricating one.

Second, the vendor's seg_prod table is the "groups of similar products and services" disclosure, not the reportable segments. AWS appears in both, which is why it looks like a segment. North America and International do not appear in seg_prod at all. Do not mix the two tables.

Third, seg_geo is corrupted for every year except FY2025, and the 10-K explains exactly how (Section 8). The FY2024 row reads "North America Segment $438.015B, International Segment $93.832B, Germany $40.856B, Japan $27.401B, United Kingdom $37.855B" — which sums to $637.959B, precisely FY2024 revenue. But the FY2024 North America segment was approximately $387.5B, not $438.0B. The 10-K contains two separate tables: reportable segments, and "net sales attributed to countries that represent a significant portion of consolidated net sales". The vendor's FY2024 row is the country table with the United States mislabelled "North America Segment" and rest-of-world mislabelled "International Segment". FY2025's row is the genuine segment table. The two years are therefore not comparable and no year-on-year segment growth rate is computed from seg_geo.

The one thing this file cannot give us, and it matters. The brief for this dive correctly identifies segment operating income as more important than segment revenue for Amazon. Neither source supplies it. The vendor payload has no segment profit field at all. The SEC filings contain the table — the 10-Q says "Operating income by segment is as follows (in millions)" at the top of page 32 — but every numeric table has been stripped by the text extraction, in both the 10-K and both 10-Qs. What survives is the narrative, which is directionally useful (North America up on unit and advertising sales; International up on the same; AWS up on sales, partially offset by infrastructure spending) but carries no numbers. AWS operating margin is therefore not stated anywhere in this dive, because it cannot be sourced. This is stated as a gap rather than estimated around.

2. The single most important thing to understand about these numbers

Two-thirds of Amazon's reported Q2'26 profit is an unrealised mark on a private company that is also one of its largest customers, and which Amazon is itself partly funding.

The 10-Q filed 2026-07-31 states it directly:

> "Other income (expense), net was $1.1 billion and $53.4 billion during Q2 2025 and Q2 2026, and $3.9 billion and $69.1 billion for the six months ended June 30, 2025 and 2026 … The net gain of $53.4 billion in Q2 2026 and $69.1 billion for the six months ended June 30, 2026 is primarily from upward adjustments for observable changes in price relating to our nonvoting preferred stock in [a privately held artificial-intelligence developer] and the reclassification adjustment for the gains on available-for-sale debt securities from the portions of our convertible notes investments … that were converted to nonvoting preferred stock."

The circularity is the part that deserves the most scrutiny. We are not alleging impropriety; these are arm's-length transactions disclosed in full. But the analytical point stands and should be stated plainly: a material portion of Amazon's contracted backlog is owed by customers Amazon has helped capitalise, and a material portion of its reported earnings is an unrealised mark on those same customers. Both the backlog and the earnings are real. Neither is independent of the other. Nothing in this dive values Amazon on trailing GAAP earnings, and no forward multiple in Section 6 uses the FY2026 consensus row.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over 12-24 months decomposes as: EPS growth (+30% from FY2027's $10.42 to FY2028's $13.57 on consensus) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION assumed, from 26.6x FY27E toward ~22x FY28E) + shareholder yield (NEGATIVE, approximately −0.9% from dilution, with no dividend and no buyback).

Our base case explicitly assumes multiple compression, and we should say why. The market currently pays 26.6x forward earnings for Amazon. Our $300 base is 28.8x FY2027E but 22.1x FY2028E — that is, we assume the price advances roughly in line with one year of earnings growth while the multiple the market pays at each date drifts down. The mechanism is not sentiment; it is composition. As capital intensity rises, the earnings become lower-quality: more of them are non-cash marks, more of the cash they generate is immediately reinvested, and the depreciation charge against them grows faster than revenue. A business earning $13.57 a share in FY2028 with negative or barely-positive free cash flow does not deserve the same multiple as a business earning $10.42 with a rising cash conversion rate. Peer convergence points the same way: the market is currently repricing the whole hyperscaler complex from asset-light software multiples toward capital-intensive infrastructure multiples, and today's session is what that looks like in one day.

Almost all of the expected return in our base case is earnings growth, and that is the honest framing. From $277.42 to $300 is +8.1% — roughly one quarter of the consensus FY2027-to-FY2028 EPS growth, with the rest absorbed by de-rating and dilution. The bull case at $375 requires the multiple to HOLD at ~36x FY2027E, which requires free cash flow to inflect visibly and early. That is the fragile leg and it is not in the base.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch.

Amazon is one of the two or three best businesses in the world and this is not a call on the business. Revenue growth has accelerated for four consecutive quarters to 19.6%. AWS grew 37%. Operating margin expanded 226 basis points. Advertising compounds at 22% on a $68.6B base with no capital behind it. The company discloses approximately $496B of contracted customer obligations with a 6.4-year weighted-average life. Estimate coverage is the deepest of any listed company. Everything about the franchise argues for owning it.

What argues against owning it at $277.42, today.

The reported earnings are not what they appear. Two-thirds of the largest quarterly profit in corporate history is a non-cash mark on an illiquid private holding that is simultaneously a major contracted customer and partly funded by Amazon itself. On our derivation, the quarter beat consensus by 7%, not 216%, and the trailing multiple is ~36.8x rather than the 22.3x every screen displays.

The cash flow is negative and the trajectory is steepening. Trailing free cash flow is approximately −$11.1B. First-half capital expenditure rose 73% year on year to $96.3B and the company says it expects it to increase further. Capex runs at 2.3x depreciation, and every dollar of that gap is a future charge on a fixed schedule.

The funding has changed character. Unsecured notes roughly doubled in six months to $132.1B, with a further $25.0B issued after quarter-end at coupons up to 6.25% and effective rates to 6.33% on paper maturing in 2066. Return on invested capital is 8.48%. The spread between what the new capital costs and what the existing capital earns is thin.

There is no yield to wait on. No dividend. No buyback in three years, with $6.1B authorised and unused. Diluted shares rising 0.9% a year. The shareholder-yield term in the return bridge is negative.

And we have no edge. Our base fair value of $300 is below the street's $322.68, against a configuration of 84 buys, 9 holds and 1 sell. When our number is below consensus and our reasoning is a known, discussed, widely-modelled risk, the honest verdict is not Buy. Base +8.1%, bear −17.4%, bull +35.2%, at a 52-week high, on a gap-down day, with 86 days to the next catalyst. That is a Watch.

Pre-registered KILL criteria — what would move this from Watch to Avoid:

  • A further shortening of estimated useful lives. Management has moved server lives twice in three years; the last move cost $1.4B of depreciation, "primarily" in AWS. A move from five years to four across the full base, against $200B a year of spend, would be a multi-tens-of-billions annual charge appearing without warning in a footnote.
  • AWS growth below 30% in any quarter while capital expenditure is still rising. 37% in Q2'26 is the number to hold. The adverse combination is decelerating revenue against accelerating spend, which is the precise shape of a capacity overbuild.
  • FY2026 capital expenditure closing above $220B — the level Josh Brown named on 2026-07-28 as a negative "even if right for the business" — without a corresponding rise in the disclosed remaining-performance-obligation figure above $496B.
  • The remaining performance obligation flat or falling at the next disclosure. This is the bull case's single load-bearing fact and it is disclosed quarterly.
  • Operating margin below 12% for two consecutive quarters. Q3'26 is already guided to roughly 12.3% at the midpoint.
  • Evidence of stress in the debt issuance — a materially wider new-issue spread, a pulled deal, or reliance on the commercial paper programme, which currently carries zero drawn against a $30.0B capacity.

Pre-registered FLIP conditions — what turns this into Buy — Tactical:

  • A single quarter in which operating cash flow growth exceeds capital expenditure growth, i.e. visible free-cash-flow inflection. This is the primary trigger and it is checkable in one line of the cash-flow statement.
  • A price at or below approximately $247 (the 50-day average) on sector beta rather than company-specific news, which puts the base case at roughly +21% and the bear case at only −7%.
  • First use of the $6.1B repurchase authorisation. After three years of zero, any repurchase is an unmistakable statement that management believes the spending peak has passed.
  • The next disclosed remaining performance obligation materially above $496B with the weighted-average life holding near 6.4 years — the backlog outgrowing the capex is the cleanest possible refutation of the depreciation objection.
  • Verifiable evidence that the in-house accelerator programme is materially lowering Amazon's own cost per unit of compute — Gavin Baker's conviction-82 claim carries an explicit test ("Trainium 3 ramps in second half of the year"), and the second half of 2026 is now.

Where AMZN fits in the Synthos Framework Portfolio. Not yet. A 0% position with a monitoring flag, to be sized at 2-3% in the mega-cap compounder sleeve on any of the flip conditions above. For readers who already hold Amazon, this dive argues to hold and not to add; for readers who do not, it argues to wait for either the cash-flow inflection or the price. Note the batch overlap explicitly: an investor holding AMD, Micron, Lam, KLA, Applied, Intel, Marvell or ASML already owns the receiving end of Amazon's capital expenditure. Those positions and an Amazon position are not diversification — they are opposite sides of the same question, and today demonstrated that they can move violently against each other in a single session. Sizing should be done across the pair, not name by name. Logged as a tracked Synthos call (Watch) as of 2026-08-04 at $277.42.

Single biggest risk: the depreciation schedule. FY2025 depreciation and amortisation was $65.8B, or 9.2% of revenue. Capital expenditure is running near $200B a year against a five-year life on servers per the 10-K. Steady-state depreciation from that spending level heads toward $150B or more — roughly 16% of FY2027 consensus revenue. That charge arrives on a contractual, arithmetic schedule regardless of customer behaviour. The $496B contracted backlog is the answer to it, and it may well be sufficient. But the depreciation is certain and the conversion is not, and the market began pricing that asymmetry today.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that free cash flow turns positive again by FY2027. Everything else in the valuation follows from it. If operating cash flow does not reach roughly $210B — a 30% increase from $161.4B — while capital expenditure holds near $200B, Amazon spends a third consecutive year consuming rather than generating cash, funded by debt at 4.60-6.25%, with no dividend or buyback, and a rising share count. A $3 trillion company that consumes cash for three years does not keep a 26x forward multiple, however good its business is.
