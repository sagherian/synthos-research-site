The Overview

Aon is one of the three big insurance brokers in the world. It does not insure anything itself; it arranges cover for large companies and takes a commission. It also advises on pensions, benefits and pay. It is a good business: clients rarely switch, and the fees rise as insurance gets more expensive.

The results published on 29 July were fine. Underlying sales grew 5%. Profit margin improved. Profit per share came in at $3.81 where analysts expected $3.80 — a beat of one cent.

Two things are less good. Total sales grew only 2%, because Aon sold part of a business it had bought. And the cash the business actually generated in the quarter fell 34%, to $483 million from $732 million. Management says that is a tax payment on the business it sold plus timing on working capital. The next results, on 30 October, will show whether that is right.

The shares have done nothing for a year: up 0.9%, against a stock market up 24%.

The reason is on the balance sheet. Aon spent heavily on acquisitions, and the accounting value of what it bought — goodwill and intangibles — is $21.5 billion, against shareholders' funds of $9.4 billion. Subtract one from the other and the company's tangible net worth is about minus $12 billion. It owes $15.2 billion. The market has marked it down for that, and it is right to.

The encouraging part is that management is fixing it: debt fell by $1.7 billion in the first six months of this year.

At $356.66 the shares cost about 16.7 times what analysts expect for 2027. Rivals usually cost more. Our estimate of fair value is $395, about 11% above the price. Analysts average $404.43 — though notably, nineteen of the thirty-eight covering the stock say Hold.

One note on our own knowledge base: it contains not one claim about Aon out of 52,021. We say so rather than reaching for something adjacent.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Brokers earn commission and fee income on risk transfer without taking underwriting risk; revenue scales with insured values and with premium rates, both of which rise structurally as the world's assets become more valuable and its risks more complex; and client relationships are sticky because placing a large corporate programme requires institutional knowledge that takes years to rebuild. Three readings.

The reported top line understates the business by three points and the gap is disclosed. Total revenue grew 2% while organic revenue grew 5%; the difference is the NFP Wealth divestiture, completed in the fourth quarter of 2025, which the 10-Q identifies as "NFP's Wealth businesses within our Human Capital segment." That drag annualises out of the comparison by the fourth quarter of 2026, at which point reported and organic growth converge.

The margin is doing the work and it is doing it well. Adjusted operating income rose 5% on revenue up 2%, and 7% on revenue up 4% across the half, with the adjusted operating margin near 28% and expanding. Management's own framing — "adjusted operating margin expansion, strong adjusted EPS growth and double-digit free cash flow growth" — is half-weighted per house rule, and the third of those three claims is not what the quarter shows.

Free cash flow fell 34% in the quarter and that is the number to hold on to. $483 million against $732 million, with the half up only 4% to $846 million against $816 million. Management attributes the shortfall to "the cash tax payment related to NFP Wealth and impact of working capital" offsetting adjusted operating income growth. That is a plausible and specific explanation for a one-off; it is also the single largest negative datum in this file, and on a company whose investment case rests on cash generation funding deleveraging, it matters more than the one-cent earnings beat.

And the beat itself is the narrowest in this batch. $3.81 against $3.80 — 0.3% — on revenue 0.7% BELOW estimate. Against the prior four prints (+4.8%, +1.7%, +2.1%, +2.6%), Aon's beats are consistently small, which is what a well-modelled recurring-revenue business produces and is a point in its favour rather than against.

4a. What today's price assumes (the inversion)

At $356.66 — 18.7x the 2026 consensus and 16.7x 2027 — the price embeds roughly:

  • Adjusted EPS of $19.09 in 2026 and $21.34 in 2027. (Consensus; 14 analysts each, with a 7.9% range on 2027.) The June quarter delivered $3.81 against a September consensus of $3.41; management has flagged a timing item affecting third-quarter adjusted EPS with a favourable offset thereafter.
  • Organic revenue growth stays at or above 5%. (Filed.) Reported revenue grew only 2% in the quarter and consensus assumes 4.4% in 2026 rising to 6.5% in 2027 — i.e. the NFP Wealth drag annualising out and organic holding. If organic slips to 3%, the earnings path does not survive.
