The Overview

Apollo does two things that look unrelated and are not.

First, it manages money for other people — private equity, and especially private credit, meaning loans made outside the banking system. Second, since merging with a company called Athene, it sells retirement annuities. People hand Apollo money now in exchange for income later, and Apollo invests that money — largely in loans it makes itself.

That is the clever part of the design, and independent commentators in our research base rate it highly. An ordinary fund manager has to go and raise money every few years. Apollo's annuity business supplies money that stays for decades. So Apollo lends, holds the loans against the annuities, earns the difference, and charges a fee on top.

Right now the two halves are moving in opposite directions. In the first three months of the year, the fee business earned $728 million, up 30%. The annuity spread business earned $719 million, down 11% — mostly because what Apollo has to pay annuity holders rose 27%.

Apollo reported its April-to-June results this morning. Profit per share of $2.11 came in slightly below the $2.16 expected — the first miss in five quarters. The shares went up 2.85% anyway, to $133.11.

Two warnings you should hear.

The first is about the data. This is the messiest company file we have examined. Because Apollo consolidates an insurance company, the "revenue" figure in standard data feeds is meaningless — the same file contains three different revenue numbers for overlapping periods that differ by more than twenty times. The "free cash flow" figure is also wrong, because the capital-spending line has been recorded as exactly zero for three years running. We have thrown away every ratio built on either.

The second is about private credit. Several independent commentators — including the single most highly-weighted analyst we track — have been arguing since March that private credit funds are facing a wave of withdrawals and that the value they place on their loans is too optimistic. One names Apollo's own retail credit fund specifically, saying withdrawal requests rose from 11% to 16.8% of the fund. We could not check this. That fund reports separately and none of the documents we have covers it. We report the claim, we report that we cannot verify it, and we treat the uncertainty as a reason to wait.

Our fair value estimate is $144, about 8% above the price. Analysts on average say $150.67. Neither is far enough above $133 to justify buying into a name we cannot fully audit.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Three things follow from that table and they are the whole analysis.

First, the two engines are now the same size. $728 million against $719 million. A year earlier the split was $559 million against $804 million — the insurer was 44% larger than the asset manager. It is now smaller.

Second, the spread decline is a funding-cost story, not an asset-quality story.
