The Overview

When you play a free mobile game and an advertisement appears between levels, something has to decide, in a fraction of a second, which advertisement you see and what the advertiser pays for it. AppLovin makes the software that decides. Its engine, called Axon, predicts which users are most likely to install and spend money in an advertised app, and advertisers pay AppLovin for delivering those users.

That turns out to be an astonishingly profitable thing to sell. The company employs 898 people and last year collected $5.5 billion. Of every dollar it collects, about 88 cents is left after the direct cost of running the servers, and about 64 cents ends up as profit. It spends almost nothing on equipment — $28 million last year, against $4 billion of cash generated. There are not many businesses like this anywhere.

Until the middle of 2025 AppLovin also owned a portfolio of mobile games. It sold them, so the company you are looking at now is one thing only: the advertising engine.

Here is the part that requires care. AppLovin's revenue is the number of app installations it delivers, multiplied by how much it charges for each one. Last year the number of installations barely moved — up 3% — while the price per installation rose 72%. In the first quarter of this year the number of installations actually fell 18%, while the price per installation rose 93%. So the company is not growing by delivering more; it is growing by charging much more for less. That can be a sign of a genuinely better product — a better-targeted install is worth more, so advertisers pay more. It can also be a sign of a business running out of room. From outside the company there is no way to tell, because the price is set inside AppLovin's own auction, not in a market anyone else can observe.

The shares cost $419.70. That is 43% below where they were at their peak within the last year. Analysts expect the company to earn about $21.57 per share in 2027, which means you are paying about 19.5 times that. For a business growing 30% a year with these margins, that is not an expensive price — and the average analyst target is $649.53, more than 50% above where the shares trade.

Our own estimate is $470, which is 12% above the price and well below what the analysts say. And the company reports its quarterly results tomorrow morning. When our number is only modestly above the price, sits far below the crowd's, and a scheduled event with the power to move the stock 20% in either direction is one day away, the honest answer is to name the price we would want and wait for it.

1. What the business is, and what the disclosure will and will not tell you

AppLovin describes a platform, not a product line. Its advertising solutions, per the 10-K, are Axon Ads Manager, MAX, Adjust and Wurl:

  • Axon Ads Manager — the self-serve platform advertisers use to set goals and let the engine optimise spend. The 10-K states flatly that it "comprises the vast majority of revenue" and that this revenue "is determined dynamically based on advertisers' campaign goals." The Axon branding was launched publicly in 2025 alongside the self-serve platform.
  • MAX — the mediation tool advertising networks use to buy app inventory; revenue is a percentage of client spend.
  • Adjust — measurement, attribution and fraud prevention, acquired April 2021; revenue is primarily an annual software subscription.
  • Wurl — connected-television content distribution and advertising, acquired April 2022; usage- and CPM-based.

No revenue figure is disclosed for any of the four. Since the Apps divestiture the 10-K states: "Following the sale of the Apps business, we operate as a single operating and reportable segment." The seg_prod block reflects that honestly — the FY2025 entry is a single line, "Reportable Segment": $5,480,717,000, which reconciles to reported revenue exactly.

That is the central disclosure fact about this company and it cuts both ways. There is no way, from public filings, to size Axon Ads Manager against MAX, to size mobile gaming against the e-commerce vertical the company has been expanding into since 2024, or to size Wurl's connected-television business. The 10-K discusses e-commerce in the risk factors and in the strategy narrative — "since 2024 we have begun expanding our customer base to include web-based e-commerce advertisers" — and never once quantifies it.

The concentration the 10-K discloses is structural, not customer-level. No single customer is named as exceeding 10% of revenue. Elsewhere the same filing lists "some of the largest global internet platforms, such as Meta and Google" among its advertising clients. Meta and Google are simultaneously the distribution layer, large customers, and the most capable competitors. That is the long-run risk in one sentence, and it is the company's own.

Governance: a controlled company. Three share classes. Class A has one vote, Class B has 20 votes, Class C has none. AppLovin is a "controlled company" under Nasdaq rules and may elect not to comply with certain corporate-governance requirements.

Volume has gone from +50% to +3% to −18% in two years while price has gone from +22% to +72% to +93%. The composition of AppLovin's growth has completely inverted, and the total has held up only because price accelerated faster than volume decayed.

There are two readings and we do not have the evidence to choose between them.

The benign reading is that this is what a better model does. Axon targets more precisely, so each installation it delivers is worth more to the advertiser, so the advertiser bids more and AppLovin serves fewer, better-qualified installations. Under this reading, falling volume is a feature: the engine is discarding low-value inventory. The margin expansion — gross margin from 83.9% to 88.9%, operating margin from 59.3% to 78.2% — is consistent with it, because serving fewer, higher-value impressions costs less infrastructure per revenue dollar.

The adverse reading is that the mobile-gaming install base is contracting, that AppLovin is harvesting price from a shrinking pool of advertisers who have nowhere better to go, and that the price series has a ceiling somewhere. Under this reading, the 93% figure is not evidence of strength but of how hard the company is now having to push a single lever.

What makes this the risk rather than a curiosity is that net revenue per installation is not a market price. It is the realised outcome of an auction AppLovin operates, into an inventory pool AppLovin mediates, measured by a metric AppLovin defines and does not further disaggregate. There is no external series that corroborates or contradicts it. For a company where this one number is now the whole growth story, that is a genuine analytical gap and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

The 2026-08-05 print is the next observation. Whether installations fall again, and whether revenue per installation holds near 90%, is more informative than the revenue headline.
