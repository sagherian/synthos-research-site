The Overview

Broadcom designs chips but does not manufacture them. Its two businesses are, first, designing custom processors for a small number of very large technology companies that want their own artificial-intelligence hardware instead of buying standard parts — plus the networking chips that wire those machines together — and second, selling infrastructure software to large enterprises, mostly the VMware business it bought in 2023.

The first of those businesses has just done something unusual. In its quarterly report filed on 9 June, Broadcom disclosed that it has $164.6 billion of firmly committed customer orders on its books. Seven months earlier, in the annual report, the same figure was $33.3 billion. The company says the jump came from one long-term contract for custom artificial-intelligence processors signed in the February-to-May quarter. About 30% of the total is expected to turn into sales within a year — roughly $49 billion, against total sales last year of $64 billion.

Sales in the May quarter grew 48% from a year earlier. Analysts expect sales to grow 65% this fiscal year and another 65% next. That is extraordinary for a company of this size.

Now the awkward part. If you divide the share price by the profit Broadcom actually reported under standard accounting rules over the last four quarters, you get a multiple of about 70 times. That is very expensive. Two things cause it. First, when Broadcom bought VMware it had to record the value of the acquired technology and customer relationships as an asset and then write it off over time — $8.2 billion of write-offs last year alone, which is a bookkeeping charge, not cash going out the door. Second, Broadcom pays its staff heavily in shares — $7.6 billion last year, about 12 cents of every dollar of sales — and that is a real cost, because it means existing shareholders own a little less each year.

We treat the first as an accounting artefact and the second as a genuine expense. On the measure analysts actually use, the shares cost about 21 times what Broadcom is expected to earn in the year to November 2027. For this growth rate, that is not expensive.

Three things to hold against it. Five customers account for about 45% of sales and a single distributor for 32% — if one of them decides to design its own chips, a large part of this disappears. Broadcom has quietly guaranteed a customer's equipment lease payments up to a maximum of $29 billion, which appears in no debt figure anywhere. And if you subtract goodwill and acquired intangibles, the company's book value is negative.

And the timing. The shares rose 6.61% today — from $392.23 to $418.16 — but so did the whole semiconductor sector; one peer rose 12.8%, another 10.9%. Broadcom announced nothing. Anyone buying tonight is paying 6.6% more than yesterday for exactly the same information. Our estimate of fair value is $470, about 12% above the price. Buy some; keep money back for the gap to close.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business actually is

The vendor's own company description is stale and should be discarded. It says Broadcom's "operations are segmented into four primary divisions: Wired Infrastructure, Wireless Communications, Enterprise Storage, and Industrial & Other" and that it "maintains a significant team of 19,000 full-time staff." The four-division structure is the FY2018 reporting format — the vendor's own seg_prod block shows it was abandoned after that year — and the 19,000 headcount contradicts the profile.employees field of 33,000 inside the same payload. The 10-K is unambiguous: "We have two reportable segments: semiconductor solutions and infrastructure software." We use the filing.

The software line went from $7.637B to $27.029B in two years — that is the VMware acquisition (closed 2023-11-22) plus the conversion of that base to subscription. The 10-K attributes FY2025 software growth to "strong demand for our VCF product, including license revenue recognized on contracts where customers do not have the right to terminate and the transition to a subscription license model," and semiconductor growth to "strong demand for our networking solutions, primarily custom AI accelerators and AI networking products."

One disclosure materially changes how the software line should be read. The 10-K states: "In fiscal year 2025, we included upfront license revenue of $7,800 million within products revenue. To conform to the current year presentation, we reclassified $4,601 million of upfront license revenue from subscriptions and services revenue to products revenue for fiscal year 2024." $7.8 billion — 12.2% of total company revenue and 28.9% of the software segment — is point-in-time upfront license recognition, not subscription. That is a lumpier, less annuity-like revenue stream than the word "software" implies, and it is the mechanism by which a software segment can grow 25.8% in a year.

The 10-K adds the detail that matters for policy risk: "In fiscal years 2025 and 2024, 17% and 20%, respectively, of our net revenue came from shipments or deliveries to China (including Hong Kong)," while noting that end demand is frequently located elsewhere.

Customer concentration is the defining structural feature and it is getting worse. The 10-K: "Direct sales to one semiconductor solutions customer, which is a distributor, accounted for 32% and 28% of our net revenue for fiscal years 2025 and 2024" and "we believe aggregate sales to our top five end customers, through all channels, accounted for approximately 40% of our net revenue for each of the fiscal years 2025 and 2024." The 10-Q updates the second figure: "approximately 45% of our net revenue" for the May 2026 quarter and the two quarters then ended, against 40% a year earlier.

Headcount 33,000 (profile). No contradicting figure was located in the extractable 10-K text; the DESC field's 19,000 is rejected.

One structural note on the fiscal calendar. Broadcom operates on a 52- or 53-week year ending the Sunday closest to 31 October. FY2025 ended 2025-11-02 and was a 52-week year; FY2024 was a 53-week year, which flatters FY2024 comparisons slightly and is worth remembering when reading the +23.9% FY2025 growth rate.

2. Three earnings numbers, a 70x headline, and which one we value on

Trailing four-quarter GAAP diluted EPS is $6.00. At $418.16 that is a 69.7x multiple, and the vendor's own priceToEarningsDilutedRatioTTM of 69.69 confirms the arithmetic. Quoting that number without explaining it would be dishonest in either direction.

Here is what suppresses it, taken from the filings rather than asserted.

(a) Amortisation of acquisition-related intangibles: $8.201B in FY2025. The 10-K discloses depreciation expense separately — "Depreciation expense was $574 million, $593 million and $502 million for fiscal years 2025, 2024 and 2023" — and total depreciation and amortisation for FY2025 was $8.775B. The residual, $8.201B, is amortisation of purchased intangibles: 12.8% of revenue and 35.5% of pre-tax income. The independent cross-check is the balance sheet: net intangible assets fell from $40.583B to $32.273B over the year, a decline of $8.310B, consistent with a small amount of additions. This charge is non-cash and is a direct consequence of purchase accounting for VMware. The 10-K confirms the run-off is already beginning: amortisation "recognized in operating expenses decreased $1,213 million, or 37%, in fiscal year 2025 ... primarily due to full amortization of customer-related intangible assets from previous software acquisitions other than VMware."

(b) Stock-based compensation: $7.568B in FY2025, 11.6% of revenue. The 10-K: "Total stock-based compensation expense was $7,568 million and $5,670 million for fiscal years 2025 and 2024." The 10-Q shows the run-rate rising: $2,092M in the May quarter against $1,771M a year earlier, and $4,268M for two quarters against $3,051M. There is $20.106B of unrecognised cost still to be expensed over a 3.0-year weighted service period, and $13.527B of restricted units vested in the first half of FY2026 alone.

(c) A tax benefit, not a tax charge. FY2025 carries a $397 million benefit from income taxes on $22.729B of pre-tax income, "primarily due to the recognition of uncertain tax benefits from expiration of statutes of limitations and audit settlements, and excess tax benefits from stock-based awards." Trailing effective tax rate: 3.8%. This flatters GAAP earnings in the opposite direction from (a) and (b), and it is not sustainable — the 10-K warns that Singapore's global minimum tax "will become effective in our fiscal year ending November 1, 2026" and that "we expect a material impact from the enactment of these laws on our consolidated results of operations and cash flows for our fiscal year 2026."

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over 12-24 months decomposes as: EPS growth (+42.7% in FY2026 and +68.6% in FY2027 on consensus) + multiple drift (we assume material COMPRESSION, from 21.4x FY2027E to roughly 24x — which is expansion against the forward year but a large compression against the 36.1x being paid on FY2026) + shareholder yield of ~0.6% (dividend only, because the buyback does not shrink the count).
