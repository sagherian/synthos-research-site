The Overview

Bank of America is one of the four largest banks in the United States. It takes deposits from about seventy million customers, lends the money out, manages roughly $4.9 trillion of wealth-management client balances through Merrill and its private bank, and runs a large trading and investment-banking operation.

It has just had a very good year. In the three months to June it earned $9.1 billion, up from $7.2 billion a year earlier — a 34% increase in earnings per share. The reasons are straightforward: it is earning a wider margin on deposits than a year ago, its trading business had an exceptional quarter with revenue up a third, and fees from arranging deals rose 50%. Meanwhile the losses it takes on loans that go bad actually FELL, which is the opposite of what people worried about eighteen months ago.

The board has responded by raising the dividend 14%, and the bank is buying back about $24 billion of its own shares a year. Between the dividend and the buybacks, roughly 7% of the company's value is being handed back to shareholders annually. That is a genuinely strong return and it is the main reason to own this.

Two things argue against paying up today. The first is simply price: the shares closed at the highest level of the past year, and our estimate of what they are worth is $65 against a price of $62.92 — about 3% of upside, and slightly less than the average analyst expects. The second is a rule change. Banks the size of Bank of America must hold a minimum amount of capital, and regulators set that minimum. The bank's own filing says the surcharge that determines its minimum is expected to go UP on 1 January 2027. Today it holds 11.2% against a 10.0% requirement — a cushion of just over one percentage point. If the requirement rises, some of the money currently going into buybacks has to stay in the bank instead.

A note on the data. This is the most error-strewn company file in this batch, and we say so rather than quietly working around it. The data vendor reports Bank of America's revenue as $191.6 billion; the real, meaningful figure — after deducting the interest the bank pays out — is $113.1 billion. One quarter of the income series is reported on a different basis from the rest. The geographic table adds up a region and then adds the same region's parts again, double-counting $15.4 billion. And it reports an "enterprise value" of $1.15 trillion, which treats customer deposits as if they were company debt. None of those numbers appears in our conclusions.

The single most important disclosure point: which "revenue" is real

A bank has two revenue numbers and only one of them means anything. Gross revenue — interest income plus noninterest income — includes the interest the bank pays depositors and bondholders on the way in. The figure banks actually report and manage to is "total revenue, net of interest expense."

The vendor reports the gross figure and labels it revenue. For FY2025 it prints $191.567B. The correct figure is $113.097B — $191.567B less $78.470B of interest expense. Every margin the vendor computes off the gross number is therefore meaningless: a "gross margin" of 56.1%, an "operating margin" of 19.7% and a "net margin" of 15.9% are all divided by a denominator 69% too large.

Two independent checks confirm the $113.097B figure and they are worth showing, because they also prove the correct interpretation of the geographic table:

1. FY2025: $191.567B − $78.470B = $113.097B. The vendor's seg_geo FY2025 rows, once the double-count is removed (below), sum to exactly $113.097B.

2. FY2024: $192.434B − $90.547B = $101.887B. The vendor's seg_geo FY2024 rows sum to exactly $101.887B.

And the 10-K names the basis directly: the geographic disclosure presents "revenue, net of interest expense, income before income taxes and net income by geographic area." The vendor's geographic table is on the correct bank basis while its income statement is not.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+13.1%, FY2026E $4.633 to FY2027E $5.242) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 12.0x to roughly 11.5x on the respective forward year, −4%) + shareholder yield (+7.4% — but note the buyback component is already inside the EPS growth, so counting only the 2.03% dividend avoids double-counting) ≈ +11%, against a base fair value that is 3.3% above spot.

Our base assumes mild multiple compression, and the reason is regulatory rather than fundamental. The bank's earnings power is not in question; its ability to keep converting that earnings power into per-share growth at the current rate is, because the January 2027 G-SIB increase raises the capital that must be retained. A bank returning 7.4% of its market value annually deserves a higher multiple than one returning 5%, and the difference between those two states is a regulatory decision that has already been announced.

To be explicit about the guardrail: 13.6x FY2026 becoming 12.0x FY2027 at a constant price is mechanical rolldown. The compression we assume is a change in what the market pays at each date — 12.0x forward today to roughly 11.5x forward in a year — and it is modest.

Most of the expected return here is earnings growth and dividend, which is the healthiest possible composition, and it is why this is not an Avoid. If the multiple simply HELD at 12.0x on FY2028E of $5.865, the price would be $70.4 (+11.9%); at 10.5x, $61.6 (−2.1%). The spread is narrow. This is a low-variance security and the valuation debate is correspondingly small.
