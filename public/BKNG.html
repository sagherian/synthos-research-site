The Overview

Booking Holdings owns Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, Kayak and OpenTable. When you reserve a hotel room through one of them, Booking keeps roughly 14 cents of every dollar you spend. Last quarter people booked $51 billion of travel through its sites — up 9% — and Booking kept $7.35 billion of that as revenue.

The company reported those figures today, and the share price we are using may not have taken them in yet. They were slightly better than expected: revenue came in 2.2% above what analysts had forecast, and profit from operations rose 11% with margins improving.

The most striking thing in the accounts is not the trading. It is that Booking bought back 44 million of its own shares in six months for $7.8 billion — about 5.4% of the entire company in half a year, and more than it spent in all of 2025. That is why profit per share is rising much faster than profit: there are fewer shares to divide it between. It is also why the company's balance sheet shows negative equity of $10.8 billion — it has bought back more stock over the years than it has retained in profits. That sounds alarming and is not: it holds $17.2 billion of cash against $20.2 billion of borrowings.

The shares are $194.27. They are down 10% over the past year while the American stock market rose 24% — a 34-point gap. There is one clear reason, and Booking states it in its own filing: fewer people are arriving through ordinary Google searches, and the company expects that to keep declining, which may mean paying more for advertising. In plain terms, the market is worried that when people start asking an AI assistant to book their holiday, Booking may not be the answer.

Nobody knows how that resolves. Our research library has both sides: an independent investor who thinks the fear has created a reasonable discount and values the shares at about $220, and Booking's own chief executive who says AI is a helpful tool rather than a threat — while adding, honestly, that "no moat is permanent."

On the numbers, the shares cost about 16 times what analysts expect the company to earn next year, and it generates cash equal to about 6% of its market value every year. Our estimate of fair value is $228, about 17% higher, which happens to match the average analyst almost exactly.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Plenty of cash, very high returns on capital, low capital needs — against a structural question the company itself has raised and cannot yet answer.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Revenue growing 8-9%, room nights 5%. Respectable; the profit-per-share growth is substantially bought with buybacks.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. A mature, dominant toll booth. The one genuinely non-linear thing about it right now is the risk of being re-routed.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $228 against a current price of $194.27 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Reading the growth, using the company's own bridge

The 10-Q decomposes the 9.0% gross-bookings increase exactly: "a 5% increase in room nights, approximately 2% higher constant currency ADRs, a positive impact from changes in foreign currency exchange rates of about 1%, and a positive impact from the growth in flight gross bookings."

So roughly 5 points of the 9 is volume and the remainder is price, currency and product mix. For the half the bridge is "a 6% increase in room nights, about 4% positive impact from changes in foreign currency exchange rates, approximately 2% higher constant currency ADRs" — so in the first half, currency contributed nearly as much as volume.

Three further readings that a headline does not give you.

First, the merchant-versus-agency split is a deliberate mix shift, not a demand signal. Merchant gross bookings grew 14.5% and merchant revenues 15.0% while agency fell 3.3% and 6.9%. The 10-Q: "This shift also contributed to the year-over-year decrease in agency revenues", referring to "the ongoing shift from agency to merchant bookings at Booking.com." Merchant means Booking takes the payment; agency means the property does. The shift brings payment economics and working capital in-house and is the reason the take-rate decline was only 10 basis points rather than more — the 10-Q credits "an increase in revenues related to facilitating payments" as the partial offset.

Second, the Middle East conflict is named repeatedly and specifically. "the Rest of World region, which includes the Middle East, returned to year-over-year growth following a decline in the first quarter due to the onset of the conflict in the Middle East. While direct impacts on travel within the region largely normalized in the second quarter, there were continued indirect impacts including elevated flight ticket prices, reduced flight capacity on certain routes, and a decrease in long-haul international travel demand, which negatively impacted overall room night growth." The company is telling you the 5.3% room-night growth was suppressed, and it names the reason. Flight gross bookings grew 12% with "higher average flight ticket prices... driven in part by higher fuel costs associated with the conflict in the Middle East" — the same event helping the flight line and hurting the room-night line.

Third, rental cars are shrinking. Rental car days fell 6.5% in the quarter and rental car gross bookings fell 1% — so days down, prices up. The 10-Q attributes it to "lower partner volume, as well as the impact of higher average daily car rental prices and the conflict in the Middle East on travel demand."

The disclosure that explains the share price

> From the 10-Q, verbatim: "Marketing efficiency is also impacted by the extent to which consumers book directly with us. The mix of total room nights booked by consumers coming directly to our platforms was a mid-fifties percentage over the trailing twelve months ended June 30, 2026 and June 30, 2025, respectively... This was achieved despite declines in traffic through organic or unpaid search results, which is what we call Search Engine Optimization ("SEO"). Room nights booked through the SEO channel remain a small component of our overall room nights. While we seek to adapt to evolving search engine dynamics, we expect SEO traffic to decline in the short to medium term, which may lead to increased spend in paid marketing channels."

This is the single most important paragraph in the file and it is the reason a business compounding revenue at 8-11% with a 34% operating margin trades 15.1% below its high and 34 points behind the index over twelve months.

Read it carefully, because it cuts both ways. The bad half: the company expects organic search traffic to keep declining and says the consequence may be higher paid marketing spend. That is a direct statement that customer-acquisition cost is going up. The good half: the direct-booking mix held at "a mid-fifties percentage" in both periods — unchanged year on year despite the SEO decline — and the company states that "room nights booked through the SEO channel remain a small component of our overall room nights."

We treat the paragraph as an unresolved, unquantified, company-disclosed structural risk, and we do not pretend the filing settles it in either direction. It does not.

5b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: adjusted EPS growth (+17.9%, from 2026E $10.457 to 2027E $12.326) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 18.6x on the current forward year to roughly 15.9x on the then-forward year, −14%) + shareholder yield (+0.83% dividend, with the buyback already inside the EPS growth) ≈ +4% to +6% at a constant relative multiple.

Our base of $228 therefore requires more than that, and we should be explicit: it assumes a modest RE-RATING. $228 is 18.5x the 2027 consensus against today's 15.8x — a 17% multiple expansion. That is the fragile leg of this thesis and we name it as such rather than burying it.

The justification is specific. The 15.8x multiple is not where this business has traded; it is a discount applied since the search-disintermediation fear took hold, and the twelve-month relative performance of −34 points against the index measures its size. The re-rating does not require the fear to be disproved — it requires only that it stops widening, which is what the unchanged direct-booking mix and the 2.2% revenue beat filed today are early evidence of.

If the multiple simply HELD at 15.8x on the 2028 consensus of $14.306, the price would be $226 (+16.3%) — so the base is reachable on either route, expansion on 2027 or rolldown at a constant multiple on 2028. That symmetry is why we are comfortable with a base that requires re-rating on one measure: it does not require it on the other.

Buying a 9%-growing, 34%-margin, 68%-return-on-capital business at 15.8x with a 6.3% shareholder yield, on the day it beat revenue by 2.2%, is a reasonable bet that the discount stops widening. It is not a bet that the discount is wrong, and it should be sized accordingly.
