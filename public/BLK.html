The Overview

BlackRock invests other people's money and charges a small percentage for doing it. It does this on a scale nobody else approaches: $15.3 trillion, which is roughly half the annual output of the United States economy. Most of that money sits in index funds and exchange-traded funds — the iShares brand — which charge very little, often less than a tenth of one percent per year.

That is the problem the company has been solving. Charging a tenth of a percent on an enormous pile is a fine business, but the fee keeps falling, and a business whose price per unit falls every year has to grow the pile faster than the price falls just to stand still. So over the last two years BlackRock spent about $28 billion buying four companies that do the opposite kind of investing — infrastructure, private credit, private real estate, and a data business that supports all three. Those charge ten to twenty times as much per dollar managed, and they also take a share of the profits.

The results so far are good. The money coming in the door, net of money leaving, was $868 billion over the last year. The fees the company earns on its existing assets grew 10% organically — meaning not counting the companies it bought — which is the fastest that number has moved in about a decade. Profit margins went up even while the company was absorbing four acquisitions at once.

The share price has not noticed. Over the past twelve months BlackRock shares are up 3.6%, while the American stock market as a whole is up 24.3%. That is a very large gap for a business that is compounding.

There are three reasons to be careful. The first is bookkeeping: the official profit figure ($35.31 per share last year) and the figure the company prefers ($48.09) differ by more than a third, because of accounting charges from the acquisitions. The second is that the data file we use to price this company undercounts the number of shares: it uses 155 million when the true number, including the units issued to the sellers of HPS, is 162.6 million — and up to 4.4 million more may still be issued. The third is the most important: the private-credit business it bought is exactly the corner of finance that several of the sharpest people in our research library, writing in the last five months, say is heading for trouble.

Our estimate of fair value is $1,290 against a price of $1,131.11, about 14% higher, plus a dividend of roughly 2%. The average analyst says $1,321, and — unusually — not one of the 33 analysts covering it has a target below today's price.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

5b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)
