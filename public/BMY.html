The Overview

Bristol Myers Squibb makes prescription medicines — cancer drugs, a blood thinner, treatments for heart and immune conditions. Its business divides neatly in two, and the company itself presents it that way.

One half it calls the Growth Portfolio: newer drugs still building sales. Last quarter that half sold $7.6 billion, 15% more than a year earlier, and it is now the larger half of the company.

The other half it calls the Legacy Portfolio: older drugs. Most of these are shrinking fast because their patents have expired and cheap copies are now on sale — Revlimid's sales halved in a year, Pomalyst's fell 71%.

But one drug in the Legacy half is not shrinking. Eliquis, a blood thinner, sold $14.4 billion last year — nearly a third of everything the company sells — and it grew 22% last quarter. It is enormous, it is profitable, and its United States patent protection ends in 2028. Cheap copies are already on sale in Britain and Finland. When the same thing happens in America, most of that $14 billion goes away over two or three years.

That is why analysts expect the company's sales to fall from about $49 billion this year to about $37 billion in 2030, and its earnings from $6.74 per share to $4.86.

Now the price. The shares closed at $65.895, which is exactly the highest price in the last twelve months. They are up 49% over that period, twice what the whole stock market did. A standard momentum gauge reads 87 out of 100 — the highest in this research batch and conventionally very overbought. And the average analyst target is $65.38, which is below where the shares trade; half the analysts covering the stock say hold.

The recent news genuinely justifies some of the run: five days ago the company raised its forecast for this year by about $3 billion of sales.

Our estimate of fair value is $68, about 3% above the price. That is not enough to act on, especially at a twelve-month high after a 49% run.

One more thing worth knowing about the balance sheet: because Bristol Myers has bought so many biotech companies, the accounting value of what it owns, once you strip out goodwill, is negative $8.23 a share. That is common in pharmaceuticals and it is not a crisis — the company generates $11.4 billion of cash a year — but there is no asset cushion under the price.

  • Growth Quality 5/10. The growing half is genuinely growing 15%; the whole company's consensus earnings decline every year from here.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. One genuinely novel medicine with a documented setback, and a lot of ordinary pipeline.

It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. 2. The cliff, with dates

From the 10-K's exclusivity table, the single most important row in the filing:

> Eliquis (apixaban) — United States: 2028

Supporting disclosures from the same 10-K, all verbatim:

> "for Eliquis, generics have challenged the composition of matter patents and related SPCs in various jurisdictions, and trials have taken place, or are scheduled to take place, in certain European countries. While these legal proceedings are pending, generic manufacturers have begun marketing generic versions of Eliquis in certain EU countries and may seek to market generic versions of Eliquis in other EU countries prior to the expiration date of applicable patents."

> "following certain adverse judicial decisions in the UK and Finland, generic manufacturers have begun marketing generic versions of Eliquis in these countries."

> "In November 2025, BMS received a Notice Letter from Azurity Pharmaceuticals, Inc. notifying BMS that Azurity had filed a 505(b)(2) application containing a paragraph IV certification seeking approval to market apixaban products in the U.S. and challenging a formulation patent listed in the Orange Book for Eliquis but not the composition of matter patent."

> "As is common in the pharmaceutical industry, BMS expects that sales of its branded products like Orencia, Eliquis and Opdivo will decline after the loss of market exclusivity."

And the government-pricing layer on top, which is already in force:

> "In August 2024, as part of the first round of government price setting pursuant to the IRA, the HHS announced the 'maximum fair price' for a 30-day equivalent supply of Eliquis, which applies to the U.S. Medicare channel effective January 1, 2026. In November 2025, the HHS announced the 'maximum fair price' for a 30-day supply of Pomalyst, which applies to the U.S. Medicare channel effective January 1, 2027. In January 2026, the HHS selected Orencia as a medicine subject to 'negotiation' for government-set prices beginning in 2028."

Three things follow, and the third is the one most likely to be missed.

First, the near-term evidence is better than the framework predicts. Eliquis grew 22% in the June 2026 quarter — its first full quarter with the Inflation Reduction Act maximum fair price in effect for US Medicare. Volume growth is more than offsetting the mandated price cut. That is a genuine positive and it is a large part of why the guidance was raised.

Second, the consensus path takes the cliff seriously and dates it correctly. Revenue falls $6.0 billion in FY2028 alone ($47,684M to $41,685M), which is the year the 10-K names. The sell-side is not ignoring this; it is modelling it.

Third — and this is the part worth pausing on — three of Bristol Myers Squibb's largest products face government price-setting on a schedule that is independent of patent expiry. Eliquis (30.0% of revenue) from January 2026, Pomalyst (5.7%) from January 2027, Orencia (7.7%) from 2028. That is 43.4% of FY2025 revenue subject to administratively-set prices within three years, and the 10-K warns that further selections "could, among other things, accelerate revenue erosion prior to expiry of intellectual property protections." The patent cliff and the pricing regime arrive together.
