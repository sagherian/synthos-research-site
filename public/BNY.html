The Overview

When a pension fund buys a bond, somebody has to actually hold it, record who owns it, collect the interest, handle the tax paperwork, and make sure it arrives when it is sold. That somebody is usually a custodian bank, and BNY is the biggest one in the world. It is looking after $62.6 trillion of other people's securities.

This is a fee business, not a lending business. BNY does not really take credit risk — last quarter it actually released $8 million of loan-loss reserves because things got better. What it takes is volume risk: when markets go up and trading is busy, BNY's fees go up almost automatically, and because the computers it already owns can handle the extra work, most of that extra revenue turns into profit.

That is exactly what happened last quarter. Revenue rose 13%. Costs rose 7%. Profit before tax rose 23%. Earnings per share rose 27%. The company earned 31 cents of profit for every dollar of tangible shareholder equity, which is a remarkable number for a bank of any kind, and it is handing back 87 cents of every dollar it earns through dividends and buybacks. It just said it will raise the dividend 19%.

So why not buy it?

Because everyone already knows. The shares are up 57% in the last twelve months while the market is up 24%. They cost $156.80, which is 4.78 times the accounting value of the company's tangible assets. That is a high price for a bank, and it only makes sense if the 31% return keeps happening. If it drifts back to 25% — not a disaster, just a normal cycle — the same shares are worth about $125.

Two more honest notes. First, a big chunk of last quarter's strength came from interest income rising 20%, which happened because BNY reinvested maturing bonds at higher rates. That is a one-time catch-up, not a permanent improvement, and the company itself says deposit margins are already compressing. Second, the analysts who cover BNY mostly say Hold — 12 of them, against 10 buys — and their average target of $168.50 is only 7.5% above the price.

Our own estimate is $172. That is a decent return, not a compelling one, on a stock that has already run a long way. So: watch it, and know the number at which it becomes interesting.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

Two observations that cut in opposite directions.

Tangible book value per share compounded 11.0% year on year while the share count fell 3.8% and 87% of earnings were paid out. That is a very high-quality combination: the company grew intrinsic value per share while distributing almost everything it earned.

5. Where the multiple actually comes from

This section is short because the argument is one equation.

A bank's price-to-tangible-book multiple is, to a first approximation, its return on tangible common equity divided by the earnings multiple the market will pay. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+10.8%, from FY2026E $9.194 to FY2027E $10.187) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION of roughly 6%, from today's 17.1x on the forward year toward about 16.0x) + shareholder yield (+5.5%) ≈ +10%.

Our base assumes mild multiple compression, and the reason is the tangible-book cross-check rather than any view on the earnings. At $172 the stock would be 5.24x the current tangible book value of $32.81 — and tangible book itself is compounding at 11% a year, so by mid-2027 it would be roughly 4.7x the then-current figure. In other words the base case holds the price-to-tangible-book multiple roughly flat and lets book value growth plus earnings growth do the work, which is the correct way to value a custodian and is deliberately more conservative than holding the P/E flat.

Note what this makes the return: roughly half earnings growth and half shareholder yield, with the multiple a small negative. That is a respectable decomposition. It is not, at 9.7%, a compelling one — and the difference between this and the COF case in the same batch is instructive: there, the same shape produced a 14.9% base with 2.46:1 asymmetry at 9.3x earnings; here it produces 9.7% with 1.67:1 at 15.4x.

If the multiple held at 17.1x FY2027E the price would be $174 (+11.0%). If it compressed to 13x, $132 (−15.8%).
