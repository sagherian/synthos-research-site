The Overview

Berkshire Hathaway is a collection of businesses held together by an insurance company. The insurance operations — GEICO, plus a large commercial insurer and one of the world's biggest reinsurers — collect premiums today for claims that will be paid years from now. That pool of other people's money is called float, and it is currently about $177 billion. Berkshire invests it. Crucially, the insurance business has been profitable enough that Berkshire is effectively paid to hold the money rather than paying to borrow it. That is the engine.

Around that engine sit real businesses: America's biggest freight railroad, a large regulated electric and gas utility, a grocery and foodservice distributor, a truck-stop chain, and dozens of manufacturers making everything from carpet and paint to jet-engine parts and batteries. Greg Abel took over as chief executive on 1 January 2026. Warren Buffett remains chairman.

There is one thing that makes Berkshire's reported profit numbers unreliable, and it is the same problem Markel has. Accounting rules force the company to run every change in the value of its share portfolio through its quarterly profit figure. So the reported earnings measure the stock market at least as much as they measure the business. In 2025 Berkshire reported $67 billion of profit — but $30.7 billion of that was investment gains, and there was also an $8.3 billion write-down. The actual operating businesses earned roughly $44.6 billion, which is about $20.68 per share. Screens do not do this arithmetic, so almost every stock screener in the world shows Berkshire trading at about 15 times earnings. The real figure is about 24 times.

The other headline number is the cash. Berkshire is sitting on $373.5 billion of cash and short-term US government bills. That is $173 per share, against a share price of $517 — a third of what you pay for the stock is cash. Whether that is an asset or a problem depends entirely on what gets done with it, and right now it is earning Treasury-bill interest.

Which brings us to the most useful single fact in this report. Berkshire buys back its own shares only when management believes the price is below what the company is genuinely worth. In all of 2025 it bought none at all. It restarted on 4 March 2026, and describes the amount so far as minor. So the people who know the business best were willing buyers somewhere below roughly $490, and the stock now trades at $517. Whether they kept buying is disclosed on 8 August — four days from now.

Our estimate of fair value is $540 against a price of $517. The professional analysts who cover the stock have an average target of $465.50, which is ten percent below today's price. The stock closed today at its highest level in a year. None of that says sell a good business; all of it says do not start a position this week.
