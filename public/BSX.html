The Overview

Boston Scientific makes medical devices — stents, heart-rhythm equipment, endoscopy tools, devices for urology and spinal-cord stimulators. It is one of the largest medical device companies in the world.

Its share price has fallen from about $108 to $49.12 in the past year. That is a loss of roughly $89 billion of value.

The first thing to establish is whether the business fell with it. It did not. The quarterly report filed yesterday shows sales of $5.44 billion, up 7.5%; gross margin of 70.7%; and adjusted profit of $0.86 a share against the $0.835 analysts expected. That is the sixth consecutive quarter in which the company has beaten expectations.

So what happened? The filings answer it. On 4 February this year, Boston Scientific published its results for 2025 and the shares dropped sharply. A month later, shareholders sued, alleging the company had made "false and misleading statements concerning the Company's financial guidance and anticipated growth in the U.S. electrophysiology business unit."

Electrophysiology is the treatment of irregular heart rhythms, and Boston Scientific's newer technology there had been the reason investors paid a premium price for the shares. When growth in that business came in below what people expected, the premium disappeared. In July, the lawsuit was expanded to include an allegation that a company officer traded shares while holding information the public did not have.

That lawsuit is real, it is unresolved, and it is the main reason to be careful here.

Now the other side. Three things have happened that the standard data file does not contain.

First, the company started buying its own shares. Boston Scientific had bought back nothing in 2023, 2024 or 2025. In May it committed $2 billion in one transaction, at a reference price of $52.68 — above today's price — and it has $3 billion more approved.

Second, in July the board approved a cost programme intended to remove $500 million a year of expense by 2029.

Third, and most striking: the insiders are buying. Two directors bought shares in the open market. And on 31 July, the executive who runs the Cardiology division — the very division the lawsuit is about — acquired 64,198 shares at $46.73, roughly $3.0 million. There is not one discretionary sale in the file.

The shares cost $49.12. Analysts expect $3.43 of earnings in 2027, so you are paying about 14 times. This company traded above 25 times for most of the last decade. Of 44 analysts, none has a sell rating, and the most pessimistic target is $50 — above today's price.

Our estimate of fair value is $60, about 22% above the price, with a bear case of $39 and a bull case of $82 — so roughly three dollars of upside for every one of downside.

One warning about timing. On a common momentum measure (RSI), the shares are at 82 — the most "overbought" reading of the twelve companies in this batch — because they have already bounced 15% off their low. Buying a small position and adding on weakness is the right way to act on this; buying it all today is not.

2. The securities class action — the overhang, quoted in full

This is the most important disclosure in the file and it appears in no vendor field. From the 10-Q's commitments and contingencies note:

> "On March 5, 2026, purported Company shareholder John Rudolph Troike, individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, filed a putative securities class action complaint in the United States District Court for the District of Massachusetts against the Company and certain current officers stemming from the drop in the Company's stock price on February 4, 2026 following the release of the Company's fourth quarter and full year 2025 results. The complaint alleges violations of Sections 10(b) and 20(a) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 based on allegedly false and misleading statements concerning the Company's financial guidance and anticipated growth in the U.S. electrophysiology business unit. The complaint seeks, among other relief, unspecified compensatory damages, unspecified equitable relief, and costs and expenses. On May 20, 2026, the Court appointed the Indiana Public Retirement System as lead plaintiff. On July 20, 2026, plaintiffs filed an amended complaint, adding additional named plaintiffs Miami Fire Fighters' and Police Officers' Retirement Trust and Detroit General Retirement System, and additionally alleging violation of Section 20A of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 against a current officer."

Five observations, stated as facts rather than judgements.

First, this disclosure dates the collapse. The share-price drop occurred on 2026-02-04, on the release of fourth-quarter and full-year 2025 results. The earnings calendar shows that print BEAT: adjusted EPS of $0.80 against a $0.781 estimate. So the stock fell on guidance, not on results — which is exactly what the complaint alleges.

Second, it names the business. "The U.S. electrophysiology business unit." That is the pulsed-field ablation franchise inside Cardiovascular, and it is the business that took Boston Scientific's organic growth from mid-single digits to high teens between 2023 and 2025.

Third, the escalation on 2026-07-20 is material and recent. A Section 20A claim is the insider-trading provision of the Exchange Act: it alleges a defendant traded contemporaneously with the plaintiffs while in possession of material non-public information. It is directed at "a current officer." Two weeks after that amendment, the Executive Vice President and Group President of Cardiology acquired 64,198 shares in the open market — which is worth stating alongside the allegation without drawing a conclusion from it.

Fourth, three public pension funds are now named plaintiffs — Indiana Public Retirement System as lead, plus Miami Fire Fighters' and Police Officers' Retirement Trust and Detroit General Retirement System. Institutional lead plaintiffs are associated with cases that are litigated rather than dropped.

Fifth, and this is the honest quantification: the company has not accrued for it and neither can we. The 10-Q states generally that "Unless included in our legal accrual or otherwise indicated below, a range of loss associated with any individual material legal proceeding cannot be reasonably estimated." Securities class actions of this profile at companies of this size have historically settled in the hundreds of millions rather than the billions — against a $73 billion market capitalisation and $4.5 billion of annual operating cash flow. We do not deduct a specific number from fair value; we hold the verdict at Tactical because of it, and we make it the first kill criterion.

One further legal datum for scale: the June-quarter adjusted-EPS bridge contains $76M pre-tax of "Litigation-related net charges/credits", $0.04 per share — the ordinary run-rate of a medical-device company's legal costs and not obviously a reserve for this case.
