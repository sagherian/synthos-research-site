The Overview

Caterpillar makes the yellow machines — excavators, bulldozers, mining trucks — and, increasingly, something more interesting: the engines, turbines and generators that produce electricity.

That second business is why the shares have doubled in a year. Building an artificial-intelligence data centre requires an enormous amount of power, and getting connected to the electricity grid can take years. So the companies building them buy their own generators instead, and Caterpillar is one of a handful of firms in the world that can supply them at scale. The company's own filing says its power business is growing because of "increasing energy demand to support data center build-out related to cloud computing and generative Artificial Intelligence."

The evidence is in the order book. At the end of March, Caterpillar had $62.7 billion of firm orders it had not yet filled — an increase of $11.5 billion in three months, which is a startling number. About $24.8 billion of that is not due to be delivered for more than a year. For a company that sells roughly $75 billion a year, that is close to ten months of revenue already sold.

This morning the company reported results for the three months to June, and they were the best in its history: sales up 24%, profits up 65%, and earnings per share 31% above what analysts expected.

And here is the thing to understand. The shares opened 11% higher on that news, reached $935 — and then fell all afternoon to close at $876.27, barely nine dollars above the day's worst price. When a company beats expectations by 31% and its shares finish the day well off their highs, the market is saying it already knew.

Three things temper the enthusiasm. Import tariffs are costing Caterpillar between $2.2 and $2.4 billion this year — real money against roughly $11 billion of annual profit — and although the Supreme Court ruled in February that about $1.0 billion of those tariffs was imposed unlawfully, the company says it is not probable it will get the money back and has recorded nothing. Second, part of the growth in the construction business came from dealers stocking up rather than end customers buying, and that reverses. Third, price: the shares cost about 38 times last year's profit, where this company has historically traded in the teens.

Our estimate of fair value is $880 against a price of $876.27 — essentially the same number. The average analyst says $943. When our number equals the price and the shares just failed to hold an 11% gap on record results, the honest answer is to hold what you own.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

Caterpillar was founded in 1925, operated as Caterpillar Tractor Co. until 1986, and is now headquartered in Irving, Texas with 118,000 full-time employees and Joseph E. Creed as chief executive. NYSE-listed since 1929; CUSIP 149123101.

The segment structure was reorganised in 2025 and the vendor has not kept up

From the 10-Q filed 2026-05-06: the reportable segments are Power & Energy, Construction Industries, Resource Industries and Financial Products, plus an All Other Segment that is "included as a reconciling item between reportable segments and consolidated external reporting." The filing notes two structural facts that matter for reading any historical series:

  • "Power & Energy" is the renamed successor to what the vendor's pre-2025 rows call "Energy and Transportation."
  • Locomotives moved segments: "Responsibility for business strategy, product design, product management and development, manufacturing, marketing and sales and product support for diesel-electric, hybrid and battery-electric locomotives and components and other rail-related products and services, including remanufacturing and leasing moved from Power & Energy to Resource Industries."

Three observations.

First, $62.7 billion against trailing revenue of $74.729 billion is roughly ten months of sales already contracted. For a company whose historical problem was zero visibility past the next dealer order, that is a structural change in the quality of the business.

Second, $11.5 billion of net backlog growth in one quarter is larger than the entire Resource Industries segment's annual revenue. It is also larger than the sequential revenue increase, which means orders are being placed faster than they can be filled.

Third, and this is the risk rather than the reward: $24.8 billion — 40% of the backlog — is not expected to be delivered within twelve months. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+23.4%, FY2026E $24.783 to FY2027E $30.580) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 28.7x to roughly 24x on the respective forward year, −16%) + shareholder yield (+2.0%) ≈ +7% to +9%, against a base fair value 0.4% above spot.

Our base assumes meaningful multiple compression and we should be explicit about why it is not a rolldown artefact. Today the market pays 28.7x on the next fiscal year. In twelve months, paying 24x on the then-next year would be a genuine 16% de-rating. We assume it for two reasons. First, consensus growth decelerates on its own path: EPS +32.8%, +23.4%, +20.5%, then +2.9%. A multiple appropriate to 23% growth is not appropriate to 3%. Second, and more important for a cyclical, a backlog that grew $11.5 billion in a quarter cannot keep growing $11.5 billion a quarter. The moment backlog growth flattens, the market re-prices Caterpillar from a visibility story back toward a cycle story, and the cycle multiple for this company is in the teens.

Almost all of the expected return is earnings growth, and the multiple is fighting it. If the multiple simply HELD at 28.7x on FY2028E of $36.844 the price would be $1,058 (+20.7%); if it compressed to 20x, $737 (−15.9%). The spread between those outcomes is far wider than for any other name in this batch, which is what a 1.605 beta means in practice.
