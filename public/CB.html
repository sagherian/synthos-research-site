The Overview

Chubb sells insurance — to companies against fire, liability and cyber attack; to wealthy households against damage to homes and art; to farmers against crop failure; and, increasingly, life and savings products in Asia. It is Swiss-registered and operates almost everywhere.

There is one number that tells you whether an insurer is good, and it is called the combined ratio. It is the total of claims paid and costs incurred, divided by premiums collected. Below 100 means the company makes money on the insurance itself, before earning anything on the money it holds between collecting premiums and paying claims. Most insurers hover near 100 and rely on investment income.

Chubb's combined ratio last quarter was 83.8%. Stripping out storms and adjustments to old claims, the underlying figure was 82.2% — and it was 82.3% the year before. Flat, at a very good level, is the whole point: it means the discipline is repeatable rather than lucky.

The company also holds $88 billion of money set aside to pay future claims, which it invests for its own account until the claims come due. That is why a modestly profitable insurance operation can produce a 15% return on shareholders' money.

Chubb is buying back its own shares aggressively — $2.1 billion in six months, twice last year's pace — which shrank the share count 1.4% while equity still grew.

The shares cost $348.335, which is about 1.78 times the accounting value of the company's net assets, and about 12 times what analysts expect it to earn next year. Neither number is stretched for a business earning 15% on equity.

Two things stop this being a straightforward purchase. The first is that the upside is modest: our estimate is $370, about 6% above the price, and analysts on average say $356, which is less. The second is a governance observation rather than a financial one: in a single week in May, the company's president and chief operating officer sold 23,000 shares and gave away 62,352 more, reducing his personal holding by about 38%.

There is one more thing worth saying plainly. The automated data feed we use is badly wrong on this company. It thinks Chubb has one business segment when it has six. Its geographic data has not been updated since 2014. It reports the first quarter of 2026 as having made zero operating profit when the company actually earned $2.3 billion. Everything in this analysis comes from the company's own filings instead.

  • Downside Risk 4/10. The best score we assign in this batch: exceptional underwriting, modest leverage, low market correlation.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Revenue up 66% in five years and earnings nearly tripled, but analysts expect this year's earnings to be lower than last year's.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. Insurance compounds; it does not accelerate. That is not a criticism.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $370 against a current price of $348.33 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Read the bottom row and then the row above it, in that order.

The bottom row — 82.2%, 82.3%, 82.2%, 82.3% across four periods — is the quality signal. It is the combined ratio for business written in the current accident year with catastrophes stripped out: no weather, no reserve releases, no prior-year noise. Chubb has earned approximately 17.8 cents of underwriting profit on every premium dollar, four periods running, with a variance of one tenth of a point. For a company writing across six segments, fifty-plus countries and every major line of business, that consistency is the moat.

The row above it — catastrophe losses of 4.0 points in the quarter against 10.5 points in the first half of 2025 — is the noise. The 10-Q is explicit that the first-half improvement from 90.4% to 83.9% "reflects the impact of the California wildfire catastrophe losses in the prior year, including the unfavorable impact of the ceded reinstatement premiums on the expense ratio." Almost the entire headline improvement is the absence of last year's wildfires, not this year's performance. Anyone reading the headline combined ratio as a 6.5-point operational gain is misreading it.

Prior-period development, which is where insurers hide and where they are found out:

> "Pre-tax net favorable PPD for the six months ended June 30, 2026 was $742 million in our active companies, including net favorable development of $715 million in short-tail lines and net favorable development of $27 million in long-tail lines... Our corporate run-off portfolio had adverse development of $173 million, primarily driven by adverse development for molestation-related claims."

Three observations. First, $742 million favourable against $587 million in the prior-year half is a genuine positive and adds 2.5 points to the reported combined ratio. Second, 96% of it is short-tail — property, auto physical damage and surety — which is the honest kind, because short-tail reserves settle quickly and are hard to manipulate. Long-tail favourable development was only $27 million, with workers' compensation favourable and general casualty adverse. Third, the run-off portfolio's $173 million of adverse development on molestation-related claims is a genuine and growing liability, up from $83 million in the prior-year half. It is disclosed, it is reserved, and it is the kind of long-tail exposure that does not stop.

Gross unpaid losses and loss expenses were $88,018 million at the start of 2026, against $84,004 million a year earlier, with reinsurance recoverable of $18,346 million. Net unpaid losses of $69,672 million is the float — the money Chubb holds and invests until claims are paid — and it grew 5.1% year on year.
