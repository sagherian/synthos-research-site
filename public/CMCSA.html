The Overview

Comcast sells internet, mobile phone service and television to American households under the Xfinity name, and separately owns NBC, Sky in Britain, the Peacock streaming service, Universal's film studio and the theme parks.

It is taking itself apart. In January it spun off its cable television channels into a separate listed company called Versant. In June it said it would split the rest in two by the middle of 2027 — the media and theme-park businesses in one company, the broadband and mobile business in the other.

The reason is visible in the results. The internet business, which is the profitable core, is shrinking. Revenue from home broadband fell 5.5% last quarter. Comcast lost 167,000 broadband customers and 280,000 television customers in three months. Mobile carriers now sell home internet over their networks, and fibre companies are digging up the same streets, and Comcast's answer has been to cut prices and give new customers a free mobile line for a year — which keeps customers but reduces revenue.

The mobile business is the bright spot and it is genuinely bright: Comcast added 448,000 mobile lines last quarter, its best ever, and only 7% of its own broadband customers have bought one. There is a lot of room there.

The shares are down 23% over a year and about 60% from their peak. You now pay about seven times what analysts expect the company to earn next year, and collect a 5.3% dividend while you wait.

The thing to understand before anything else is the debt. Comcast owes $110 billion. The entire stock market values the company's shares at $88 billion. So the lenders have a bigger claim on this business than the owners do. That is not a crisis — the company throws off roughly $18 billion of spare cash a year and the debt is investment grade — but it means the share price moves about twice as much as the value of the underlying business, in both directions.

We think the shares are worth about $29 against $25. That is enough to keep on a list. It is not enough to buy a shrinking business with more debt than equity value while it is in the middle of splitting itself in two.

  • Downside Risk 7/10. Debt exceeds market value, on a revenue base that is contracting.
  • Growth Quality 3/10. Flat for four years; the largest line is falling 5.5%.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. Wireless convergence at 7% penetration is real. The rest is corporate engineering.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $29 against a current price of $24.93 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. Two separations in eighteen months, and the payload knows about neither

Versant — completed 2026-01-02

From the 10-Q filed 2026-07-23, verbatim:

> "On January 2, 2026 (the 'Versant Separation Date'), we completed the previously announced separation of Versant Media Group, Inc. ('Versant') into an independent publicly traded company comprised of select cable television networks and complementary digital platforms through a tax-free spin-off (the 'Versant Separation'). The Versant Separation did not meet the criteria to be presented as a discontinued operation, and Versant's results are included in our results of operations for the three and six months ended June 30, 2025."

That last sentence matters for every year-on-year comparison in this file: the 2025 periods contain Versant and the 2026 periods do not. The cash flow statement records cash transferred to Versant, net of $(750) million and the equity statement an $(8,107) million Versant Separation entry.

NBCUniversal and Sky — announced June 2026, targeted mid-2027

> "In June 2026, we announced our intention to separate into two independent publicly traded companies through a tax-free spin-off of NBCUniversal and Sky (the 'NBCUniversal Spin-off') comprised primarily of the Media, Studios and Theme Parks segments and the Sky businesses within our Residential Connectivity & Platforms and Business Services segments. The NBCUniversal Spin-off is expected to be completed in mid-2027, subject to the satisfaction of customary conditions, including obtaining final approval from our Board of Directors, receipt of tax opinions and regulatory approvals, and completion of financing arrangements. We expect to retain an ownership interest of up to [a stated percentage] in the NBCUniversal Spin-off entities for up to one year after the completion of the spin-off, which we intend to monetize in a tax-efficient manner over time. There can be no assurance that a separation transaction will occur... The condensed consolidated financial statements and related notes do not reflect the proposed NBCUniversal Spin-off."

On FY2025 segment revenue this separates approximately $48 billion of Media, Studios and Theme Parks from approximately $81 billion of connectivity — and it creates two capital structures where one now carries $110,443 million of debt. How that debt is allocated is the single most important undisclosed term in this file and it is named in the filing as a condition ("completion of financing arrangements").

And a segment realignment nobody outside the filings can see

> "Beginning in the first quarter of 2026, we updated the composition of our segments to align with the segment-level information that is regularly provided to our Co-Chief Executive Officers, who are the chief operating decision maker, including (1) adjusting the Media segment to exclude the historical results of Versant; (2) reclassifying the results of our regional sports networks to Corporate and other from the Media segment; (3) reclassifying the results of Xumo, our streaming platform joint venture with Charter Communications, to the Residential Connectivity & Platforms segment from Corporate and other; (4) reclassifying certain shared expenses into the related Media, Studios and Theme Parks segments...; and (5) adjusting the Media segment and Versant for the effects of the commercial services agreement."

Five separate reclassifications, none of them in the vendor payload, and a governance disclosure delivered in passing: the chief operating decision maker is now "our Co-Chief Executive Officers", while the vendor profile still names Brian L. Roberts alone. The archive contains no succession disclosure and this dive does not speculate about one; the fact is recorded because it is a material governance change stated in a filed document and absent from every commercial data field.

A further reclassification affects the most important revenue line in the company: "Beginning in the first quarter of 2026, commission revenue from the sale of certain direct to consumer ('DTC') streaming services is presented in domestic broadband revenue or video revenue based on whether a customer is entitled to receive the DTC streaming service through a broadband or video service offering... Previously, all of these amounts were in video revenue. Prior periods have been reclassified to reflect the current year presentation." So the −5.5% domestic broadband revenue figure is on a restated basis and is like-for-like; recorded so the reader knows the comparison was checked.

A 10% fall in the enterprise value is therefore roughly a 21% fall in the share price, and a 10% rise is roughly a 21% rise. That is why a company whose revenue has been flat for four years has a share price 59.6% below its multi-year peak, and it is the whole explanation for both the risk score and the width of the fair-value range below.

It is not a distress situation. It is a leverage situation, and the knowledge base's one surviving claim — nine years old and about somebody else — is precisely about that: outsider chief executives running cable at a disciplined leverage band for its tax shield. Single biggest risk: debt larger than equity, on a shrinking revenue base. Comcast owes $110,443 million and its shares are worth $88,468 million. The enterprise is roughly $190 billion and the equity is 47% of it, so a 10% move in the enterprise value is roughly a 21% move in the share price. Meanwhile the largest revenue line in the company — domestic broadband — fell 5.5% year on year, residential broadband customers fell 167,000 in the quarter, and the 10-Q attributes it to "an increasingly competitive environment" while disclosing that management is deliberately reducing revenue per customer to defend the base. The company generates enormous cash, the debt is investment grade, and the dividend is comfortably covered — none of that is in question. What is in question is whether an enterprise whose principal asset is losing customers to two classes of new entrant deserves 5x EBITDA, 6x, or 4x — and at 2.1x gearing, the difference between those three answers is the difference between $16 and $40.
