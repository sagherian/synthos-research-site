The Overview

CME Group runs the world's biggest marketplace for futures contracts — agreements to buy or sell interest rates, stock indexes, currencies, oil, gas, grain, gold and copper at a future date. It also runs the clearing house that stands behind every trade and guarantees that both sides get paid.

That second part is the business. Because CME both matches the trades and guarantees them, a position opened at CME has to be closed at CME. You cannot take it somewhere cheaper. That is why CME has kept roughly 90% of the US interest-rate futures market for decades, and why it earns 65 cents of operating profit on every dollar of revenue — a margin almost no other public company achieves.

It also has more cash than debt, the lowest share-price volatility of any company in this batch, and it pays out essentially all of its profits as dividends: $11.25 per share over the last year, a 4.28% yield.

So why are the shares 20% below their high and down 6% over the past year while the market rose 24%?

One reason. A new kind of contract called a perpetual future, which grew up in crypto trading, works differently. In a normal futures market the clearing house guarantees settlement, which means it must collect enough margin to cover losses. In a perpetual market, a losing trader can only lose the money they put up, and if things go badly the exchange automatically closes positions rather than chasing anyone for more. That lets those venues offer far more leverage than CME can.

Our research library contains both views, and they are recent. One commentator, in June, said the selloff was "way overdone" and that he wanted to buy CME because "it's the king." Another — with the highest skill rating in that part of our library — said in March that traditional guaranteed-settlement clearing simply "can't compete."

The evidence so far is genuinely mixed. Over the first six months of this year, the number of contracts CME traded per day rose 10%. But in the second quarter alone it fell 1%, with interest-rate contracts down 6% and energy down 13%. CME says that is because markets got calmer after a turbulent start to the year, not because it lost customers. That is plausible and it is not proven.

At $262.63 the shares cost about 20 times what analysts expect the company to earn in 2027. Our estimate of fair value is $297, about 13% above the price, and you collect a 4.28% dividend while you wait. Analysts on average say $304 — but five of the thirty-seven covering the stock rate it a sell, which is the most negative view of any company in this batch.

That is why our rating is a Buy — but a tactical one: a superb business at a discount, with one genuine unanswered question about its future.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Driver
"Long-run, CME owns the deepest liquidity pools in global interest-rate, equity-index and energy futures, and the physics of futures markets protect them: a contract opened at CME must be closed at CME, so liquidity does not migrate incrementally the way it can in fungible markets — it migrates all at once or not at all.
