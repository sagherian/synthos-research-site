The Overview

Costco charges people an annual fee — $65 in the United States, $130 if you want the Executive tier — for the right to shop in its warehouses. In exchange it sells them a deliberately small number of products, fewer than 4,000 per store, at a markup so thin that almost nobody can compete. The fees are close to pure profit. The merchandise is close to break-even by retail standards. That is the whole model, and it works extraordinarily well: 92.3% of American and Canadian members renew every year.

The company has 914 warehouses, about 285 of them outside the United States. It owns almost all of them — of the 189 sites it leases, 141 are land-only leases where Costco built and owns the building itself. It has more cash than debt. It gets paid at the register roughly the same day it pays its suppliers, so growth funds itself.

So why is the stock down over the past year while the market rose 24%?

Because the price got ahead of the business. At $947.85 you are paying about 48 times last year's earnings for a company expected to grow earnings around 10% a year. That is a price that requires everything to keep going right and the market to keep being willing to pay it.

And one specific thing is about to stop going right. In September 2024 Costco raised its annual membership fee. Because the fee is recognised gradually over each member's year, the increase kept boosting reported fee income for two years. The annual report says it produced about 40% of the growth in membership income last fiscal year. By the most recent quarter, that had dropped to 25%. At the next results announcement, on 24 September, it will be zero. Fee income growth will then have to come from new members and upgrades alone.

The most recent quarter already showed the seams. Sales grew 12%, which sounds excellent — but 2.2 percentage points of that was simply the price of petrol going up, and petrol makes almost no profit. Strip that and currency out, and the actual merchandise business grew about 7%, with its profit margin slightly lower than a year ago. Costco earned $4.93 a share when analysts expected $5.00 — the only miss in the recent run.

Two things are genuinely better than the published numbers suggest. First, the balance sheet: the most recent quarterly filing shows about $20 billion of cash and investments against roughly $6 billion of borrowings, far stronger than any screen reports. Second, the standard story that Costco makes nearly all its profit from membership fees is out of date — fees are now 51% of operating profit, down from 65% five years ago, because the merchandise business has genuinely improved.

Our estimate of fair value is $930 against a price of $947.85 — slightly below today's price. Every published analyst target, including the most pessimistic one at $1,000, is above the current price, which means even the market's bears are assuming the shares get more expensive relative to earnings. We are not assuming that.

This is a company worth owning at the right price. We do not think this is it. Watch it, and buy it if the de-rating finishes its work.

2. Membership — the engine, the stale statistic, and the tailwind that is running out

This is the section that decides the name, and none of it is visible in the vendor payload.

2a. How the fee works

"Our annual fee for these memberships is $65 in the U.S." Executive members pay "an additional annual fee of $65" and earn "a 2% reward on qualified purchases (generally up to a maximum reward of $1,250 per year)." The 10-K reports that Executive members numbered 38.7 million at FY2025-end (against 35.4 million and 32.3 million in FY2024 and FY2023) and that "the sales penetration of Executive members represented approximately 73.6% of worldwide net sales in 2025."

Renewal rates, from the filings, quarter by quarter:

Both filings give the same explanation for the drift: "Renewal rates were negatively impacted by a higher number of memberships sold online, including through digital promotions, entering the renewal rate calculation. These memberships renew at a slightly lower rate on average." That is a mix effect, not a deterioration, and we read it as such — but it is a mix effect that will not reverse, because the online channel is the growth channel.

Deferred membership fees — the forward book, and the vendor reports it as zero. The 10-K: "Deferred membership fees at the end of 2025 and 2024 were $2,854 and $2,501." That is +14.1%, and it is contracted fee income that will be recognised over the following twelve months. The vendor's balance sheet reports deferredRevenue = 0.00 in all three years. The filing wins; we use $2,854M (Section 8).

The counter-case, stated fairly, is that the company will raise the fee again. The 10-K explicitly lists "membership fee changes" among the matters its forward-looking statements address, and the last increase was effective 2024-09-01. A new increase is the single cleanest upside catalyst available and no consensus estimate in the file contains one. It is also entirely at management's discretion and therefore, under our house rule, an input rather than evidence. It is not in our base case; it is in our bull case, explicitly.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over 12-24 months decomposes as: EPS growth (+10.4% FY2027E, +10.1% FY2028E on consensus) + multiple drift + shareholder yield (~1.0% on a four-year average including specials; 0.62% on the current dividend run rate alone).

Our base case assumes multiple COMPRESSION, and we state the size of it precisely. Today the market pays 41.8x the fiscal year ending August 2027 — i.e. 41.8x the year-ahead estimate. Our base case of $930 pays 37.2x the fiscal year ending August 2028 — 37.2x the year-ahead estimate at that future date. On a like-for-like year-ahead basis that is roughly 11% of multiple compression over twelve to twenty-four months. The arithmetic reconciles exactly: +10.1% earnings growth − 11% multiple compression + 1.0% yield ≈ −1.9%, which is our base fair value against spot.

Four reasons the compression is our base rather than our bear. (a) The largest recent earnings tailwind expires on a known date — the fee increase laps at the 2026-09-24 print, and the filings document its decay from 40% to 35% to 25% of membership income growth. (b) Consensus itself models deceleration, from +12.8% (FY2026E) to +10.4% to +10.1% to +8.9% to +4.8% by fiscal 2030; a multiple in the forties is not usually sustained through a visible deceleration to single digits. (c) The compression has already started and the market is telling us so — down 13.4% from the closing high, below both moving averages, negative on every relative horizon while earnings grew double digits. (d) The justification most often offered for the multiple — that this is really a subscription business — is 24 points weaker than the version everyone quotes (Section 2b: fees are 51.3% of operating income, not ~75%).

Our knowledge base supplies dated third-party readings of the multiple, and they are the closest thing to a historical anchor we are permitted to use. One source recorded in January 2022 that "Costco re-rated from ~25x to over 40x earnings — stock outpaced the business itself." Another in August 2025 that "low-growth staples trade at Nvidia-like 35-42x multiples." Another in October 2025 quoted Costco at 50x and in December 2025 used it as the expensive benchmark against which technology looked cheap. The band the tracked sources record over the last four years is roughly 35x to 50x. Today's 47.7x trailing sits in the top quartile of that band; our base of 46.8x trailing / 37.2x forward sits in the middle of it.

  • U.S. and Canada renewal rate falling below 91.5%. It has run 92.3% / 92.1% / 92.2% across the last three disclosures; a break below 91.5% would be a change in kind, not a mix effect.
  • Core merchandise gross margin, measured on core merchandise sales, declining for three consecutive quarters. It fell nine basis points in the third quarter of fiscal 2026 after rising 16 basis points in fiscal 2025, and the 10-K warns that "higher tariffs are more likely to adversely impact rather than improve our results."
  • Shopping frequency growth falling below +2%, which would mean comparable sales are entirely price and mix.
  • The warehouse opening cadence falling below the mid-twenties, against 24 net new in fiscal 2025 and 29 planned for fiscal 2026 — square-footage growth is the only volume lever and it is already the slower part of the algorithm.
  • A leveraged acquisition or a change in the self-funding balance-sheet policy, which is one of the specific things the knowledge base identifies as constitutive of the business.

Pre-registered FLIP TO BUY criteria — what would make this a position, and at what price:

  • A price in the $850-870 area — the 52-week closing low is $850.00 — which is roughly 38x FY2027E and produces double-digit expected returns on unchanged consensus. This is the primary trigger and it requires nothing from the company.
  • An announced membership fee increase. The last was effective 2024-09-01; the 10-K explicitly flags "membership fee changes" as a matter within its forward-looking statements; no consensus estimate in the file contains one. On roughly $5.5B of fee revenue at very high incremental margin this is the largest available upside surprise, and it would justify buying at a materially higher price than $870.
  • A special dividend of the fiscal-2024 kind. Net cash is roughly $11.7B and rising $4-5B a year; the fiscal-2024 special cost $6,655M and is fully documented in the 10-K.
  • Membership fee growth holding at or above 10% at the 2026-09-24 print with the fee increase fully lapped, which would directly falsify the central bearish argument in this dive and would warrant an immediate upgrade.
  • A reclaim of both moving averages (currently $955.38 and $957.88) on above-average volume after the September print, which would signal the de-rating had run its course.

Single biggest risk: that a 47.7x multiple meets a 10% grower whose largest recent tailwind has just expired. Nothing needs to go wrong operationally. If consensus is met exactly and the market simply pays the middle of the band our tracked sources record for large-cap staples rather than the top of it, the stock is worth $930; if it pays the bottom, $770. The business risk in this name is close to nil and the valuation risk is the whole position.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that membership fee revenue keeps compounding at a double-digit rate after 2026-09-24. The filings document the fee increase's contribution decaying from ~40% to ~35% to ~25% of membership income growth, and the growth rate itself stepping from +14% to +11%. That line is 51.3% of operating income. If it settles at 7-8% rather than 11-14%, roughly two points come off operating income growth — and at 41.8x forward earnings, two points of growth is worth considerably more than two points of price.
