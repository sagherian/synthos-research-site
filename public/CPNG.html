The Overview

Coupang is Korea's Amazon, except it went further than Amazon did: instead of relying on outside delivery companies, it built and owns the whole chain — the warehouses, the trucks, the drivers. That is why it can promise next-morning delivery reliably, and why competitors that rent their logistics cannot match the experience. One of the investors we follow, who has sat on Coupang's board for fifteen years, called the delivery experience "jaw-dropping" and said in April 2025 he was still buying shares.

The business is big and growing: $34.5 billion of sales last year, up 14%. And an increasingly valuable part of it is not retail at all — it is letting other merchants sell on the platform and charging them fees and advertising. That piece grew 27% last year to $7.1 billion, nearly three times as fast as the retail piece, and it carries much higher margins. This is why gross profit margin has climbed from 25% to 29% in two years.

Here is the problem. The company made $208 million of profit in 2025. In the first quarter of 2026 it lost $266 million. Analysts now expect it to lose $491 million for the full year before recovering. Our data does not say why. Coupang reports its results in one lumped expense line and does not break out profit by segment — a criticism one of our sources made directly back in 2024 — so from these filings we cannot tell whether the losses come from the new businesses (food delivery, streaming, Taiwan, the Farfetch luxury site), from price competition in Korea, or from something else entirely.

The stock has fallen 51% from its high and now trades at less than one times sales, which is cheap for a business of this quality. But the second-quarter results come out today, and they are not in our data. Buying the day before you can see the number that matters is not investing, it is guessing.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Plenty of cash, but currently loss-making, with negative working capital and genuinely poor disclosure.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Revenue growth has halved in two years, but the mix is improving fast.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. A long compounding runway in one country, not an exponential.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $21 against a current price of $16.42 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

The single most important fact in this table: Third-Party Merchant Services has gone from 10.6% of revenue in FY23 to 20.6% in FY25, and it is compounding at more than double the rate of the retail line. Marketplace commissions and advertising carry structurally higher margins than buying and reselling goods, and this mix shift is exactly why gross margin expanded from 25.4% (FY23) to 29.2% (FY24) to 29.4% (FY25). That is 400 basis points of margin on a $34.5B revenue base — roughly $1.4B of incremental gross profit purely from mix.

And here is the disclosure problem. Coupang reports segments in its own filings as Product Commerce and Developing Offerings (per the company profile: Eats delivery, Play streaming, fintech, and the Farfetch luxury marketplace, plus operations across Taiwan, Singapore, Japan and elsewhere). This data file contains none of that split. There is no segment revenue, no segment operating income, no geographic breakdown whatsoever — seg_geo is completely empty. The income statement shows $0 R&D in every single year and one consolidated SG&A line of $9.67B for FY25.

Our own knowledge base called this exact problem eighteen months ago. business_breakdowns (2024-10-20, conviction 55, neutral): "Coupang runs ~25% gross margins with one lumped opex line; weak disclosure is a real criticism, though it hit EBIT/FCF profitability in 2023." The criticism has aged into the central obstacle to underwriting this name today. We cannot attribute the current losses to a segment because the data does not permit it.

Three deteriorations happening at once:

1. Revenue growth has halved — from +17.8% (Q3'25) to +10.9% to +7.5%. Note that Q4'25 revenue ($8,835M) was below Q3'25 ($9,267M) and Q1'26 fell again to $8,504M. Two consecutive sequential declines.

2. Gross margin has given back its gains — 31.3% (Q4'24) to 29.4% (Q3'25) to 27.0% (Q1'26), the lowest since Q1'24. Since the mix has been improving (third-party growing faster), a falling gross margin means the underlying unit economics are being pressured — most plausibly by price competition, promotional intensity, or the cost of a newly built network operating below capacity. The file cannot distinguish these.

3. Operating expenses have not flexed. SG&A ran $2,536M (Q4'25) and $2,539M (Q1'26) on revenue that fell from $8,835M to $8,504M. Flat costs against falling revenue produces exactly the -$242M operating loss observed.

FY25 in full: revenue $34.53B, operating income $473M (1.4% margin), net income $208M. Consensus FY26: net income -$491M, EPS -$0.286. That is a $700M year-over-year swing into loss on 7.7% revenue growth.

Precedent matters here, and it cuts both ways. Coupang has been through a far worse version of this: FY21 operating income was -$1.494B on $18.4B of revenue (-8.1% margin), and by FY23 it had reached +$473M (+1.9%). Management has demonstrated it can turn losses into profits when it chooses to. But FY23's 1.9% operating margin remains the all-time peak, on a business now 42% larger — Coupang has never demonstrated that this model produces more than about two cents of operating profit per dollar of revenue. Everything in the bull case depends on that ceiling being a choice rather than a limit, and this data cannot tell us which.

3. The balance sheet and the working-capital engine

FY25 year-end: cash $6,318M, no short-term investments, inventory $2,256M, total current assets $9,691M; total current liabilities $9,357M; short-term debt $960M, long-term debt $648M, total debt $4,635M (the gap between $1,608M of funded debt and $4,635M total is approximately $3.0B of capital-lease obligations against the $7,032M fulfilment property base); total assets $17,787M; equity $4,623M.

  • Our computed net cash: $6,318M − $4,635M = $1,683M net cash. The vendor's enterprise value of $28.57B implies net cash of only $905M; our EV is $27.79B and all EV multiples here use it. If you exclude capital leases and count only funded debt, net cash is $4,710M — a materially different picture. Both framings are defensible; we use the conservative one (leases as debt), because a fulfilment network's lease obligations are as fixed as its mortgage.
  • Current ratio 0.97 — working capital is negative ($9,691M current assets against $9,357M current liabilities gives +$334M at year end, but the trailing-twelve-month figure in the file is -$260M). This is by design, not distress.
  • Cash conversion cycle: -53.7 days. Inventory turns in 29.7 days, receivables collect in 3.6 days, and payables are stretched to 87.1 days. Coupang collects from customers seven weeks before it pays suppliers. This is the Amazon model and it is a genuine structural advantage — the business is financed by its own suppliers. It also means growth generates cash and contraction consumes it, which is the risk embedded in the current deceleration.
  • Leverage: total debt $4,635M against a TTM EBITDA that our own arithmetic puts at roughly $740M (Q2'25 $345M + Q3'25 $320M + Q4'25 $174M + Q1'26 -$99M). That is 6.3x gross debt to EBITDA, or net cash on a net basis. The EBITDA collapse is the story: $345M to $320M to $174M to -$99M across four quarters.

6a. What today's price assumes (the inversion)

At $16.42 — 0.79x TTM EV/sales and 22.0x FY28E consensus EPS — the price embeds roughly:

  • Revenue reaccelerates from the current +7.5% to +14.4% in FY27, adding $7.4B of revenue between the TTM $35.1B and the FY27E $42.6B. (Source: consensus, 12 analysts.) Falsifier: two more quarters below +10% YoY. Note that Q1'26 already printed +7.5% and revenue has now declined sequentially twice.
  • The company swings from a -$491M net loss (FY26E) to a +$730M net profit (FY27E) — a $1.22B turn in twelve months — and then to +$1.30B in FY28. (Consensus.) Falsifier: today's Q2 print showing an operating loss wider than Q1'26's -$242M.
  • Operating margin recovers to roughly 3% to support the FY28E EPS of $0.747 on $47.3B of revenue — above the all-time peak of 1.9% achieved in FY23, on a revenue base 94% larger. (Our arithmetic from consensus net income.) Falsifier: any full year in which operating margin fails to exceed 1.9%.
  • THE MOST FRAGILE ASSUMPTION: that the FY26 losses are investment rather than erosion. The 0.79x sales multiple already reflects deep scepticism — it prices Coupang roughly in line with a low-margin retailer, not a marketplace. But even that multiple requires the loss to be temporary. If the 27.0% Q1'26 gross margin is the new normal rather than a trough, the FY27-28 earnings path does not exist at any multiple. This is the assumption the entire equity rests on, and this file cannot test it.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return ≈ EPS growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield.

Because Coupang is currently loss-making, the base case cannot be built on earnings growth from a positive base — the return has to come from the market re-rating a sales multiple as profitability returns. Our base case of $21 is constructed two ways that converge:

1. Sales-multiple route: 0.95x EV/sales on FY27E revenue of $42.56B = $40.4B EV, plus $1.68B net cash = $42.1B equity value ÷ 1,855M diluted shares = $22.70.

2. Earnings route: FY28E EPS $0.747 × 26x = $19.42.

Averaging to ~$21. This explicitly assumes multiple EXPANSION from 0.79x to 0.95x EV/sales — roughly a 20% re-rating — and the driver named is straightforward: a retailer that returns to profit gets paid as a marketplace rather than as a reseller. That expansion is the fragile leg of the bridge. If the margin does not recover, the multiple does not expand, and the stock is worth what it is worth today. There is no dividend; buybacks were $243M in FY25 (0.8% of market capitalisation), a rounding error against a $491M loss. Shareholder yield contributes essentially nothing.

Note carefully: at an unchanged price the EV/sales multiple mechanically declines from 0.79x (TTM) to 0.59x (FY28E) simply because revenue grows. That rolldown is not a de-rating and should not be read as one.

8. Moat & competitive position

The moat is real and our panel is right about its nature. Coupang's advantage is owned end-to-end fulfilment in a dense, urbanised market — $7.03B of property, plant and equipment, 108,000 employees, and a delivery promise that third-party-dependent marketplaces cannot match on reliability. business_breakdowns states the mechanism precisely: rivals "undercut on price but lose on reliability and returns." Vertical integration in logistics is expensive to build, slow to replicate, and improves with density — every incremental order in an existing zone raises route efficiency. That is a genuine, compounding structural advantage.

Three qualifications, all supported by the data:

1. It is a single-country moat. Korea has roughly 52 million people. The knowledge base frames the runway against total retail penetration rather than e-commerce share — a real and long runway, but a bounded one. The file's seg_geo block is entirely empty, so we cannot size the international businesses at all.

2. Naver is a serious competitor (named by the panel as the primary rival, followed by SSG), and a search-and-commerce incumbent with its own network effects is a different class of opponent than a pure marketplace.

3. The moat has never produced more than a 1.9% operating margin. A moat that cannot be converted into durable profit is a moat around a low-return business. The single most important open question about Coupang is whether the ceiling is strategic choice or structural limit — and no data available to us answers it.

Capital allocation is the weaker flank. The Farfetch acquisition — roughly $500M for a ~$4B GMV luxury marketplace — was flagged by our own panel as strategically unclear at the time of purchase. The acquisitionsNet line shows $0 in FY25 and +$68M in FY24, so no further large deals appear in the file. Buybacks total $421M across FY24-25, meaningful but not decisive.

9. Data integrity — what we rejected and why

Vendor corruption checking is standard practice for these dives. For CPNG:

  • REJECTED: the entire consensus EBITDA and EBIT series. The est block reports negative EBIT in every forecast year — -$138.3M (FY26E), -$158.2M (FY27E), -$176.0M (FY28E), -$181.2M (FY29E) — while reporting positive net income of +$729.7M (FY27E) and +$1,301M (FY28E) in the same rows. Separately, the FY25 estimate row shows EBITDA of $307.1M against reported FY25 EBITDA of $1,200M — off by a factor of four. This series fails internal consistency and is excluded entirely. Only revenue, net income and EPS from est are used.
  • REJECTED: FY29E as a forecast. EPS of $0.622 sits below FY28E's $0.747 and revenue growth of +3.0% follows +11.2%, both driven by a shrinking analyst panel (14 EPS contributors for FY28E falling to 8 for FY29E). Treated as noise.
  • CORRECTED: enterprise value. The vendor reports $28.57B, implying $905M of net cash. From the FY25 balance sheet, cash of $6,318M less total debt of $4,635M gives $1,683M of net cash and an EV of $27.79B. All EV multiples here use our figure.
