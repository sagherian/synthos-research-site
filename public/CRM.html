The Overview

Salesforce sells software that companies use to keep track of their customers — who called, what they bought, what they complained about, which deals are close to closing. It is sold by the seat: you pay a monthly fee for each employee who logs in. About 95 pence in every pound of revenue works that way, on multi-year contracts, and the company currently has about $68 billion of contracts signed but not yet counted as revenue.

The whole argument about this company is one sentence long. If artificial-intelligence assistants do the work that people used to log in and do, companies need fewer seats, and Salesforce gets paid less. Salesforce's answer is that the assistants themselves have to use the software — and that it will charge for that usage, by the unit, on top of the seats. It has started building the machinery for this and given it a name in its accounts, but it has not yet told anyone how much money it makes from it.

So we look at whether the damage has started. It has not, visibly. Revenue in the most recent quarter was up 13%, though about half of that came from a company Salesforce bought and about two points from currency movements — the underlying rate is nearer 7%. Customers are leaving at the same rate as last year. The signed-but-unbilled contract base is still growing. That is not proof of safety, because these are multi-year contracts and damage would take time to show, but it is what the evidence says today.

What has changed dramatically is the balance sheet. In March 2026 Salesforce borrowed $25 billion and used all of it to buy back its own shares, at an average price of $198.34. The stock closed today at $190.99 — so those shares were bought for more than they are now worth. The company went from having modest debt to owing about $30 billion more than it holds in cash, in three months.

The stock has already fallen a long way: down about 24% over a year in which the market rose 24%, and 28% below its high. Because of that fall it is no longer expensive. You pay about 13.5 times next year's expected profits — roughly what an average company costs, for a business that still grows.

The oddity is the analysts. Of 97 ratings, 74 say buy, and the average price target is $265.75 — 39% above the price. Not one of them has a target below where the stock trades. The nearly identical company in this batch, Adobe, has a target just 1% above its price. We think the Salesforce targets simply have not been updated.

Our own estimate of fair value is $210, about 10% above the price. That is a real gain but not a large one, and the range around it is wide — $130 if the bears are right, $275 if the analysts are. Earnings arrive on 2 September, and they contain the one number that would tell you which. Waiting 29 days costs almost nothing.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Strong cash generation and a big contracted backlog, but $30B of fresh net debt, negative tangible book, and a terminal-value question the data cannot answer.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. Roughly 7% organic constant-currency, dressed as 13% by an acquisition and currency; margins genuinely up about 100bp a year.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. No path from seats. A real, unsized option in consumption pricing.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $210 against a current price of $190.99 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

State it plainly: on a weighted-average basis, the majority of fiscal 2026's repurchase spend offset compensation dilution rather than shrinking the company.

That changed in March 2026, and Section 5 deals with how.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over twelve months decomposes as non-GAAP EPS growth (+10.4%, consensus FY2027E $14.155 → FY2028E $15.627) + multiple drift (we assume a HOLD at ~13.4x, neither expansion nor compression) + shareholder yield (0.90% dividend only).

Our base of $210 assumes NO re-rating whatsoever. It is 13.4x FY2028E non-GAAP EPS against 13.5x FY2027E today — the same multiple, rolled forward one year. All of the expected return is earnings growth. We do not count the buyback as yield, because it is now financed with debt rather than free cash flow, and a repurchase funded by borrowing transfers value between capital providers rather than creating it.

We assume a hold rather than expansion deliberately, and it is the most consequential judgement in this dive. The street's $265.75 requires 17.0x FY2028E — a 26% multiple expansion from here — and nothing in the published target set explains why the multiple should expand on a business whose organic growth is decelerating. Our bull case of $275 is essentially the street's case, held at low probability. Our bear of $130 is ~8.3x FY2028E — compression to the "Ford multiple" the knowledge base names repeatedly — on a modestly missed number.

If you need multiple expansion for this to work, you are betting on sentiment reversing on the single most crowded negative narrative in software. We are not making that bet, which is why the base case is only +10%, and why +10% is not enough.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch.

The case for owning it.
