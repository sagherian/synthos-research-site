The Overview

Almost every company runs software on thousands of laptops, servers and cloud machines, and every one of those is a door an attacker can walk through. CrowdStrike puts one small piece of software — it calls it a sensor — on each of those machines. The sensor watches what is happening and reports back to CrowdStrike's own computers in the cloud, which compare what they are seeing across every customer at once. If an attack pattern shows up at one company, every other customer is protected against it almost immediately. That shared-learning effect is the heart of the business.

The clever commercial part is what comes next. Once that one sensor is installed and the data is flowing, CrowdStrike can sell you extra protections — for your cloud servers, for your employees' identities and passwords, for your web browsers, for the log files your systems produce — without installing anything else. Each extra product costs the company very little to deliver, so almost all of the extra money is profit. That is why the gross margin is about 75 cents on every revenue dollar.

Two years ago the company badly hurt itself. On 19 July 2024 it pushed out a faulty update that crashed a large number of Windows computers around the world, including airline systems. Customers stayed, but CrowdStrike had to hand out discounts, free modules, longer contracts and flexible payment terms to keep them. Sales growth slowed for about a year. It has now clearly recovered: sales grew 19.8% a year ago and 25.6% in the most recent quarter, and the profit margins have gone back up.

So why are we not buying it? Because of what it costs. The shares are $211.22, and the company has 1.02 billion of them, so the whole business is priced at $215 billion. Last year it took in $4.8 billion of revenue. That is about 42 times revenue. On the official accounting rules the company did not make a profit at all last year — it lost $163 million. It only looks profitable if you ignore the $1.1 billion of company shares it handed to its own employees as pay, which is 23 cents of every revenue dollar.

Analysts expect adjusted profits of about $1.23 per share next year and $1.56 the year after. At $211.22 you are paying 172 times next year's number and 136 times the year after's. And here is the thing that ought to stop a reader cold: the average of the 66 analysts who cover this company thinks the shares are worth $185.33 — twelve percent less than they cost today. The stock has gone up 80% in three months and closed today at the highest price it has ever reached in the data we hold.

Our own estimate of what the shares are worth is $167. They cost $211.22.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

CrowdStrike describes itself in the 10-K as having "reinvented cybersecurity for the cloud era" with "the AI-native CrowdStrike Falcon platform – the first, true cloud-native unified platform built with artificial intelligence at the core, capable of harnessing vast amounts of security and enterprise data to deliver highly modular solutions through a single lightweight sensor." Founded 2011; IPO 2019-06-12; headquartered in Austin, Texas. Chief executive and founder George R. Kurtz; president Michael Sentonas. 10,698 full-time employees at 2026-01-31 — a figure the vendor profile reports as exactly 10698, matching the filing to the person.

The share structure is now simple, and the filing settles it. The 10-K states: "On December 11, 2024, all of our outstanding shares of Class B common stock automatically converted into an equal number of shares of Class A common stock." There is one class of common stock, one vote per share. The 8-K of 2026-06-22 confirms it from the other direction: at the annual meeting record date of 2026-04-24 there were 254,536,521 shares of Class A common stock outstanding and entitled to vote, with no Class B mentioned. There is no dual-class overhang here, and that is worth saying because dual-class structures are the norm among 2019-vintage software listings.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+26.5%, from FY2027E $1.2309 to FY2028E $1.5567) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 135.7x to roughly 105x on the respective forward year, −23%) + shareholder yield (+0.33%, less roughly 2% of dilution) ≈ −1% to −5% before any change in estimates.

Our base case assumes multiple COMPRESSION, and we should be explicit that this is not a rolldown artefact. Today the market pays 135.7x on the second-forward fiscal year. Our base assumes it pays roughly 105x on the then-second-forward year, which is a genuine de-rating of about 23%, not the mechanical effect of the calendar advancing.

We assume it for three reasons, stated so they can be argued with. First, growth decelerates on the consensus path itself: revenue +23.5%, +21.8%, +21.8%, then +15.8%; EPS +32.6%, +26.5%, +29.0%, then +14.2%.
