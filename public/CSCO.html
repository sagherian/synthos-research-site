The Overview

Cisco makes the switches, routers and network gear that move data around inside companies and inside the enormous computer warehouses that run artificial intelligence. It has been doing this for forty years and for most of the last decade it was treated as a slow, boring, cheap company: reliable profits, a good dividend, not much growth.

That changed. The companies building AI datacentres need vastly more networking equipment than before, and Cisco is one of a handful of firms that can supply it. In the three months to April, Cisco's networking sales rose 25% — an extra $1.7 billion in one quarter — and total sales rose 12%. The share price has responded: up 81% in a year, against 24% for the US market as a whole.

Our own research library flagged this early. In March 2025 one of the analysts we track argued Cisco was too cheap at about sixteen times earnings and would be re-rated as the AI buildout arrived. He was right. The shares now trade at about twenty-eight times next year's expected earnings. That is not a small move — it is nearly a doubling of what investors are willing to pay for each dollar Cisco earns.

The question now is whether there is anything left. Three things in Cisco's own filings say to be careful. First, the growth is all in one place: networking. Security sales were flat last quarter, video-conferencing fell, and — most importantly — the service and support business, which is more than a quarter of the company and is supposed to be the reliable bit, actually shrank by 1%. Second, Cisco has committed a great deal of money to make this happen: the stock of unsold inventory is up 49% and the amount it has legally committed to buy from suppliers is up 111%, much of it locked-in, multi-year contracts for memory chips whose prices are currently very high. If demand slows, Cisco owns the problem. Third, the analysts who follow the company expect sales growth to fall from about 11% this year to under 1% by 2029.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $120. They cost $121.74. The average analyst says $126.44. When our number is below the price and below the crowd's, and the company reports results in eight days, the honest answer is to hold what you own and not add.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Financially sound — interest covered nearly ten times, huge cash generation — but a stretched working-capital position, negative working capital of nearly $3 billion, and a share price near its high.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Real and verified, but confined to one product line while the recurring service base shrinks.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. A supplier to an exponential, not one itself, selling to customers who can and do design their own alternatives.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $120 against a current price of $121.74 — a premium price for a business we still like.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

Cisco describes itself in the 10-Q as designing and selling "a broad range of technologies that help to power, secure, and draw insights from the Internet." Chief executive Charles H. Robbins; approximately 86,200 employees as of 2025-07-26 per the 10-K, of whom approximately 25,600 are in worldwide sales and marketing. Listed on NASDAQ; IPO February 1990; fiscal year ends the last Saturday of July, so "FY2025" means the twelve months to 2025-07-26 and "Q3 FY2026" means the quarter to 2026-04-25.

The reportable segments are geographic, and the vendor mislabels one of them

The 10-K, Note 19, is unambiguous: Cisco is "primarily managed on a geographic basis consisting of three segments: the Americas, EMEA, and APJC." Performance is measured on segment revenue and segment gross margin only — research and development, sales and marketing, and general and administrative expenses are not allocated to segments.

The vendor's seg_geo block supplies exactly three lines and they sum exactly to revenue — $33.656B + $14.824B + $8.174B = $56.654B for FY2025, matching reported revenue to the dollar. But the largest line is labelled "UNITED STATES", and it is not the United States. The 10-K states plainly:

> "Revenue in the United States was $30.4 billion, $28.7 billion, and $29.9 billion for fiscal 2025, 2024, and 2023, respectively."

The vendor reports $33.656B under "UNITED STATES"; the filing shows US revenue of $30.4B; the $33.656B figure is the AMERICAS segment. We use the filing. The gap is $3.3 billion, or 10.9% of the line. The corroboration is in the 10-Q's own commentary, which breaks Americas product revenue growth into "the United States, Canada and Mexico by 22%, 9% and 32%, respectively, partially offset by a decline in Brazil of 10%" — four countries inside one segment that the vendor calls a country.

The Q1 FY2025 tax benefit — a one-off that inflates every FY2025 comparison

The vendor's own quarterly data contains the tell and it is worth stating explicitly. In the quarter ended 2024-10-26, Cisco reported pre-tax income of $2.267B and net income of $2.711B — net income exceeded pre-tax income, because the income tax line was a benefit of $444M. The 10-Q corroborates and sizes it: nine-month FY2026 EPS growth of 24% was "partially offset by the income tax benefit of $720 million we had in the first nine months of fiscal 2025."

Consequence: FY2025 GAAP net income of $10.180B and the FY2025 effective tax rate of 8.3% ($920M on $11.100B of pre-tax income) are not clean operating results. Stripping the $720M benefit gives roughly $9.46B of clean FY2025 net income and about $2.37 of clean GAAP diluted EPS against the $2.55 reported — a 7% overstatement. We flag this rather than smooth it, and we note that our trailing-twelve-month window (Q4 FY2025 through Q3 FY2026) does NOT contain the benefit, so the trailing EPS of $3.02 and the 40.3x trailing multiple used throughout this dive are clean. It is the year-on-year comparisons against FY2025 that are flattered.

3. Gross margin, memory, and the working-capital commitment

Product gross margin fell 2.5 percentage points year on year in the April quarter and 1.0 point over nine months. The 10-Q's own attribution table names three drivers — product mix, pricing and productivity — and defines productivity to include "overall manufacturing-related costs, such as component costs (including memory)". The commentary is direct:

> "Product gross margin decreased by 2.5 percentage points primarily driven by negative impacts from product mix and to a lesser extent, pricing, partially offset by productivity improvements. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+12.0%, FY2026E $4.276 to FY2027E $4.787) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 25.4x to roughly 23x on the respective forward year, −9%) + shareholder yield (+2.6%) ≈ +5% to +6%, against a base fair value that is 1.4% below spot.

Our base case assumes multiple compression, and the reason is in the consensus estimates themselves, not in a view of our own. The forward EPS growth path is +12.9%, +12.0%, +10.1%, then +1.7%. The revenue path is +11.0%, +9.3%, +6.8%, then +0.4%. A multiple appropriate to 12% growth is not appropriate to 2% growth, and the market normally begins discounting that a year ahead of the deceleration. By FY2028 the market is looking at FY2029, and FY2029 consensus is a flat year.

To be explicit about the guardrail: 28.5x FY2026 becoming 25.4x FY2027 at a constant price is mechanical rolldown, not compression. The compression we assume is a genuine change in what the market pays at each date — from 25.4x forward today to roughly 23x forward in twelve months — and we assume it because the growth being purchased is visibly shorter-dated than it was.

Almost none of the expected return is re-rating, and that is a change. For the last eighteen months, re-rating was the whole return: the stock rose 81.4% while EPS rose roughly 12%. The re-rating leg is now spent. What is left is 12% EPS growth against 9% assumed compression and 2.6% of yield. If instead the multiple simply HELD at 25.4x on FY2028E of $5.272, the price would be $134 (+10.1%); if it compressed to 20x, $105 (−13.7%).

This is a name where our research process worked and the correct response to that is discipline, not a victory lap.
