The Overview

Carvana buys used cars, fixes them up, photographs them, and sells them online. You never go to a dealership. It is now the second-largest used-car retailer in America and by far the fastest-growing.

Last quarter it sold 197,325 cars — up 38% from a year earlier, in a market that was shrinking. Revenue was up 52%. It made $513 million of profit. Those are exceptional numbers and there is no way to make them look bad.

Here is the problem. It made less money per car than it did a year ago. Gross profit per car fell from $7,426 to $7,014. And after all costs, the profit margin fell from 12.4% to 10.4%. So the company is selling many more cars at a slightly worse price. Growing volume while margin shrinks is fine if the margin decline is temporary and the volume is permanent. It is a problem if it is the other way round.

Management's own forecast makes this uncomfortable. They expect $2.7 to $3.0 billion of adjusted profit this year. Divided by the revenue analysts expect, that is a margin of 9.4% to 10.4% — lower than last year's 11.0%, in a year when revenue grows more than 40%.

There is a second thing worth understanding, and it is the most important fact about this company. Carvana does not mainly make money selling cars. It makes money selling the loans. When you buy a car from Carvana, Carvana lends you the money and then sells that loan on. Last quarter, that activity produced $526 million of profit out of $680 million of total operating profit — about three-quarters. One commentator in our knowledge base said exactly this, and the filings confirm it. It matters because loan profits depend on interest rates, and management says rising rates are why that line went down last quarter.

Against all of that sits the genuinely exciting part. Carvana has only 2% of the used-car market. And it has already built and paid for the factories to handle roughly twice the cars it currently sells, with land for four times. It spent only $147 million on new property last year on $20 billion of sales. If it simply fills the capacity it already owns, profit rises a lot without much new spending.

The shares cost $68.02. Analysts expect $2.20 of earnings in 2027, so you are paying about 31 times. Our estimate of fair value is $68 — the same as the price. The average analyst says $85.45, but the most pessimistic says $67, which is below today's price, and the vendor's own summary of all 46 analysts is "Hold", not "Buy".

We are not seeing anything here the market is missing. That is what Hold means.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

Read those two tables together and the quarter resolves cleanly. Carvana sold 38% more cars at prices roughly 10.7% higher, which is why revenue grew 52%. Gross profit per car fell $412 and fixed cost per car fell $278, so contribution per car fell $134. Because the denominator — revenue per car — rose $3,601 at the same time, the margin percentage fell twice as fast as the economics did: 200 basis points of adjusted EBITDA margin, of which roughly a third is genuine contribution loss and two thirds is arithmetic from higher average selling prices.

That distinction matters and we make it explicitly because it cuts both ways. A margin percentage falling because average selling prices rose is not a deterioration; a $298 fall in adjusted EBITDA per unit is. The per-unit measure is the honest one and it is down 7.1%.

Management's explanation, quoted in full because it is the causal claim the whole file rests on: "GAAP and Non-GAAP Total GPU were in line with our expectations in aggregate, with a shift in allocation between (i) Retail GPU, which was higher due to increasing industry retail prices following recent FTC guidance requiring automotive dealers to include mandatory dealer fees in advertised prices (note: Carvana doesn't charge these fees), and (ii) Other GPU, which was lower due to increasing benchmark rates. With both industry retail prices and benchmark rates drifting higher in the quarter, we followed the market on retail pricing but kept customer-facing interest rates stable."

Half-weighted as management framing — and note what it concedes. Carvana chose not to pass rising benchmark rates through to customer interest rates, and absorbed the difference in Other GPU. That is a demand-protecting decision, it is defensible, and it means the Other GPU compression is a policy choice with a volume benefit rather than an involuntary squeeze — which also means it can persist as long as the company keeps choosing volume.

Other gross profit is principally the gain on sale of the auto loans Carvana originates, plus ancillary products. In the June quarter it was $526M against $680M of operating income — 77.4%. For FY2025 it was $1,733M against $1,881M of operating income — 92.1%.

The knowledge base names this correctly and it is the most useful thing in the lane. A claim dated 2025-11-25 states that "80% of profit comes from finance/interest, not car buying/selling." The filings put it at 77.4% for the most recent quarter and 92.1% for the most recent full year. That is a knowledge-base claim that is verifiable against a primary document and turns out to be right, which does not happen often enough to pass over.

What it means for risk. Carvana's operating income is, to a first approximation, a spread business layered on a retail business. The spread is set by the gap between the interest rate Carvana charges customers and the rate at which it can sell the resulting loans. Management has told us it is currently choosing to compress that spread to protect volume. A 15% decline in Other GPU from $2,666 to about $2,266, on an annualised 790,000 units, is roughly $316M of annual gross profit and, given the fixed cost base, close to the same amount of operating income — approximately 12% of the guided full-year adjusted EBITDA. That sensitivity, not used-car demand, is the risk that determines this equity.

3. The capacity that is already paid for

The bull case for Carvana is not the growth rate. It is that the growth needs almost no capital.

From the second-quarter shareholder letter: "In the quarter, we integrated retail production capabilities at three additional ADESA locations, bringing our total integrated sites to 19. We also began construction of our first full buildout at an ADESA location, which we expect will begin producing vehicles in early 2027. Our current footprint gives us fully built out annual capacity for approximately 1.5 million retail units, with real estate to support annual retail production of 3 million retail units."

Against a June-quarter run rate of 197,325 units — approximately 789,300 annualised — that is roughly 53% utilisation of built-out capacity and 26% of the land.

The capital numbers corroborate it. FY2025 purchases of property and equipment were $147 million on $20,322 million of revenue — 0.72% — against depreciation and amortisation of $275 million. Property and equipment, net moved from $2,814M at 2025-12-31 to $2,859M at 2026-06-30, a $45M increase, in six months during which retail units grew 38%. First-half 2026 capital expenditure was $102M.

And the operating leverage is already visible in the one line where it should appear first: SG&A per retail unit fell from $3,846 to $3,568 year on year, and on a non-GAAP basis from $3,385 to $3,228.

The stated destination, half-weighted as management framing: "We remain firmly on the path to selling 3 million cars per year and to achieving 13.5% Adjusted EBITDA margin by 2030 to 2035." That is a nine-year window with a four-year spread in the target date, which is a way of saying the company does not know when — and it is being stated in the same quarter adjusted EBITDA margin fell to 10.4%.

The tax receivable agreement is the largest thing in this file that appears in no vendor field. At $2,130M it is 41% the size of total debt, $1,645M of it is owed to related parties — the pre-IPO LLC unitholders, principally the Garcia family — and it is a contractual cash obligation that ranks ahead of common shareholders. It is being paid down: $37M of tax receivable agreement payments in the first half of 2026, against $17M in the first half of 2025. At that pace it is a very long-dated liability, and any acceleration in Carvana's use of its deferred tax assets increases it.

The deferred tax asset is $2,968M — 20.4% of total assets. It arose from the FY2025 release of a valuation allowance, which is what produced the $2,785M income tax benefit in the FY2025 income statement. A company whose largest single asset after inventory and property is a deferred tax asset is a company whose book value is contingent on continuing to earn taxable income.

Cash flow is a poor lens for this business and we say so rather than quoting a misleading yield. First-half 2026 net cash provided by operating activities was $345M against net income of $918M, because operating cash flow absorbs both the $833M increase in vehicle inventory and the gross flows of the lending business — $9,005M of finance receivable originations against $9,323M of proceeds from sale in six months. Trailing-twelve-month operating cash flow is $1,120M and trailing capital expenditure is $191M, giving free cash flow of $929M — a 1.25% yield on the vendor's market capitalisation, which reconciles exactly to freeCashFlowYieldTTM of 1.245%. The vendor's arithmetic is correct. The measure is simply not informative for a company whose operating cash flow is dominated by inventory growth and loan warehousing, and we do not use it in the valuation.

5. Valuation — priced in or room?

Two share counts, and the difference is the first thing to settle. The vendor reports a market capitalisation of $74,606,762,069, which at $68.02 implies 1,096,835,667 shares. The 10-Q reports 719,916,415 Class A and 380,547,355 Class B shares outstanding at 2026-07-27 — 1,100,463,770 combined, within 0.33%. But the company states directly in the earnings release: "Assuming full conversion of LLC units and other dilutive effects, there would have been 1.127 billion shares of Class A common stock outstanding." On the company's own fully-converted count the market capitalisation is $76,658M, 2.7% above the vendor's.

Reported EPS is on a different base again. Diluted Class A EPS of $0.42 is $310M of income attributable to Carvana Co. divided by 739,960 thousand diluted Class A shares — it excludes both the 380,647 thousand Class A Units and the 6,487 thousand Class B Units held by the non-controlling interest, which the filing lists as anti-dilutive. So reported EPS is struck on roughly 66% of the fully-converted share count while capturing only 60.4% of consolidated net income, because Carvana Co. bears corporate tax that the pass-through units do not. On an as-converted basis, EPS is approximately 7-10% below the reported figure. Consensus is on the reported basis and so are the multiples below, but the overhang is real and stated.

Reconditioning capacity already built for approximately 1.5 million units against a run rate near 790,000, real estate for 3 million, and FY2025 capital expenditure of $147 million on $20.3 billion of revenue. SG&A per retail unit down $278 year on year. This is one of the better operating records in this programme and none of the caution below is a criticism of the execution.

What stops it being a Buy, in three specific numbers. First: adjusted EBITDA per retail unit fell $298 to $3,897 — the leverage curve went backwards in the quarter volume grew 38%. Second: management's own FY2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance of $2.7-3.0bn implies a 9.4-10.4% margin against the 11.0% Carvana delivered in 2025 — the company is guiding margin down in a year revenue grows 42%, and every forward estimate in this file requires the opposite. Third: 77.4% of operating income is Other gross profit — the gain on sale of originated auto loans — and management says it is compressing on benchmark rates. The equity is a leveraged bet on a lending spread wearing a retailer's clothes, the knowledge base's one useful claim says exactly that, and the sell side does not express its targets in those terms.

What stops it being an Avoid. The volume growth is real and industry-beating; the capacity is paid for and quantified; net debt of $2.6bn against $2.7-3.0bn of guided adjusted EBITDA is not a solvency question; and the stock has already underperformed the index by 32 points over twelve months, so the margin disappointment is at least partly in the price.
