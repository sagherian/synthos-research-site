The Overview

CVS is four businesses in one company, and understanding them separately is the only way to understand the stock.

The first is Aetna, a health insurance company. It collects premiums and pays medical bills. If the bills come in higher than expected, it loses money quickly — and that is exactly what happened in 2024 and 2025.

The second is Caremark, a pharmacy benefit manager. It sits between drug makers, insurers and pharmacies, negotiating prices and deciding which drugs are covered. It is a profitable and politically unpopular business.

The third is the retail pharmacies — the CVS stores.

The fourth is care delivery: clinics and home-health services CVS bought to complete the picture. In September 2025 the company wrote off $5.7 billion of the value of those purchases, admitting it had paid too much.

Put together, CVS collected $402 billion last year — more revenue than any other company in this research batch by a factor of five — and reported profits of $1.40 per share, down from $6.49 two years earlier.

Now the turn. There is one number that governs Aetna's profitability: the share of premium income spent on medical care, called the medical benefit ratio. In the first quarter of 2026 it was 84.6%, against 87.3% a year earlier. That 2.7-point improvement is worth roughly $4 billion a year, and it is why management raised its profit forecast in May and why the shares have risen 67% in twelve months.

The shares cost $104.42. Analysts expect adjusted earnings of about $8.44 per share in 2027, so you are paying about 12.4 times. That is not expensive.

Three things stop this being simple. First, the company owes about $85 billion net, and its profits cover its interest bill only 1.9 times — thin. Second, it still carries $85 billion of goodwill on its books from past acquisitions, and it has just demonstrated it will write some off. Third, the company reports results tomorrow morning.

Our estimate of fair value is $114, about 9% above the price. The average analyst says $109.

One last thing worth reporting because it is unusual. Our knowledge base search for this company returned twenty-five results, of which nine turned out to be about shining infrared light into people's brains to treat dementia. The abbreviation PBM means "pharmacy benefit manager" in finance and "photobiomodulation" in neuroscience, and a search that does not know the difference will tell you all sorts of things about CVS Health that have nothing to do with it.

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced.
