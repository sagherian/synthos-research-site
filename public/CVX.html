The Overview

Chevron pumps oil and gas out of the ground, ships it, refines it into petrol and diesel, and sells it. Almost all of its profit depends on one number it does not control: the world price of crude oil.

In the three months to June 2026 that price was very high — Brent crude averaged $104 a barrel, against $68 in the same three months a year earlier. The reason is a conflict in the Middle East that has disrupted supply. Chevron therefore had the best quarter it has ever had: it made $12.1 billion in three months, which is more than it made in the whole of 2025. It also produced more oil and gas than ever before, mostly because it bought a company called Hess, and it ran its American refineries harder than ever before.

Then it used the money sensibly. It paid down $8.4 billion of debt in three months — the most it has ever repaid in a quarter — so it now owes about $28.5 billion net against cash earnings running at roughly $45 billion a year. It raised the dividend for the 38th consecutive year, to $1.78 a quarter, which is $7.12 a year on a $190 share, or 3.7%. And it is still buying back its own shares, with $36.5 billion of authorisation left.

So why has the share price gone nowhere? Because everyone can see that $104 oil is a wartime price. Analysts expect Chevron to earn about $14.75 a share this year and then about $12.60 next year — less, not more. The market is pricing the shares at roughly 15 times that lower number, which is a normal price for an oil company. It is not treating this year as the new normal, and neither should you.

There is one genuinely new thing. In the same quarter, Chevron agreed to build a power plant in West Texas and sell its electricity directly to a Microsoft data centre for the next 20 years — about 2.67 gigawatts, which is a very large amount. Artificial-intelligence data centres need enormous quantities of electricity and there are not many organisations that can build power stations at that scale on a commercial schedule. If Chevron signs more of these, it becomes partly a power company, and power companies are valued differently from oil companies. That is not in anybody's forecast yet.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $195. They cost $190.375. That is a 2.4% gap, and the average analyst says $211, which is higher than we do. When our number sits essentially on top of the price, the right answer is to hold what you own — collecting a 3.7% dividend and a buyback worth roughly another 3% — rather than to buy more at a moment when profits are being flattered by a war.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Very strong finances and a low-volatility share (beta 0.49), attached to a profit line that is entirely at the mercy of the oil price — and that oil price is currently abnormal.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Production up 20% and costs down $3 billion, both real and both verified in the filings — but revenue is expected to fall, not rise, over the next two years.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. One genuinely interesting option — the Microsoft power contract — bolted to a business that by its nature cannot compound.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $195 against a current price of $207.92 — a premium price for a business we still like.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

Chevron describes itself as an integrated energy company operating through two reportable segments, Upstream (exploration, development, production, transport, liquefied natural gas, pipelines and a gas-to-liquids facility) and Downstream (refining, marketing, transport, chemicals and additives), with a residual All Other covering cash management and debt financing, corporate functions, insurance, real estate and technology. Chief executive Michael K. Wirth; approximately 43,039 employees; incorporated 1926, NYSE-listed since 1921; CIK 0000093410.

The disclosure structure matters more than usual here, because the vendor mishandles it. Chevron reports segment revenue in a four-column grid — U.S. Upstream, International Upstream, U.S. Downstream, International Downstream — on two bases: "before elimination" and after a large intersegment revenue elimination. For FY2025 the eliminations total $46.938B, a quarter of reported revenue. Read the wrong column and the numbers are meaningless.

Three things about this table are worth pausing on.

First, the special items are trivial, so the quarter is real as reported. The reconciliation shows total special items of +$144M after tax (an international downstream asset sale gain of $230M and a U.S. upstream asset sale gain of $115M, against pension settlement and curtailment costs of −$86M and a −$130M legal reserve) plus foreign currency effects of −$49M. There is no one-off gain propping this up — if anything the adjusted figure is the more conservative one. We checked specifically for it and it is not there.

Second, $1.4B of the improvement is disclosed as favourable timing effects, which the company defines as mark-to-market on financial derivatives ahead of physical delivery plus LIFO inventory effects. That is roughly 12% of the quarter's earnings and it is genuinely non-recurring in direction — timing effects reverse. We treat the underlying operating result as nearer $10.6B than $12.1B.

Operating cash flow of $22.6B against $4.5B of capex produced $18.1B of free cash flow in three months. Total debt fell a record $8.4B, from roughly $45.5B to $37.075B. Net debt fell from $34.461B at 31 December to $28.545B. Debt-to-CFFO improved to 0.8x and net-debt-to-CFFO to 0.6x. A company at a cyclical peak that uses the peak to retire debt rather than to chase volume is behaving correctly, and it is a genuine mark in favour of management quality.

3. Growth, and the parts of it that are not the oil price

Production is the genuinely durable improvement. Second-quarter net oil-equivalent production of 4,070 MBOED was 674,000 barrels per day above the year-ago quarter, +20%, split $382,000 U.S. and $292,000 international. U.S. production of 2,077 MBOED was a company record. The release attributes the increase to the Hess acquisition plus growth in the Permian Basin and the Gulf of America, partly offset by curtailments in the Saudi-Kuwait Partitioned Zone caused by the Middle East conflict.

Cost reduction is the second durable improvement, and both targets were beaten early:

> "During the second quarter, the company achieved its structural cost reduction target six months early by capturing $3 billion in annual run-rate savings. Furthermore, we delivered $1.5 billion of annual run-rate synergies related to the Hess Corporation acquisition within one year of closing." — Michael Wirth, 2026-07-31

We half-weight that as management framing, per house rule. But the underlying disclosures are checkable and specific: the Hess synergy figure is described as exceeding the initial target by 50%, and the structural cost programme is stated as $3-4B by the end of 2026 against 2024 levels, with $3B already banked. Both are attributable to named, dated, quantified disclosure rather than adjectives.

Refining ran at records. U.S. refinery crude unit inputs of 1.07 million barrels per day, up 2%, at more than 97% crude unit utilisation. International inputs fell 10% to 598 MBD, which the release attributes directly to Middle East supply disruptions — the same conflict that produced the oil price took away a tenth of the international refining throughput, which is the clearest statement available that the war is not an unambiguous positive for this company.

Portfolio activity, all disclosed in the same release:

  • Completed sale of the Hong Kong downstream fuels and lubricants businesses.
  • Agreed to sell the 50% interest in Singapore Refining Company plus downstream assets in Singapore, Vietnam, Australia, Indonesia, the Philippines and Malaysia — expected to close in 2027.
  • Signed heads of agreement with the Government of Iraq on potential participation in West Qurna 2, Nasiriyah and an export pipeline.
  • Signed the 20-year, ~2.67 GW behind-the-meter power purchase agreement with Microsoft for a West Texas data centre.

The Asian downstream divestitures are a revenue subtraction consensus may not have fully modelled, and they are one plausible explanation for why the street's FY2027 and FY2028 revenue estimates ($202.275B, $199.978B) sit below the FY2026 figure by more than the crude price alone would explain.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS change (−14.6%, from FY2026E $14.752 to FY2027E $12.601) + multiple drift (EXPANSION, from 12.9x to roughly 15.5x on the respective forward year, +20%) + shareholder yield (+6.7%) ≈ +9% to +11%.

That decomposition is deliberately unusual and it is the honest description of what owning this stock is. Almost none of the expected return is earnings growth — consensus has earnings falling. The return is the dividend plus the buyback plus a multiple that expands as reported earnings decline, which is exactly what happens to cyclical equities on the way down from a peak: the P/E goes up while the price goes sideways. Our base fair value of $195 assumes multiple expansion to 15.5x FY2027E, and the justification is not optimism — it is that 12.9x on a peak year and 15.1x on a normalised year are the same statement about value, and the market will settle on the normalised one.

Integrated majors earn their highest reported profits at exactly the moment their forward earnings are most at risk, and the market knows it, which is why the stock is flat over three months into a record print. The 6.7% cash yield is real, the balance sheet is genuinely repaired, and a holder is being paid properly to wait. A buyer is paying a normal multiple for peak-cycle earnings with no discount for the cycle. That is a Hold, and it would become something else on price rather than on news.
