The Overview

DoorDash delivers restaurant meals and, increasingly, groceries. It does not own restaurants or vehicles; it runs the app that connects a hungry person, a merchant and a driver, and takes a fee from all three. Last October it bought Deliveroo, the British equivalent, so it now runs three of these marketplaces across more than thirty countries.

It is growing very fast. Revenue rose 33% in the most recent quarter — more than twice the rate of any other company in this research batch — and it has grown every year for at least six.

It has also, finally, started making money. Until 2024 it lost money every year. In 2025 it made an operating profit of $723 million and a net profit of $935 million.

So why is the share price down 18% over the past year while the market rose 24%?

Two reasons.

The first is arithmetic. At $202 you pay about 80 times what analysts expect the company to earn this year. For that to make sense, profit has to rise very fast — analysts expect it to grow 77% in 2027 and 59% in 2028, far faster than revenue. That requires the business to become much more profitable per order, and the most recent quarter went the wrong way: revenue grew 33% while operating profit actually fell slightly.

The second is that the profit is not quite as real as it looks. DoorDash paid its staff $1,051 million in shares last year — more than its entire reported net profit — and it paid almost no tax, at an effective rate of 0.6%. Both of those will normalise.

There is a genuine long-term idea underneath, and it is the most interesting one in this batch. Independent analysis in our knowledge base found that on a typical $31 order DoorDash is "lucky to make $1" — because most of the money goes to the person driving the car. DoorDash executives describe an in-house delivery robot, 300 pounds, 20 miles per hour, driving itself, that has been operating in Phoenix for two years. If machines eventually do the driving, the company that owns the customers and the routes owns the savings. That is worth a great deal. It is also, at this stage, something we can only see through management's own description, and an independent analyst said in 2023 that the robotics programme had already been scaled back.

We think fair value is about $225 against $202.

The company reports tomorrow, and it has come in below expectations by about 19% in two of the last four quarters. That is not a day to act.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.
