The Overview

Datadog makes the dashboards that tell companies whether their software is working. If a website slows down, a database fills up, or an application starts throwing errors, Datadog is what the engineers are staring at.

It is a very good business. Revenue grew 32% in the most recent quarter — faster than the 28% it grew last year, which is unusual and encouraging. Of every dollar of revenue, 80 cents is gross profit. It generated $1 billion of spare cash last year while spending only $50 million on equipment, and it has about $2.9 billion more cash than debt.

So why "Avoid"?

Because of what you have to pay, and when.

The shares closed at $288.15 — which is, to the cent, the highest price they have traded at in the past year. They are up 96% in three months. At that price you are paying about 101 times what analysts think the company will earn in 2027, and about 749 times what it actually earned last year under standard accounting rules.

The gap between those two numbers is worth understanding. Datadog pays its staff heavily in shares — $751 million last year, or 21% of all revenue. The "adjusted" profit figure everyone quotes pretends that cost does not exist. Under normal accounting, Datadog's core operations lost $44 million last year.

There is one more thing. Forty analysts cover the company and forty of them are positive — and their average price target is $265, which is 8% below where the stock now trades. The stock has run past the people who like it.

And the company reports its next quarterly results in two days. Buying a stock at its all-time high, at 119 times earnings, forty-eight hours before it tells you how the quarter went, is not investing.

Our estimate of fair value is $250 — about 13% below the current price.

None of this says the business is bad. It says the price is wrong today. The section at the end names what would change our mind and at what level.

  • Downside Risk 8/10. Highest here. All of it price, accounting composition and timing.
  • Growth Quality 9/10. Accelerating to 32% with 80% gross margins and real cash generation.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. Consumption-priced monitoring in a world of multiplying software. A genuine case.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $250 against a current price of $288.15 — consistent with our call to stay away or wait for a better setup.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

Three observations.

The acceleration is real and it is the most important fact here. Revenue growth went 27.0% → 26.1% → 27.7% across three years and then 32.1% in the March quarter. A 27%-grower re-accelerating to 32% at $4 billion of scale is rare, and it is the change that justifies a large part of the 112.5% twelve-month return. That is a company spending to defend and extend a position, and it is a defensible choice; it is also the reason the trailing GAAP price-to-earnings ratio is 748.8x.
