The Overview

Deere makes tractors, combine harvesters, construction diggers and road-building machines, and it also runs a bank that lends farmers the money to buy them. About four-fifths of the business is farm equipment and construction equipment; the rest is the lending.

Farming has had two bad years. When crop prices fall, farmers stop buying new tractors, and Deere's sales fell from $60 billion in 2023 to $45 billion in 2025 — a quarter of the business gone in two years — with profit falling by half. Deere itself expects the market for its biggest, most expensive tractors and combines in North America to fall another 15 to 20% this year.

Some parts are recovering. Construction equipment sales rose 29% last quarter and small farm equipment rose 16%. But the biggest and most profitable division — the large tractors and precision-farming technology — fell 14%, and its profit fell 39%.

Two things about the data are worth knowing before looking at any screen.

First, when analysts publish a revenue forecast for Deere, they are forecasting only the machinery sales. When the company reports "revenue," it includes the lending business too. These are different numbers by about $6 billion a year, and comparing one to the other makes it look as though sales are falling when the machinery business is actually expected to grow about 9%.

Second, Deere looks heavily indebted — around $56 billion net — and it is not, in the way that number implies. Almost all of that debt belongs to the finance arm and is matched against $64 billion of loans to farmers. The actual machinery company owes about $3 billion net. That is the difference between a fragile balance sheet and a very solid one.

The shares are $617.14. The company has told investors it expects to earn $4.5 to $5.0 billion this year, which works out to $16.64 to $18.48 per share. Analysts are forecasting $18.09 — above the middle of the company's own range. At $617 you are paying about 34 times those earnings. Our estimate of fair value is $640, about 4% higher. The average analyst says $691, but the analyst community as a whole rates it a Hold, with six firms outright telling clients to sell.

The company reports again on 20 August, sixteen days away.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. The machinery balance sheet is excellent. The multiple is high and the biggest market is still shrinking.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. Two segments growing strongly, the largest one falling hard. Revenue recovering, earnings not yet.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. The precision-farming technology is genuinely valuable and genuinely non-linear — but it sells more content per tractor, not more tractors.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $640 against a current price of $617.14 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and the disclosure trap inside it

Deere & Company, Moline, Illinois; chairman and chief executive John C. May; approximately 73,100 employees; NYSE-listed. Fiscal year ends in late October or early November — fiscal 2025 ended 2025-11-02, and the April 2026 quarter ended 2026-05-03.

Four reportable segments, per the 10-Q and the earnings release:

  • Production & Precision Ag (PPA) — large tractors, combines, sprayers, and the guidance, telematics and autonomy technology stack.
  • Small Ag & Turf (SAT) — smaller tractors, hay and forage equipment, turf and utility.
  • Construction & Forestry (CF) — construction equipment, compact construction, forestry, and roadbuilding (Wirtgen).
  • Financial Services (FS) — John Deere Capital Corporation, which finances the equipment.

The revenue-definition trap, stated precisely because it changes the answer

Deere reports two revenue lines and the vendor payload uses BOTH without labelling either.

From the Q2 FY2026 earnings release, verbatim: "Worldwide net sales and revenues increased 5 percent, to $13.369 billion, for the second quarter of 2026 and rose 8 percent, to $22.981 billion, for six months. Net sales were $11.778 billion for the quarter and $19.779 billion for six months, compared with $11.171 billion and $17.980 billion last year, respectively."

Now the vendor file:

  • inc_q for 2026-05-03 reports $13,369M — the total basis.
  • earn_cal for 2026-05-21 reports revenueActual $11,778M — the net sales basis.
  • est.revenueAvg for the year ended 2025-11-02 is $38,286.0M against inc_a.revenue of $44,665M — so the estimate block is on the net sales basis too.

The consequence. est.revenueAvg for fiscal 2026 is $41,639.9M. A reader comparing that to the reported fiscal-2025 revenue of $44,665M computes a 6.8% decline. Compared to the correct base of $38,286M, it is an 8.8% increase. The error is 15.6 percentage points and it flips the sign. Every revenue growth figure in this dive states which basis it uses.

A second, smaller inconsistency in the same family. earn_cal.revenueActual is not on a consistent basis across periods: for the quarter ended 2025-07-27 it reports $11,783M, which equals the vendor's inc_q total figure for that quarter; for the quarter ended 2026-05-03 it reports $11,778M, which equals the filing's net sales. The basis switches between fiscal 2025 and fiscal 2026 and the field is not usable for a growth series.

And a third: the vendor's fiscal-2025 quarterly comparatives do not match the filing

The Q2 FY2026 release states prior-year total net sales and revenues of $12,763M for the April 2025 quarter and $21,272M for the first half. The vendor's inc_q reports $12,525M and, with the January 2025 quarter, $20,787M — a shortfall of $485M, or 2.3%. The vendor's fiscal-2025 annual total of $44,665M is correct, so the discrepancy lies in the quarterly allocation. The filing wins: every year-on-year quarterly comparison in this dive is computed from the earnings release, not from inc_q.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: diluted EPS growth (+24.3%, from FY2026E $18.089 to FY2027E $22.479) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 34.1x to roughly 23.5x on the respective forward year, −31%) + dividend yield (+1.05%) ≈ +4% to +5%.

Our base case assumes substantial multiple compression, and this is the defining feature of valuing a cyclical off a trough rather than a de-rating call. Today the market pays 34.1x on the current fiscal year because earnings are depressed. Our $640 is 23.5x the fiscal-2028 consensus and 28.5x fiscal 2027. As earnings normalise, the multiple must fall or the price becomes indefensible on any historical basis — Deere earned $34.80 a share at the fiscal-2023 peak and the shares did not trade at 34x that.

This means almost none of the expected return survives the multiple. If the multiple simply HELD at 27.5x on the fiscal-2028 consensus, the price would be $748 (+21.2%); at 20x, $544 (−11.9%). The entire spread between a good and a bad outcome is a multiple assumption on a cyclical, and we have no informational edge on it.

The bull case at $815 is 30.0x the fiscal-2028 consensus mean, or equivalently 23.0x the fiscal-2028 consensus HIGH of $35.48 — i.e. it can be reached either by a full-cycle earnings recovery at a normalised multiple or by a partial recovery at a premium one.
