The Overview

Danaher does not make anything you would recognise. It makes the equipment and the chemicals that other companies use to discover drugs, manufacture them, and test people for disease. When a biotechnology company grows a therapeutic protein in a tank, the filters and the single-use bags and the purification media are likely Danaher's. When a hospital laboratory runs a blood test, the analyser may well be Danaher's. When a pathologist stains a tissue slide, the same.

That is an excellent kind of business, because roughly 82 cents of every revenue dollar is recurring — customers who bought the machine must keep buying the consumables — and because switching suppliers means revalidating a regulated process, which nobody does casually.

Danaher is also famous for how it is run. It buys companies, applies a management system it has refined for forty years, improves their profitability, and uses the cash to buy more companies. The Synthos knowledge base mentions Danaher four times and every mention is about that: it is named as an example of the model, alongside Berkshire Hathaway and Constellation Software.

Three things have happened recently that the automatic data feed does not know about.

First, in June, Danaher paid about $9.8 billion in cash for Masimo, which makes patient monitors and sensors for hospitals. It borrowed most of the money, in euros and Swiss francs, at interest rates between 1.65% and 2.51%. The company's debt, net of cash, went from about $13.8 billion at the end of last year to about $22.2 billion in June. The data feed still shows the old figure.

Second, on 3 August — the day before this was written — Danaher announced a new chief executive. Julie Sawyer Montgomery takes over on 1 October, and Rainer Blair, who is still listed as chief executive in the data feed, leaves the board.

Third, and most importantly, Danaher has not really grown for four years. It sold $29.5 billion of goods in 2021 and $24.6 billion in 2025. Part of that is a business it spun off in 2023, but not all of it: the pandemic created enormous demand for vaccine-manufacturing supplies, customers over-ordered, and then they stopped ordering for two years. Management now expects 3% to 4% growth this year.

The shares cost $194.73. Analysts expect the company to earn about $9.26 per share in 2027 on the measure the company prefers, so you are paying about 21 times. But that measure leaves out about $1.9 billion a year of write-offs on the value of the companies Danaher has bought — roughly $2.22 per share. Counting it, you are paying about 31 times earnings for a business growing 3-4%.

Our estimate is $205, about 5% above the price and below the average analyst target of $221.58. That is not enough of a gap to act on, especially with a new chief executive taking over in eight weeks.

The financing, all of it postdating the vendor's balance sheet:

  • CHF 2.383 billion of Swiss franc notes, private placement completed 3 June 2026 through a wholly-owned finance subsidiary, in seven series: 1.65% due 2031, 1.88% due 2033, 2.10% due 2036, 2.25% due 2038, 2.38% due 2041, 2.51% due 2046 and 2.50% due 2056.
  • Commercial paper, supported by a 364-day unsecured revolving facility expiring 15 April 2027.

Coupons of 1.65% to 2.51% on thirty-year money are not available to a US-dollar issuer, and Danaher's willingness to issue in Euro and Swiss franc is a genuine, underappreciated structural advantage for a company whose model is serial acquisition. It also creates foreign-currency exposure on the principal, which the filing does not quantify here.

The pro forma disclosure, from the same note ($M): treating Masimo as if acquired at the start of the comparable prior year, second-quarter 2026 sales would have been $6,511M against the $6,265M reported, and six-month sales $12,866M against $12,203M. Masimo contributed only sixteen days to the June quarter. Pro forma diluted EPS for the June quarter was $1.27 against $1.24 reported — accretive, but not dramatically so at this stage.

The 10-Q is explicit that the accounting is unfinished: "The Company is continuing to evaluate certain pre-acquisition contingencies including property, plant and equipment, acquired intangible assets, certain acquisition-related liabilities and legal contingencies associated with the acquisition of Masimo." The filing separately discloses litigation between Masimo and Apple running in both directions, all of which "existed at the time of acquisition."

Three observations. First, the vendor's profile.ceo field still reads "Rainer Blair" and will be wrong from 1 October. 4. The earnings-quality question a serial acquirer cannot avoid

Danaher's entire forward valuation is struck on adjusted EPS, and the largest adjustment is the amortisation of the assets it bought.

Management guides approximately $1.9 billion of acquisition-related intangible amortisation for FY2026, at an approximately 17% tax rate and roughly 709 million average diluted shares:

> $1,900M pretax × (1 − 0.17) = $1,577M after tax ÷ 709M shares = approximately $2.22 per share

Against consensus FY2026 adjusted EPS of $8.496, that is 26.1% of the number. A GAAP-equivalent figure would be near $6.28, and the multiple would be 31.0x rather than 22.9x.

The historical record corroborates the size of the gap. FY2025 consensus adjusted EPS was $7.734 against reported GAAP EPS of $5.07 — a $2.66 difference. FY2024: $7.505 adjusted against $5.33 GAAP. The gap is persistent, growing, and mechanically tied to the acquisition cadence: every deal adds intangibles, and every dollar of intangibles adds to the add-back. The Masimo transaction alone added $4,844M of identifiable intangibles.

There is a genuine argument on each side and we will not pretend otherwise.

For the add-back: the amortisation is non-cash, the acquired customer relationships and technology do not actually expire on the accounting schedule, and free cash flow — which is what pays for buybacks, dividends and the next acquisition — is unaffected. The evidence supports this: FY2025 operating cash flow was $6,416M against GAAP net income of $3,614M, an income-quality ratio of 1.66, and free cash flow was $5,260M. Cash generation is genuinely stronger than GAAP earnings suggest.

Against the add-back: Danaher's business model is acquisition. Excluding the cost of the thing you do for a living is not a normalisation, it is a redefinition. A company that spends $9.8 billion every few years to keep revenue flat has a real economic cost that GAAP amortisation approximates and adjusted EPS omits.

Our resolution. We report the forward multiple on adjusted EPS, because that is the basis every estimate in the file is struck on and mixing bases produces nonsense — but every fair value in Section 6 is sanity-checked against free cash flow and against the GAAP-equivalent multiple, and the ~31x GAAP figure is why our base sits below the street's. The honest summary: on cash, Danaher is reasonably valued; on earnings, it is not; and the difference between those two statements is $1.9 billion a year.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch.

What is genuinely excellent: 81.9% of revenue recurring, at $20,127M and growing 7.7% over two years while non-recurring fell; a 58.5% gross margin held within a 56-61% band across six years spanning a pandemic boom and a two-year destock; $5,260M of free cash flow on $24,568M of revenue, a 21.4% conversion, with an income-quality ratio of 1.66; three segments serving pharmaceutical, biotechnology and clinical-laboratory customers whose demand does not disappear; a forty-year serial-acquisition record that the knowledge base names alongside Berkshire Hathaway and Constellation Software; access to thirty-year money at 2.50% in Swiss francs; and $3,088M of stock repurchased in FY2025 against a shrinking share count.

In the Synthos frame, a name where our fair value sits 5% above the price and below the street's, with an empty conviction lane and a leadership transition inside the horizon, is a Watch by construction.
