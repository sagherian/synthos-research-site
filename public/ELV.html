The Overview

Elevance Health sells health insurance to about 118 million Americans, mostly under the Blue Cross Blue Shield brand. It also owns Carelon, which provides pharmacy, mental-health and care services — partly to its own members and partly to other insurers.

An insurer makes money on the gap between the premiums it collects and the medical bills it pays. That gap has been closing. The share of premiums paid out in medical costs — the company calls it the benefit expense ratio — was 88.9% a year ago and 89.7% in the most recent quarter. Each percentage point is worth roughly $1.65 billion a year of pre-tax profit.

The result is that profits are falling. Adjusted earnings per share were $33.21 in 2024, roughly $30 in 2025, and the company's own guidance for 2026 is at least $26.75 — two consecutive years of decline.

And management has told the market it gets worse before it gets better. Its guidance for the full year is a benefit ratio of 90.2%. The first half ran at 88.3%. That means the second half has to be around 92%. Analysts expect $4.70 per share in the next quarter, against $7.45 in the last one — a 37% drop that everyone is already expecting.

So why is this a "Watch" and not an "Avoid"?

Because of how insurance works, and the clearest statement of it in our research files comes from an outside analyst rather than from the company: "Health insurance is short-tail — one-year liabilities repriced every 12 months — so profit misses aren't perpetual; margins rebuild." An insurer that under-charged this year can raise prices next year. Analysts expect exactly that: earnings recovering to $29.64 in 2027 and $34.00 in 2028.

Meanwhile the balance sheet is improving — debt as a share of capital fell from 42.1% to 40.8% — cash generation doubled in the first half, and the company bought back $1.36 billion of its own shares.

The shares cost $377.905, about 12.8 times what analysts expect for 2027. Our estimate of fair value is $400. Bad case $291; good case $532. That is about $1.77 of possible gain per dollar of loss — real, but below our two-to-one bar.

One thing worth knowing about the accounting. The company's guidance says GAAP earnings will be at least $19.85 per share but adjusted earnings at least $26.75. The difference — $6.90 per share, more than a quarter of the adjusted number — is described only as "net unfavorable items." That is a very large gap to take on trust, and it is one of the reasons we want to see a quarter before acting.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Four observations.

First, premiums did not grow at all. $41,279 million against $41,271 million — plus 0.0%. All of the 1.4% total revenue growth came from product revenue, service fees and a 44.9% increase in net investment income. A health insurer whose premium line is flat is either losing members or holding price, and in the current environment probably both.

Second, the benefit ratio and the operating ratio deteriorated together, which is unusual and worse. Benefit expense ratio +80 basis points; operating expense ratio +100 basis points. The first is medical cost trend, which management does not control; the second is administrative, which it does. Operating expense rose 11.0% in the quarter and 15.3% in the half against revenue up 2.0%.

Third, the reported "beat" and the reported decline are both true. The earnings calendar records $7.45 actual against a $6.21 estimate — a 20.0% beat — on the adjusted basis. The GAAP diluted line fell from $7.72 to $6.71. The gap between $7.45 adjusted and $6.71 GAAP is $0.74 in a single quarter, consistent with the full-year guidance of approximately $6.90 of net unfavourable items.

Fourth, the buyback is doing real work. Average diluted shares fell 3.5% year on year, which converted a 16.1% decline in shareholders' net income into a 13.1% decline in EPS.

The second half is guided materially worse than the first. The first half ran a benefit expense ratio of 88.3%. Full-year guidance is 90.2%. The guidance describes it only as "net unfavorable items" and adds that it "does not include any other adjustment items beyond those reported in the Company's first quarter 2026 earnings" — so the items are identified somewhere the extraction does not reach. An adjustment of that size, sustained, is not an adjustment; and this dive flags it rather than accepting the adjusted figure uncritically.

Operating cash flow doubled while earnings fell 17.8%, driven by a $4,124 million increase in accounts payable and other liabilities and a smaller receivables build ($1,831 million against $4,097 million). For an insurer, operating cash flow leads and lags the income statement through reserve timing, and a doubling alongside falling earnings is a timing effect rather than an improvement in profitability. That figure should be read with care: for a health insurer, operating cash flow includes movements in medical claims payable, unearned premium and policy liabilities, which are float rather than earnings.
