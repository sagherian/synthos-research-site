The Overview

Emerson sells the machinery that runs factories, refineries and power plants — the valves that control flow, the instruments that measure it, and the software that decides what to do. Over the last few years it also bought two software companies: AspenTech, which optimises industrial processes, and National Instruments, which makes test equipment.

It reported results this morning and they were good, without qualification. Sales grew 7%. New orders — the thing that tells you about next year rather than last quarter — grew 7%. Profit margins widened. Cash generation rose 36%. And the company raised its forecast for the year, which almost none of the companies in this research batch has done.

The market noticed. The shares rose 2.6% today and are now 1.7% below their highest price of the past year, having risen 17% in three months. The technical indicators say the stock is more stretched than any other name we looked at this week.

So the question is not whether Emerson is a good business — it plainly is — but what is left in the price. You pay about 22 times what analysts expect it to earn in the year to September 2027, for a company they expect to grow profits about 11% a year. The average analyst thinks the shares are worth $162, which is 2% above where they closed.

One accounting point worth knowing. Emerson reports two profit numbers: a statutory one of about $4.89 a share this year, and an "adjusted" one of about $6.55. The $1.66 difference is 84% amortisation of the intangible assets created when it bought those software companies. That charge does not go away for many years, and every valuation you read — including the multiples above — uses the higher number.

We think the shares are worth about $168 against $159. Nine dollars, on a company that has just reported and just risen. That is not enough.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Excellent margins and cash flow; heavy goodwill, negative tangible book, commercial-paper funding.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Orders up 7% — the right leading indicator — on ~3.5% full-year underlying growth.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. A genuine, deliberate mix shift toward software, bought rather than built.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $168 against a current price of $158.88 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

The adjustment is $1.66 per share, 34% of guided GAAP earnings, and 84% of it is intangible amortisation.
