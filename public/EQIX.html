The Overview

Equinix owns about 240 buildings around the world full of other companies' computers. That sounds like a landlord business, and legally it is — Equinix is a real estate investment trust. But the reason customers pay a premium is not the floor space. It is that everyone else is already in the building. Inside an Equinix facility a bank can plug directly into a cloud provider, an exchange, a network carrier and a hundred others with a short cable rather than sending traffic across the public internet. Once a customer is plugged into that web, leaving is very expensive.

Because Equinix is a REIT, the usual "price divided by earnings" number is misleading — accounting rules force it to charge enormous depreciation on buildings that are not actually losing value. The industry instead uses adjusted funds from operations, or AFFO, which is roughly the cash available to pay dividends. The data file this report is built from does not contain that number at all — it shows a price-to-earnings ratio of 67 for a company that actually trades at about 23 times AFFO. So we rebuilt AFFO from the quarterly report.

On the right measure, the last quarter was outstanding. Revenue rose 16.4% — a big jump from about 6% a year earlier. Profits before depreciation rose 23.7%, and the profit margin widened from 50.0% to 53.2%. AFFO per share rose 18.9%. The Americas region grew nearly 25%.

The problem is what it costs. Equinix spent $4.3 billion building last year while generating $3.9 billion of cash from operations. The gap is filled with borrowing and by selling new shares. The number of shares has risen 12% in five years, which means each existing share owns a slightly smaller piece. Total borrowings are about $21 billion, and in late July the company arranged a new $5.5 billion credit line to keep funding it.

The shares cost $1,051.53. Fifty-two analysts follow the company and thirty-nine say Buy, with an average target of $1,191.65. Our estimate is $1,160 — slightly below theirs.

One more thing worth knowing: three executives and directors sold shares in the open market this summer, the most recent a director selling 4,000 shares at $1,019.28 the day before this report is dated.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
