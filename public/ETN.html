The Overview

Eaton makes the electrical equipment that gets power from the grid into a building and distributes it safely inside — switchgear, circuit protection, busbars, backup power systems, transformers. Roughly two-thirds of its business is that; the rest is aircraft hydraulics and controls, and a vehicle-parts division it is in the process of giving away.

The reason the shares are at a record is artificial-intelligence data centres. A data centre is, from Eaton's point of view, an enormous electrical installation. Every megawatt of computing needs a lot of what Eaton sells, and the buildings are being commissioned faster than the industry can supply the parts.

The evidence for that is unusually solid, because it is in orders rather than opinions. At the end of June, Eaton had $24.1 billion of firm customer orders it had not yet delivered — about ten months of sales. In its largest division, new orders in the quarter came in 41% higher than a year earlier, and it took $1.30 of new orders for every $1.00 it shipped. In its international electrical division, the order book has doubled.

Two things complicate the picture. First, in March Eaton paid $9.55 billion for Boyd Thermal, a maker of cooling systems for data centres. It borrowed most of that money, and its debt net of cash went from $9.1 billion to $19.9 billion in six months. The accounting charges from that purchase are why official ("GAAP") profit per share fell 16% last quarter even though the underlying business grew. Second, the cost of copper and steel has risen sharply, taking about 3.5 percentage points off the gross profit margin.

There is also a corporate change the data we normally rely on knows nothing about: on 10 June Eaton agreed to hand its vehicle-parts business to Dana Incorporated, receiving about $1.1 billion in cash while Eaton's own shareholders end up owning at least half of the combined company.

The shares are $444.72 — the highest closing price of the last year. They trade at about 28 times what analysts expect the company to earn next year. Our estimate of fair value is $495, about 11% higher; the average analyst says $496. We think the analysts' revenue forecast for next year is too low given the order book, which is the main reason we are buying rather than watching.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

The margin problem, in the company's own bridges

Electrical Americas margin fell from 29.5% to 27.5%. The 10-Q: "a 470 basis point decline from higher commodity inflation, partially offset by a 260 basis point increase from higher sales."

Electrical Global margin fell from 20.1% to 19.8%, with "a 460 basis point decline from higher commodity and wage inflation, partially offset by a 240 basis point increase from higher sales, a 100 basis point increase from favorable mix, and an 80 basis point increase from operating efficiencies."

Aerospace margin ROSE from 22.2% to 22.8%, absorbing "a 300 basis point decline from higher commodity and wage inflation."

Consolidated gross margin: 33.5% against 37.0%, a 350 basis point decline.

This is a price-cost timing problem, not a competitive one, and the bridges prove it — in every segment the volume, mix and efficiency contributions are positive and the single negative line is input cost. The favourable reading is that Eaton has not lost pricing power; the unfavourable one is that a company shipping into a 1.3 book-to-bill should be able to reprice, and has not yet.
