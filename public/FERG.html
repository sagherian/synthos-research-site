The Overview

Ferguson sells the unglamorous physical stuff that goes inside buildings: pipes, valves, fittings, water heaters, air-conditioning equipment, fire sprinklers, and the water and sewer products that cities buy. Plumbers, contractors and city works departments are the customers. It is the biggest player of its kind in North America, with 35,000 employees, and about 95 percent of its sales are in the United States with the rest in Canada.

It is a good business in a boring way. It barely spends anything on equipment — under one percent of sales — and yet earns a 35 percent return on shareholders' money. It borrows carefully, covers its interest bill fifteen times over, pays a dividend that eats only a quarter of profits, and buys back a lot of its own stock.

But here is what actually happened over the past few years, which most write-ups skip. Between 2022 and 2025, sales went up only about 2.5 percent a year, and profitability got worse — the operating profit margin fell from 9.9 percent to 8.5 percent. Earnings per share actually went down over those three years, from $9.69 to $9.32, and that is after the company shrank its share count by 9 percent. Without the buybacks it would have looked considerably worse. Housing slowed, construction slowed, and Ferguson slowed with it.

This year is better. Over the first nine months of the current financial year, profits are up 21 percent and the margin has recovered by a full percentage point. That is a real recovery. The catch is the most recent quarter: sales were down 2 percent versus a year earlier. So the profit recovery is coming from running the business better and buying back stock — not from selling more. That is a fragile kind of recovery.

And then there is the timing. The stock jumped nearly 7 percent in one day on August 3rd — our data doesn't say why — and it reports results on August 10th, six days after this was written. Paying up 7 percent right before a results announcement, in a company whose last reported quarter had falling sales, is not a good bet. Our estimate of what it is worth, $251.50, is almost exactly where it trades.

So: a fine business, priced about right, one week before the news that matters. Wait for it.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What they actually sell — and what the segment disclosure will and will not tell you

Ferguson distributes plumbing and heating supplies (pipes, valves, fittings, water heaters, kitchen and bathroom fixtures), HVAC and refrigeration equipment, fire sprinkler systems, water management products (water meters, irrigation, drainage, geosynthetics, stormwater), and industrial MRO (flanges, HDPE, custom fabrication, water and wastewater treatment, pipe-valve-fitting systems) to residential, commercial, civil/infrastructure and industrial customers across the United States and Canada. It employs 35,000 people. CEO Kevin Murphy.

The segment disclosure in this dataset is thin and we should say so. seg_prod contains exactly one row — FY2025, "United States Segment: $29.269B". seg_geo contains exactly one row — FY2025, "Canada Segment: $1.493B". That is the entire segment history available here.

What it does tell us, cleanly: US 95.1%, Canada 4.9% of FY2025's $30.762B. Ferguson is, for practical purposes, a US business with a Canadian tail. What it does not tell us, and we will not invent: the end-market split between residential, commercial, civil and industrial; the product-line mix; or any data-centre or electrification exposure. There is no data-centre revenue disclosure in this file.

The five-year picture looks better only because FY2020-FY2022 captured the post-pandemic construction and building-materials inflation spike — the FY2020-to-FY2025 revenue CAGR of 9.06% is almost entirely front-loaded into those two years.

What is genuinely good. Operating margin recovered 100 basis points year over year on the nine months, from 7.55% to 8.55%, driving operating income up 17.7% on revenue up only 3.9%. EPS is up 21.5%. That is real operating leverage and it is the core of the bull case.

4. The serial-acquirer frame — and what the capital allocation has actually become

The task frame for Ferguson is organic versus acquired growth and incremental margins. Here is what the data supports.

The most important observation in this section: Ferguson has stopped being a consolidator and become a share-shrinker. Acquisition spend halved — $650M and $616M in FY2022-FY2023 down to $260M and $301M in FY2024-FY2025 — while buybacks ran $948M in FY2025, which is 3.1x the acquisition spend. Whatever the reason (price discipline, a thin target pipeline, or a deliberate strategy shift), the growth algorithm has changed materially, and it matters because a distributor that stops buying share is a distributor whose growth is now purely organic and cyclical.

The dataset does not disclose organic versus acquired revenue growth and we are not going to estimate it. What we can say is that with only ~$300M of annual bolt-on spend against $30B of revenue, the acquired contribution to recent growth is necessarily small — which means the 2.5% annual revenue growth of FY2022-FY2025 was very nearly all organic, and that is the number to judge the business on.

The cash-conversion yellow flag. FY2025 free cash flow conversion was a healthy 86.4%. But the TTM figures tell a different story: incomeQualityTTM is 0.598 — operating cash flow is only 59.8% of net income — producing a trailing price-to-free-cash-flow of 48.1x against a price-to-earnings of 23.7x, and a free cash flow yield of 2.08% against an earnings yield of 4.26%. That gap is a working-capital build (inventory grew from $4.188B to $4.492B in FY2025, and cash conversion cycle is 59.0 days). It is not alarming in a recovering distributor restocking into demand — but the earnings recovery is not yet a cash recovery, and that distinction should decide how much credit the FY2026 EPS growth gets.

The unexplained item — flagged, not resolved. Retained earnings fell from $9.589B (FY2024) to $6.776B (FY2025), a decline of $2.813B, in a year with $1.856B of net income and $1.437B of dividends plus buybacks combined. Simple arithmetic leaves a residual of roughly $3.2B that this dataset does not explain. It is most likely a capital or corporate reorganisation entry rather than an economic loss — total equity actually rose from $5.616B to $5.832B over the same period, which argues strongly against value destruction. But the dataset does not say, we will not guess, and any reader should check the FY2025 statement of changes in equity before relying on book-value-derived figures.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

expected return ≈ EPS growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield

Over a two-year horizon to FY2028E, consensus adjusted EPS grows from $11.29 to $13.93 — 11.1% a year. Shareholder yield is meaningful here and worth counting properly: the 1.40% dividend plus roughly 3.1% a year of share-count reduction gives about 4.5% — though the buyback is already inside the EPS growth figure, so counting it twice would be double-dipping. The clean decomposition is therefore ~11% EPS growth (of which about a third is the buyback) plus a 1.4% dividend, less multiple drift.

Our base case assumes essentially FLAT multiples — 19.9x FY2027E today becoming 20x FY2027E at fair value. We are explicitly not underwriting expansion, and we are not forecasting compression either. The reasoning: at 20x for an 11% EPS grower with 24% ROCE, the multiple is neither stretched nor cheap, and there is no obvious catalyst for the market to re-rate it in either direction absent a change in the growth rate.

Note the rolldown trap. 22.1x FY2026E becoming 17.9x FY2028E at an unchanged $250.08 is mechanical, not a re-rating — it is the estimate advancing. Anyone quoting "under 18x" as evidence of cheapness is quoting a 2028 number at a 2026 price.

So the bridge is: +11.1% a year of EPS growth, +1.4% dividend, roughly 0% from the multiple — call it 12-13% a year IF consensus is met. The entire question is the "if," and it rests on 6% revenue growth from a business that just printed minus 2.0%.

  • We differ from consensus on the durability of the revenue line, not on the quality of the business. 9. Verdict, kill-criteria and the flip conditions

Watch (pre-catalyst). No position before 2026-08-10.

Ferguson is a legitimately high-quality business — 35.1% return on equity, 17.7% ROIC, 24.0% ROCE, 15.4x interest coverage, 1.58x leverage, capex under 1% of revenue, a 24.5% dividend payout, and a buyback that has retired 9.0% of the shares in three years. Nine-month FY2026 shows a real margin recovery: operating margin 8.55% versus 7.55%, operating income plus 17.7%, EPS plus 21.5%.

Three things say wait. First, the top line has not recovered — revenue grew 2.5% a year from FY2022 to FY2025 and Q3 FY2026 printed minus 2.0%, so the entire FY2026 improvement is margin and buyback rather than volume. Second, the earnings recovery is not yet a cash recovery — trailing operating cash flow is only 59.8% of net income, putting the stock at 48.1x trailing free cash flow against a 23.7x P/E. Third, the timing is bad — a plus 6.72% single-session gap the data does not explain, six days before the Q4 FY2026 print, into a base fair value of $251.50 that is 0.6% above spot. We have no edge at this price, and the house rule for no-edge names is Watch.

Pre-registered kill / avoid criteria:

  • A second consecutive quarter of flat or negative revenue at the 2026-08-10 print (consensus $8.676B) — the Q3 minus 2.0% was the trend, not an artefact, and the 6% consensus growth path is wrong.
  • Operating margin giving back the nine-month gain, falling back toward the 7.5%-8.0% zone.
  • Cash conversion staying below ~70% of net income for another two quarters — the working-capital build is structural, not transitory, and the free-cash-flow multiple is the true one.

  • Losing the reclaimed 200-DMA ($240.30) on the print — the gap was a fake-out and the downtrend resumes.

  • OR a pullback to the $201-$215 zone (near the 52-week low) on no fundamental deterioration, which takes the FY2027E multiple to 16-17x for a 35%-ROE distributor. That is a price with a margin of safety.
