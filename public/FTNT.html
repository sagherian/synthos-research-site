The Overview

Fortinet makes the equipment and software that sits at the edge of a company's network and decides what traffic is allowed in and out — firewalls, plus the subscriptions that keep them updated with the latest threat information, plus the support contracts that keep them running. Its distinguishing trick is that it designs its own specialised chip for the job, which is why it can sell physical boxes and still keep about 70 cents of every hardware dollar as gross profit, and about 87 cents of every service dollar.

In the three months to June it did very well. Sales rose 26%, the amount it invoiced customers rose 33%, and the cash it generated after capital spending was $966 million against $284 million a year before. It raised its forecast for the year. The shares have doubled in six months.

Here is what a reader needs to see underneath that. Fortinet reports its revenue in two pieces. The hardware piece grew 52%. The service piece — subscriptions and support, which is 62% of the company and the part that renews automatically every year — grew 14%. Subscriptions on their own grew 13%.

That difference matters because the two behave completely differently. Service revenue is an annuity: it arrives whether or not customers buy anything new. Hardware revenue is a cycle: companies replace their firewalls every five to seven years, and when a large batch of boxes bought several years ago all comes due at once, sales spike and then settle back. The people who follow this company for a living think that is what is happening — the average forecast for 2027 is 11% revenue growth, down from 19% this year.

The shares cost $168.29. That is 45 times what analysts expect Fortinet to earn in 2027. For comparison, those same analysts think the shares are worth $151.31 — less than the price. The middle estimate among them is $164 — also less than the price. Only 29 of the 68 who follow it call it a buy; 33 say hold and six say sell.

There is one more fact worth holding onto. In early 2026 Fortinet's own board was buying back its shares at an average of $76.68. Six months later they cost $168.29. The company was a very good judge of value at $77. Nothing tells us it would be at $168.

Our own estimate of what the shares are worth is $152 — 10% below the price, and almost exactly where the average analyst sits.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. The finances cannot break — $3.6 billion of net cash and almost no debt. The price has nothing under it: it is at an all-time high and every published target is below it.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. Genuinely large and genuinely accelerating, but narrower than the headline: the recurring two-thirds is growing 14%.
  • Exponential Potential 6/10. A real platform with its own silicon, in a market that grows with corporate security budgets rather than on an adoption curve.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $152 against a current price of $168.29 — consistent with our call to stay away or wait for a better setup.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Six months to June 2026: Product $1,418.1M against $968.0M, +46.5%; Service $2,479.4M against $2,201.7M, +12.6%; total $3,897.5M against $3,169.7M, +23.0%.

Read those two rows against each other and the entire investment question is visible. The company's headline growth rate is 25.6%. Its recurring growth rate is 13.7%. The gap is a hardware replacement wave, and hardware is not an annuity.

Two honest qualifications, because this cuts both ways.

First, the bull qualification, and it is substantive. Every FortiGate sold in a refresh drags subscription and support revenue behind it for the life of the box. The mechanism by which a product cycle becomes a service annuity is visible in the deferred revenue balance: $7,675.7M at 30 June 2026 against $6,567.6M a year earlier, up 16.9% — faster than the 13.7% at which service revenue is currently being recognised. Remaining performance obligations were $7.73B, of which $3.86B converts within twelve months and $3.08B in years two and three. Billings — revenue plus the change in deferred revenue — grew 33% to $2,372.1M. The invoiced base is running well ahead of the recognised base, and that is precisely what you would expect to see if the refresh were converting into an annuity rather than evaporating.

Second, the bear qualification. Billings and deferred revenue both include the hardware. A three-year subscription sold alongside a box inflates deferred revenue at the moment of the refresh and then amortises away. The forward-looking test is not the level of deferred revenue but whether service revenue growth accelerates above 13.7% in the coming quarters. It has not yet.

And gross margin already shows the mix effect. Blended GAAP gross margin was 80.2% in Q2 2026 against 81.5% in Q2 2025 — a 130 basis point decline. But it is not a pricing problem: product gross margin actually IMPROVED, from 67.4% to 69.8%, while service gross margin was 86.6% against 86.7%. The blended decline is arithmetic — a lower-margin line growing four times faster than a higher-margin one. That is a fact worth holding: the margin dilution is mix, not price, and it will reverse when the refresh does.

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+13.3%, from FY2027E $3.743 to FY2028E $4.240) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 45.0x to roughly 36x on the respective forward year, −20%) + shareholder yield (+1.5% to 2.5%, all buyback) ≈ −5% to −7%.

Our base assumes meaningful multiple compression, and the reason is the growth profile the multiple is attached to, not a rate or risk argument. Today the market pays 45.0x on the next calendar year. A 45x multiple on a low-teens grower survives only while the current quarter's 26% headline is fresh. The compression we assume is not the mechanical rolldown of a forward year — at a constant price, 45.0x FY2027 becomes 39.7x FY2028 automatically, and that is not re-rating. We are assuming the market pays 36x FY2028E in twelve months' time against 45.0x FY2027E today: a genuine 20% de-rating.

The distinction that matters. We are not questioning the quality of this business, and nothing above should be read that way. Our objection is that the market has taken a replacement cycle and paid a compounding multiple for it, and the segment table — 62% of revenue growing 13.7% — says which one this is. When our own base sits 9.7% below the price, lands within a dollar of a street consensus that is itself below the price, and the knowledge base contributes one stale tactical observation, the honest answer is Watch.
