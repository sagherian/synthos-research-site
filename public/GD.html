The Overview

General Dynamics makes four things. It builds nuclear submarines — one of only two companies in America that can. It builds armoured vehicles for armies. It runs computer systems for the US government. And it makes Gulfstream private jets.

Most of its customers are governments, and the way that business works is that you win a contract today and deliver over five or ten years. So the useful number is not what the company sold last quarter — it is how much work it has already been ordered to do. That number is $136.5 billion, which is about two and a half years of revenue, and it went up by $5.7 billion in three months.

There is an even better way to see it. In the defense businesses, for every dollar of work General Dynamics finished in the first half of the year, it received $1.80 of new orders. That ratio — called book-to-bill — being well above 1.0 means the pile of future work is growing, not shrinking. The new orders were mostly for more Virginia-class submarines and for armoured vehicles.

The Gulfstream business is also doing well: sales up 15% and profitability improved. And the company collected a lot of cash — $4.0 billion in six months, nearly triple last year — which cut its net borrowings almost in half.

So why only "Watch" and not "Buy"?

Because the shares already reflect all of this. They cost $385.76, which is within 2% of their highest price of the past year. Thirty-five analysts follow the company and most say Buy; their average target is $420.67. Our own estimate of fair value is $420. We agree with the market almost exactly, which means we have no advantage — and paying a full price for a consensus view is not how good returns are made.

Two honest worries. First, the fastest-growing part of the company — shipbuilding — is also the least profitable, earning about 7 cents on the dollar against 14.5 cents at Gulfstream. Second, and more serious: several independent commentators we track argue that cheap drones are making expensive traditional military hardware obsolete, and that newer companies are taking work from the old contractors. One of them named General Dynamics directly. He has been wrong for four months. He may not be wrong forever.

  • Downside Risk 4/10. Two and a half years of ordered work, a government customer, and very little debt.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. Excellent and unusually well documented — and the fastest-growing piece is the least profitable.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. One irreplaceable shipyard. No compounding mechanism.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $420 against a current price of $390.39 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Three observations that shape everything else.

The growth is concentrated in the lowest-margin segment. Marine Systems grew 15.3% in the first half and earns 7.3%; Aerospace grew 11.8% and earns 14.7%. Because Marine is a third of revenue and Aerospace a quarter, consolidated margin faces a structural mix headwind even when both segments improve — which is exactly what happened: Marine margin rose 40 basis points and Aerospace 130, and consolidated margin rose only 30.

Combat Systems has stopped growing. +0.3% in the quarter and +2.6% in the half, with margin down 30 basis points to 13.9%. This is the armoured-vehicle business, and it is the segment most directly exposed to the "cheap drones make armour obsolete" argument that appears twice in the knowledge base. Technologies is the odd one out. 25.7% of revenue, 4.1% growth, a 9.4% margin that is declining. It is a government-IT services business sitting inside a platform manufacturer, and it earns neither Aerospace's margin nor Marine's growth.

What a 1.8-to-1 book-to-bill actually means, stated without inflation. For every dollar of defense revenue recognised in the first six months of 2026, $1.80 of new orders arrived. Backlog grew $5.7 billion in a quarter in which the company recognised $14.1 billion of revenue. At the current pace the pile of contracted future work is growing faster than the company can consume it, which is the single most reliable leading indicator this industry produces.

Three honest qualifications.

Backlog is not revenue and its conversion rate is not disclosed here. The 10-Q states GD expects to recognise a proportion of remaining performance obligations over time, but the specific percentage falls outside the extracted text. We do not estimate it.

"Unfunded" backlog carries an explicit warning from the company itself: "The unfunded portion of total backlog includes the amounts we believe are likely to be funded, but there is no guarantee that future budgets and appropriations will provide the same funding level currently anticipated for a given program." That is a real caveat on a defense contractor whose customer's budget is set annually by Congress.

A 1.8-to-1 half-year ratio is a step-change, not a run rate. It was produced by specific large awards — Virginia-class submarine construction and combat support vehicles — and multi-billion-dollar programme awards are lumpy. A reading near 1.0 next quarter would be normal, not a deterioration.
