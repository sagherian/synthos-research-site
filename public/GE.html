The Overview

GE Aerospace makes jet engines. It sells them at very thin margins — or at a loss — and then makes its money for the next twenty-five years selling the spare parts and doing the overhauls, at roughly sixty cents of gross profit on every dollar. There are about 80,000 GE engines flying today, split between airlines and militaries, and each one is a small annuity.

That business is going extremely well. In the first six months of this year the revenue from overhauling engines rose 30%, the number of engines delivered rose 37%, and deliveries of the newest engine, the LEAP, rose 41%. Notably, the number of flights barely changed — so this growth came from more work per engine and higher prices, not from more flying. The company also signed several large new contracts, including one covering Ryanair's entire fleet of roughly 2,000 engines.

The share price has responded: up 40% over a year and 34% in the last three months alone, closing today within a whisker of its highest price of the year.

Two things make us cautious. The first is simply cost. The shares trade at about 53 times last year's adjusted profit. For comparison, the whole US market trades at roughly 25 times. You are paying a premium price for a premium business, and our own estimate of fair value, $395, is only 5% above today's $377 — and lower than the average analyst's $419.

The second is more technical and more important. A company's profit is an accounting number; the cash it actually collects is not. Over the last year GE reported $8.97 billion of profit but only about $5.0 billion of cash from operations — 55 cents of cash per dollar of profit, where last year it was 98 cents. After spending on factories, the cash left over is small enough that the shares cost 109 times it. We cannot explain the gap from the filings we can read, because the relevant tables are not extractable, and we would rather say that plainly than construct an explanation.

There is also a data point worth knowing about our own research library. GE broke itself into three companies between 2023 and 2024 — GE Aerospace, GE Vernova (power) and GE HealthCare. Nine of the twenty-three research claims we found under the "GE" label are actually about the other two companies, and all three claims from our highest-rated analyst on this name are about GE Vernova's gas turbines being expensive. Those are not views on this business, and we have excluded them.

  • Growth Quality 8/10. Excellent and unusually visible — analysts are still modelling 2029 with confidence, which is rare.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

GE Aerospace traces to Thomas Edison's 1878 founding, is headquartered in Evendale, Ohio, employs 57,000 people, and is led by H. Lawrence Culp Jr. NYSE-listed; CUSIP 369604301.

The critical structural fact, and the one the knowledge base has not absorbed: this is no longer General Electric. Between January 2023 and April 2024 the conglomerate separated into three independent public companies — GE HealthCare, GE Vernova (power, wind and electrification) and GE Aerospace, which retained the GE ticker. Any claim about gas turbines, wind, medical imaging or small modular reactors is a claim about a different registrant. Section 7 shows how badly that has contaminated this name's research lane.

Two reportable segments

From the 10-Q: Commercial Engines & Services (CES) and Defense & Propulsion Technologies (DPT). The filing notes that DPT is itself an aggregation: "We have aggregated Defense & Systems and Propulsion & Additive Technology into one reportable segment, Defense & Propulsion Technologies, based on similarity in economic characteristics."

The economic engine is stated in one sentence in the 10-K:

> "installed base of approximately 50,000 commercial and 30,000 military engines, including parked aircraft in addition to fleet in service, supports our aftermarket services business."

And the revenue mix confirms the model. The vendor's FY2024 seg_prod row — the only one in the file that is both current and interpretable — splits revenue three ways and ties exactly to reported revenue:

10. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Hold.

This is the best business model in the batch and one of the two most expensive stocks in it.

What is genuinely excellent, and all of it is filing-verified: an installed base of "approximately 50,000 commercial and 30,000 military engines"; internal shop visit revenue up 30% in the first half and LEAP engine deliveries up 41%, achieved while "commercial air travel departures were roughly flat"; second-quarter revenue up 21.1% to $13.349B; five consecutive earnings beats averaging 12.9%; new GEnx wins at United and Delta, LEAP wins at American and Copa, and a Ryanair services agreement covering "about 2,000 CFM56 and LEAP engines"; net debt of $8.102B at 0.69x EBITDA with interest coverage of 11.5x; the deepest analyst coverage of any name in this batch, with ten analysts still modelling FY2029 — which is itself a statement about how forecastable this revenue is; and a knowledge-base structural claim, corroborated by the 10-Q's own profit bridge, that engines are sold near cost and the money is made on 60%-gross-margin aftermarket worth three to five times the original-equipment value.

What we are declining to pay for: 53.1x trailing adjusted earnings, 44.5x trailing GAAP, 41.4x FY2027 consensus and 109x trailing free cash flow; a base fair value of $395 that is 4.7% above spot and 5.8% BELOW the street's $419.10; a close 0.37% from the 52-week high after a 34.5% three-month run; and above all an operating cash flow conversion of 0.555x net income against 0.98x last year — roughly $4 billion a year of reported profit not appearing as cash — which we cannot explain because the cash-flow statement is not extractable. Add a knowledge-base lane that is six claims from effectively two sources, with nine of the twenty-three raw hits belonging to companies GE spun off.

The distinction that matters. We are not saying GE Aerospace is expensive relative to what it is. An 80,000-engine certified installed base compounding aftermarket revenue at 30% is worth a premium and always has been. We are saying that we cannot currently reconcile the earnings this premium is paid on with the cash the company is generating, that our own fair value sits below the street's, and that the entry price is a 52-week high. In the Synthos frame, an outstanding business with an unexplained cash gap and no valuation cushion is a Hold — own the annuity, do not add into the high, and let the October cash-flow statement decide.
