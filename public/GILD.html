The Overview

Gilead makes the medicines that most people with HIV in the developed world take. That is an unusually good business: patients take the drugs for life, there are only a few regimens that work as well, and Gilead keeps about 79 cents of profit on every dollar of sales before overheads. It produced about $9.5 billion of spare cash last year while spending only $563 million on equipment.

The problem Gilead has had for a decade is that this is essentially all it has. HIV is 70% of sales. Its hepatitis C cure made it enormously profitable and then, by curing people, wound itself down. Its COVID drug did the same. Its cancer business, bought for $21 billion in 2020, produces $1.4 billion a year.

So this year the company went shopping. It has bought or agreed to buy four things: Arcellx for $7.1 billion in cash, which owns a cell therapy for blood cancer; Tubulis for about $3.2 billion; Ouro Medicines for about $1.7 billion (half paid by a partner); and a partnership with LEO Pharma.

Here is the part that matters for the accounts. Because each of these is essentially one drug rather than a working business, the accountants treat them as buying an asset, not a company — and the whole price is charged against profit immediately. So Gilead's June quarter shows an operating loss of $10.4 billion, and analysts expect the company to lose money for the full year 2026 and then earn about $9.65 a share in 2027.

None of that is a deterioration in the business. Sales in the June quarter were $7.8 billion, up 10% and better than expected, and the shares rose 3% today.

Two things you should know. The company reported this morning and the detailed release is not available in our source documents, so several figures cannot be reconciled. And the balance sheet on every screen is from December — before roughly $11 billion of cash left the company — so the debt figures you will see are out of date and too low.

The shares are $135.27. Our estimate of value is $148; the average analyst says $159.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. The lowest share-price volatility in this group, huge cash generation, and a balance sheet nobody can currently see.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. The base business grows about 2% a year. Everything faster has just been bought.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. A serious, well-sourced, very expensive attempt to buy a second act.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
