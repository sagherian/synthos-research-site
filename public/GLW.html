The Overview

Corning makes glass, and it has been making glass for 175 years. Some of it is the tough cover glass on a phone; some is the substrate inside a television screen; some is a filter in a car's exhaust; and some is polysilicon, the raw material for solar panels and semiconductors. But the part that matters right now is optical fibre — the hair-thin glass strands that carry light, and therefore data, between machines.

Artificial-intelligence data centres need enormously more of that fibre than ordinary ones, because thousands of processors have to talk to each other constantly and copper wire cannot carry the traffic. You cannot make more optical fibre quickly: it requires furnaces and drawing towers and cabling plants that take years to build. Corning already has them.

The result is visible in the numbers. In the three months to June, Corning's optical communications sales rose 32% and its solar sales rose 90%, and the company as a whole made 30% more profit per share than a year earlier. Two of the largest technology companies in the world signed long-term supply agreements with it in that quarter. One of them, NVIDIA, went further: it paid Corning $500 million for a warrant — a right to buy Corning shares later — and put down a $1 billion deposit, in exchange for Corning building ten times as much optical connectivity capacity in the United States. Companies do not do that unless they are worried about not being able to buy enough.

Management has told investors it intends to grow sales from about $17 billion a year now to a $40 billion annual rate by the end of 2030. That is a very large ambition and it is management's own claim, so we discount it accordingly, but the recent results are consistent with it.

Here is the problem. The shares cost $159.905. Over the last twelve months they have risen 157.7%, which is more than six times what the overall market did. Last year Corning actually earned $2.21 per share under standard accounting rules, so you are paying about 72 times what it made. On the company's own preferred measure the figure is about 56 times. Even looking two years ahead to what analysts expect in 2028, you are paying about 27 times.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $165 — about 3% above the price, and below the average analyst target of $173.75. There is one more thing worth knowing: Corning's cash flow this year looks much better than it is, because it includes that $1 billion customer deposit, which is money received in advance rather than money earned.

So: an excellent business, in the right place, at a price that already knows it. The sensible response is to decide what price would make it compelling — and wait.

Chief executive, chairman and president Wendell P. Weeks; approximately 67,200 full-time employees; headquartered in Corning, New York.

2. The transaction the vendor payload does not know about

On 6 May 2026 Corning entered a securities purchase agreement in connection with what the 10-Q calls "the long-term partnership with a customer to strengthen U.S. manufacturing for AI infrastructure." The earnings release names the customer: NVIDIA.

The terms, verbatim from the 10-Q's Note 12:

> "the Company issued and sold (i) a warrant (the "Traditional Warrant") to purchase up to 15 million shares of common stock of the Company at an exercise price of $180.00 per share, and (ii) a pre-funded warrant... to purchase up to 3 million shares of common stock at an exercise price of $0.0001 per share."

And the accounting, which matters:

> "The Traditional Warrant was issued without separate consideration and does not convey rights to a distinct good or service. Accordingly, it was accounted for as consideration payable to a customer and, as an equity-classified award, was measured at its grant-date fair value of $296 million..."

> "The Pre-Funded Warrant was issued for cash consideration of $500 million... In addition, as a result of the nominal exercise price, the underlying common shares are considered outstanding for purposes of calculating earnings per common share."

Alongside it, from Note 2:

> "During the second quarter of 2026, the Company recorded an approximate $0.7 billion net contract liability, consisting of a $1.0 billion customer deposit under a long-term supply agreement extending through December 31, 2029, partially offset by $296 million for a warrant issued to the customer... The fair value of the warrant will be recognized as a reduction of revenue as sales occur under the agreement."

Four consequences, none of which is in any vendor field.

First, there is $500 million of cash on the balance sheet that came from selling equity, not from operations, and 3 million shares that are already counted as outstanding for earnings-per-share purposes.

Second, there is a $1.0 billion customer deposit sitting inside operating cash flow. The 10-Q is explicit: first-half operating cash flow of $2,079M against $859M a year earlier was "primarily driven by higher net income and the receipt of an upfront customer deposit of $1.0 billion, related to a long-term supply agreement." Trailing free cash flow of roughly $2.40 billion therefore contains approximately $1.0 billion of prepayment. Adjusted, it is nearer $1.4 billion — and the reported price-to-free-cash-flow of 57.5x becomes roughly 98x. The contract-liability balance rose from $2.3 billion to $2.7 billion across the half.

Third, $296 million of future revenue has already been spent. The warrant's grant-date fair value will be recognised as a reduction of revenue as sales occur under the agreement. That is a direct, disclosed, scheduled headwind to reported optical revenue over the life of a contract running to end-2029, and consensus revenue estimates may or may not carry it.

Fourth, there are 15 million warrant shares at $180 outstanding — 1.7% of the share count, struck 12.6% above today's price, exercisable at any time and expiring on the third anniversary. The $137.74B market capitalisation does not include them. At $180 they represent $2.70 billion of proceeds and 15 million new shares; on a fully-exercised basis today's enterprise value would rise and the per-share arithmetic would change.

What this transaction actually signals is more interesting than the accounting. A customer that pays $500 million for a warrant, grants a further warrant on 15 million shares, and puts down a $1.0 billion deposit is not placing an order. It is co-financing capacity because it does not believe the capacity would otherwise exist. From the supplier's side, that is the strongest possible evidence that optical interconnect is a genuine physical bottleneck — and simultaneously the strongest possible evidence that Corning's growth is now tied to a small number of identifiable counterparties. Both readings are correct and both are load-bearing.

The other partnership disclosed in the same release: "Amazon announced a multiyear, multibillion-dollar agreement under which Corning will supply the optical fiber, cable, and connectivity solutions that power Amazon's expanding data center infrastructure across the United States."

3. Growth, margins and the two sets of books

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: core EPS growth (+31.7%, from FY2026E $3.268 to FY2027E $4.303) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 48.9x on the forward year to roughly 38x on the then-forward year, about −22%) + shareholder yield (+0.82%, of which some is offset by 1.2% share creep) ≈ +3% to +10%.

Our base assumes compression, and the reason is arithmetic rather than scepticism. Consensus EPS growth runs +31.7% (FY2027) then +36.5% (FY2028) then +8.9% (FY2029). The multiple the market pays has to fall as it approaches the year where growth halves, and it normally begins doing so a year early. Assuming today's 48.9x forward multiple simply persists would be assuming the market never notices the deceleration built into its own estimates.

Almost all of the expected return is earnings growth, and the multiple is working hard against it. If the multiple held at 48.9x on FY2027E the price would be $210 (+31.7%) — which is exactly the street's high target. If it compressed to 30x the price would be $129 (−19.3%) — which is exactly the street's low target. The street's own $129-to-$210 range is a pure multiple range on a shared earnings estimate, and that is the most honest description of the uncertainty here.

The bear case at $110 requires no earnings collapse — 28.3x the FY2027 consensus low of $3.888. The bull case at $240 requires no re-rating beyond today's level — 47.5x the FY2027 consensus high of $5.052, roughly today's FY2026 multiple applied a year forward.
