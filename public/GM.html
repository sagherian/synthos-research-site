The Overview

General Motors builds cars, trucks and SUVs, mostly in North America, and separately runs a bank-like arm that lends people the money to buy them.

Two things dominate the last eighteen months.

The first is a very expensive change of mind. GM had committed publicly to electric vehicles and to self-driving taxis. It has now largely reversed both. It shut down Cruise, its robotaxi business. It wrote electric-vehicle tooling down to almost nothing. The total cost of that reversal so far is about $11.3 billion — roughly one-seventh of the entire value of the company — and $4.1 billion of it was actual cash leaving in the first six months of this year alone.

The second is that the underlying business got a lot better while that was happening. The North American operation earned a 9.3% profit margin in the first half of the year, against 6.8% for all of last year, which is back inside the range management says it is aiming for. In July the company raised its own profit forecast for the year.

The company is also buying back its shares at a remarkable pace — it has retired about one share in five in a year and a half. When a company shrinks its share count that fast, each remaining share owns more of the profits, and the earnings per share can grow even if the business does not.

At $88.31 the shares cost about six and a half times what analysts expect the company to earn this year. Most companies in this research batch cost more than twenty times. Cars are always cheap, because car making is cyclical and periodically catastrophic — but six times is cheap even for cars.

Our estimate of fair value is $97, about 10% above the price. Analysts average $100.10.

Three things stop us from calling this a buy. The shares are already up 68% in a year and have barely had a down week. Four independent commentators in our knowledge base — including the single most highly weighted voice in it, who said it twice — argue that traditional carmakers cannot survive the transition that is coming. And one of GM's own executive vice presidents sold 40,000 shares at $88.44 on 3 August, the day before this was written.

  • Downside Risk 7/10. Automotive net cash and $33.6 billion of liquidity, against tariffs, China, and a strategy reversal still running.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. Revenue is flat. Every gain is margin recovery and a shrinking share count. That is real; it is not growth.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. Two non-linear bets have been withdrawn at a cost of $11.3 billion. A third, with Nvidia, is live.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $97 against a current price of $88.31 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

The mix point deserves its own sentence. Margin improved partly because GM sold fewer crossovers, including EVs, and more full-size pickup trucks. That is a good outcome for 2026 profit and a direct restatement of the strategic question the bears are asking.
