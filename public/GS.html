The Overview

Goldman Sachs makes money in three ways: advising companies on takeovers and share sales, buying and selling securities for large investors and keeping a small margin, and managing money for institutions and wealthy families.

The first two are wonderful in a busy market and thin in a quiet one. In the three months to June the market was very busy indeed. Goldman's revenue after interest costs was $20.3 billion, 40% more than a year earlier, and profit per share was $20.98 — nearly double the $10.91 of a year earlier, and 45% more than analysts expected. That is the biggest surprise of any company in this group of twelve.

The measure that matters most for a bank is how much profit it earns on the money shareholders have tied up in it. Goldman earned 25.5% on its tangible capital in the quarter. In the very same document, the firm says its own long-run target — what it expects to earn on average through a full cycle — is between 15% and 17%. So Goldman itself is telling you that 25.5% is not normal.

That matters because of what the shares cost. Investors pay $1,052.98 for a share whose tangible book value is $336.61 — a little over three times. Three times book is what you pay when you expect roughly 23% returns forever. If returns settle where the company says they will settle, the same arithmetic gives about $673.

There are two further things in the filing worth knowing. First, Goldman is handing back essentially every dollar it earns — $4 billion of share buybacks and $1.4 billion of dividends in one quarter — and it is buying its own shares at three times their book value. That flatters profit per share and it means the underlying book value per share barely grew: from $335.49 to $336.61 in six months, or 0.3%. Second, the regulatory capital ratio fell from 14.3% to 12.9% over the same six months because the risk on the balance sheet grew faster than the capital, and the company says it aims to keep only a small cushion above the minimum.

The compensation is real. The dividend has just been raised from $4.50 to $5.00 a quarter, and between that and the buybacks shareholders are receiving about 7% a year in cash — the most of any company in this group.

We think the shares are worth about $1,090 against $1,052.98. Analysts on average say $1,202, but notably they are split: 22 say buy, 30 say hold and 3 say sell — the only company in this group where the average rating is "hold". We agree with them.

  • Downside Risk 8/10. Earning far above its own stated normal, priced as though it will not stop.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. Genuinely superb execution, almost all of it a market cycle.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. A transaction business. Nothing here compounds by design.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $1,090 against a current price of $1,052.98 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Now the two facts that matter most and that no ratio in the vendor file expresses.

First: tangible book value per common share grew 0.3% in six months. From $335.49 to $336.61. In the same six months Goldman earned $11,802 million applicable to common shareholders. Common shareholders' equity actually FELL, from $109,819M to $109,714M. The reason is disclosed:

> "During the second quarter of 2026, we returned a total of $5.36 billion of capital to common shareholders, including $4.00 billion of common share repurchases and $1.36 billion of common stock dividends."

Goldman is returning approximately one hundred percent of its earnings, and it is repurchasing stock at above three times tangible book value. That is accretive to earnings per share and dilutive to tangible book value per share — and it means the return to a shareholder here is dividend plus buyback plus whatever the multiple does, with essentially no compounding of the underlying asset. By contrast Morgan Stanley, in the same six months and in the same batch, grew tangible book value per share 6.4%.

Single biggest risk: return on tangible common equity reverting to the company's own published target. The June quarter delivered 25.5%. The 10-Q states the through-the-cycle target is 15% to 17%. On the standard bank framework — the same one the knowledge base's named speaker described in 2023 — a 16% return at a 10% cost of equity and 4% growth gives 2.0x tangible book, which on the filed $336.61 is approximately $673 a share, 36% below spot. We do not forecast that outcome and we do not need to: the point is that the gap between what the firm earned and what the firm says it expects to earn is 850 basis points, and the entire premium in the price sits inside that gap.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that a 6.99% shareholder yield funded by a buyback at 3.1x tangible book can continue. Goldman returned $5.36 billion in the June quarter while its Common Equity Tier 1 ratio fell 140 basis points to 12.9%, against a stated policy of maintaining only "a 50 to 100 basis point buffer above our capital requirement." Risk-weighted assets grew 8.7% in six months and capital fell 2.5%. If the balance sheet keeps growing at that rate, either the buyback slows or the buffer disappears. And because the buyback is 5.09 of the 6.99 percentage points of shareholder yield — and because the yield is essentially the entire expected return in Section 4b — a buyback cut is not a capital-allocation footnote here; it is most of the investment case.
