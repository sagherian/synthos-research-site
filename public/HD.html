The Overview

Home Depot sells the materials and tools people use to fix and improve their homes, out of 2,359 large stores in the United States, Canada and Mexico, plus a growing network of more than 1,250 specialist supply depots serving roofers, drywall installers, landscapers and pool contractors.

Its problem is simple and it is not about the company's competence. Fewer people are walking in.

The company publishes a figure for how much its customer count is changing at stores it has owned for more than a year. That number has been negative in every single period it has reported: down 1.0% for the last full year, down 1.6% in the autumn quarter, down 1.3% in the most recent quarter. Sales have looked roughly flat only because the average amount each customer spends has risen — partly prices, partly a change in what people buy.

The reason is interest rates. Home Depot's own annual report blames "a persisting high interest rate environment" and "housing affordability pressure." When mortgages are expensive, people do not move house, and moving house is what triggers big renovation projects. People still buy paint and light bulbs; they do not replace the kitchen.

So the company has been buying growth instead. It paid roughly $17.6 billion for a roofing and building-products distributor in 2024, and that business then bought a drywall and ceilings distributor for about $5.5 billion in September 2025. Together those acquisitions supplied about $6.3 billion of extra sales last year — more than the company's entire reported sales increase. In other words, without the deals, sales went nowhere.

The deals were paid for with debt, and the debt has changed how the company behaves. Home Depot stopped buying back its own shares in March 2024 and has not restarted; the annual report says flatly that it does not plan to restart this year "as we seek to reduce our outstanding debt." The dividend, which the company has raised every year for decades, went up just 1.3% in February. Dividends now eat about 72% of the cash the business generates after investment, up from 47% two years ago.

Profits have fallen three years in a row: $15.11 a share, then $14.91, then $14.23.

And yet none of this makes the business bad. It still earns a 25.7% return on the capital it employs, generates $12.6 billion of surplus cash a year, and pays a 2.68% dividend. If interest rates fall and people start moving house again, the profit margin has real room to recover — it was 15.3% four years ago and is 12.7% now, and that gap is worth more than $3 a share.

Today the shares rose 2.4% and stopped almost exactly at their one-year average price. That average is a level traders watch closely, and the stock failed to clear it, on lighter-than-usual volume. The next earnings report is in fourteen days.

Our estimate of fair value is $362 against a price of $348.24 — about 4% higher, or roughly 7% including the dividend. That is a fair price, not a bargain and not a trap. Hold it if you own it. Do not rush.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

2. The transactions problem — what comparable sales actually say

This is the analytical centre of the name, and it is entirely a filings finding.

Home Depot discloses three linked figures every period: comparable sales, comparable average ticket, and comparable customer transactions. The definitions are the company's own: "Average ticket represents the average price paid per transaction", and comparable sales "includes sales at locations, physical and online, open greater than 52 weeks." Crucially, "Customer transactions and average ticket measures do not include results from HD Supply or SRS (including GMS)" — so these are clean readings of the core retail business, uncontaminated by the acquisitions.

The apparent stability of comparable sales is entirely a ticket effect, and in the most recent quarter it was not even that: "Foreign exchange rates positively impacted comparable sales by approximately 55 basis points for the first quarter of fiscal 2026." Strip the currency and first-quarter comparable sales were approximately +0.05% — statistically indistinguishable from zero.

The company's own explanation is consistent and it is macroeconomic, not competitive. From the 10-K: "Our comparable sales results reflect customer engagement with smaller home improvement projects, which was offset by the impact of continued macroeconomic uncertainties and other macroeconomic factors, including a persisting high interest rate environment, that continue to pressure broader home improvement demand." From the first-quarter 10-Q, with a subtly different emphasis: "customer engagement with smaller home improvement projects, despite the impact of consumer uncertainty and housing affordability pressure on home improvement demand." From the third-quarter 10-Q: "a persisting high interest rate environment pressuring large home improvement project demand."

Three observations that follow, and they carry the verdict.

First, this is a large-project recession inside a stable small-project business. Every filing draws the same distinction: small projects are engaging customers, large ones are not. Large projects are what generate the highest tickets and the Pro relationships, and they are the ones gated by mortgage rates and home turnover. That makes the shortfall cyclical in character — which is the bull case — but it also means it is entirely outside the company's control.

Second, ticket growth of 1.4-2.2% against transaction declines of 1.0-1.6% is a pattern our knowledge base flagged as a warning sign four years ago. A tracked claim from 2022-02-27 observed "rising tickets with falling volumes (Home Depot +12.5%/−5%) show demand destruction at work." The magnitudes have compressed dramatically — from +12.5%/−5% to +2.2%/−1.3% — but the sign of both terms is unchanged after four years. That is the single most useful thing in an otherwise thin conviction lane, and we do not think it is being priced.

Third, the third-quarter disclosure contains an under-noticed detail about the fourth quarter's comparison base. The 10-Q attributes part of the third-quarter weakness to "the lack of severe weather events occurring during the third quarter of fiscal 2025, while lapping hurricane-related demand in the third quarter of fiscal 2024" and to "the calendar shift which resulted in the third quarter of fiscal 2025 including one less week of summer and one additional week of fall." Weather and calendar are legitimate explanations for a single quarter and they are not available as explanations for the full-year figure or for the first quarter of fiscal 2026. The underlying transaction decline persists once they are stripped out.

Wrong one — the vendor uses two different debt definitions in two different fields. enterpriseValueTTM is reported as $403.610B. Wrong two — and this is the one the filing settles: the vendor's totalDebt captures finance leases but not operating leases, and Home Depot is a heavy lessee. The 10-K states:

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over 12-24 months decomposes as: adjusted EPS growth (+1.7% this fiscal year, +7.5% next on consensus) + multiple drift + shareholder yield (2.68% dividend; the buyback contributes nothing and the share count is rising, so this is the whole yield).

Our base case assumes the multiple broadly HOLDS with a small compression, and we state the size. Today the market pays 23.30x the year-ahead estimate ($14.944 for the fiscal year ending January 2027). Our base of $362 pays 22.53x the year-ahead estimate at that future date ($16.070 for January 2028) — approximately 3.3% of multiple compression. The arithmetic reconciles: +7.5% earnings growth − 3.3% multiple compression = +3.9%, which is our base fair value against spot, plus 2.68% of dividend for a total of roughly 6.6%.

Why a hold-with-slight-compression rather than expansion. Four reasons, all specific. (a) The share count is rising and the buyback is explicitly off for the current fiscal year — a multiple that historically rested partly on shrinking share count loses that support. (b) Dividend growth of 1.3% signals a payout at 72% of free cash flow with limited headroom. (c) Net debt of 2.64x EBITDA with negative tangible equity is a materially different balance sheet from the one the market has historically capitalised. (d) The street's own distribution — 21 hold and 4 sell ratings, the highest sell count in this batch — is inconsistent with multiple expansion.

Why not more compression in the base.   • Gross margin below 32.5% as the GMS mix fully annualises — the trajectory is 33.4% (FY2024), 33.3% (FY2025), 32.6% (Q4 FY2025), 33.0% (Q1 FY2026).

Single biggest risk: that the decline in customer transactions is structural rather than cyclical. Home Depot has now reported falling comparable customer counts in every period it has disclosed, and our knowledge base contains an observation from 2022 describing the identical pattern — rising tickets against falling volumes — as demand destruction. Four years and a full rate cycle later, the sign of both terms is unchanged. If the correct reading is that households have permanently reduced the frequency of home-improvement purchases, then the entire consensus recovery path from $14.944 to $17.481 is wrong, the acquisitions were a way of hiding it, and the 2.64x leverage taken on to fund them becomes the problem rather than the solution.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that adjusted EPS troughs this fiscal year at $14.944 and re-accelerates to $16.070. That path requires the three-year run of declining earnings to end within twelve months, on a business whose comparable sales excluding currency are approximately zero, whose gross margin is still absorbing an acquisition that only entered the results in September 2025, and whose share count is growing rather than shrinking. Consensus for the year in progress already implies only 1.7% growth. There is no margin for error in that number, and at 23.3x it is what the price is paying for.
