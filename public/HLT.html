The Overview

Hilton does not own many hotels. It licenses its brands — Hilton, Hampton, DoubleTree, Waldorf Astoria and a dozen more — to people who do own them, and takes a fee on every room sold. That is a very good business: it spent just $185 million on physical assets last year against $12 billion of revenue.

The shares have fallen. They are 10.9% below their high, 6.2% below their average price of the last fifty days, and on the standard momentum measure (RSI 31.5) they are the most "oversold" of the twelve companies in this batch. Over the past year they have returned 19.7% while the market returned 24.3%.

That is odd, because the results keep beating expectations — five quarters in a row. Revenue rose 6.5% last quarter and operating profit 10.3%.

The filings suggest where the problem is: geography.

Hotel companies measure themselves by RevPAR — revenue per available room. Across Hilton's whole system it rose 3.9%. But underneath:

  • United States: +5.4% — helped, the company says, by business travel, group bookings, and "leisure aided by the World Cup".
  • Europe: +4.3% — helped partly by the Winter Olympics.
  • Asia Pacific: +1.2% — held back by China, where the company cites "continued government restrictions" on group travel.
  • Middle East and Africa: MINUS 29.5% — "as a result of the ongoing geopolitical conflict in the Middle East".

So two of five regions are struggling, and the two that are doing well were helped by a World Cup and a Winter Olympics — events that do not repeat next year.

There is a second thing to understand, and it is about the balance sheet. Hilton has been buying back its own shares very aggressively: $3.25 billion last year, $2.9 billion the year before, $2.3 billion before that. Its free cash flow in those years was $1.9 billion, $1.8 billion and $1.7 billion. It has spent more on buying back stock than it generated in cash, every year for three years, and borrowed the difference — including a fresh $1 billion bond issued in May.

The result is that Hilton's balance sheet shows negative shareholders' equity of $5.4 billion. That is not a sign of distress — it is the arithmetic of buying back more stock than you have accumulated profits — but it does mean the company carries about 4.2 times its annual profits in net debt, and that roughly half its earnings-per-share growth comes from having fewer shares rather than from selling more rooms.

Finally, our knowledge base has a specific view about Hilton, and it is not a compliment. A claim from 2023 argues that Airbnb "doesn't compete with economy/mid-scale same-day bookings but threatens upper-scale/luxury players like Marriott and Hilton" — that is, the part of the market Hilton sits in. A separate claim from 2025 says Airbnb created "effectively infinite lodging capacity". Neither is a forecast, and both are worth knowing.

The shares cost $312.07. Analysts expect $10.40 of earnings in 2027, so you are paying about 30 times. Our estimate of fair value is $330 — about 6% above the price.

That is not enough to act on, but the chart is oversold enough that it is worth watching. We would want either system-wide RevPAR above 5%, or a price below $280.

  • Downside Risk 7/10. An excellent model on a balance sheet with minus $5.4 billion of equity.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. 3.9% RevPAR levered to 15% earnings growth by buyback.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. Genuinely capital-light and genuinely bounded.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $330 against a current price of $312.07 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

Three readings.

First, the headline 3.9% is a blended number covering a wide dispersion. A 29.5% decline in one region and a 1.2% increase in another, against 5.4% in the largest, is not a uniform business. Middle East and Africa is small relative to the system, but a decline of that magnitude does not reverse quickly and the company attributes it to a conflict with no stated end date. This is the second name in this batch materially affected by the same Middle East conflict — SLB's Middle East & Asia revenue fell 14% year on year for the same reason — which is a genuine cross-holding correlation worth recording.

Second, the two strong regions were helped by events that do not repeat. The World Cup in the United States and the Winter Olympics in Europe are both named by management as contributors. That does not make the growth fake — the company also cites business, group and inflation-driven average daily rate — but it does mean the 2027 comparison for those two regions starts against a flattered base, and the FY2027 consensus of 15.1% earnings growth is struck against it.

Third, the quality of the growth is average daily rate rather than occupancy. Management attributes system-wide RevPAR growth "primarily due to improvements in system-wide ADR, which included the impact of inflation" — so a material part of the 3.9% is price keeping pace with costs rather than more rooms sold.

Buybacks have exceeded free cash flow in each of the last three years, by a widening margin, and the gap is funded with debt. FY2025's $3,254 million of repurchases is 4.63% of the current market capitalisation and 167% of that year's free cash flow. Weighted diluted shares have fallen from 264.0 million in FY2023 to 229.0 million in the June 2026 quarter — a 13.3% reduction in under three years — and shareholders' equity has fallen from a deficit of $3,727 million to a deficit of $5,388 million.

The corporate action confirming the funding, and it is absent from the payload. From the 8-K filed 2026-05-11:

> "On May 11, 2026, Hilton Domestic Operating Company Inc. ... issued and sold $1 billion aggregate principal amount of 5.500% Senior Notes due 2031... The net proceeds of the offering of the Notes were used to repay $450 million of borrowings under the Issuer's senior secured revolving credit facility, and the remainder for general corporate purposes."

$1 billion of new unsecured debt at 5.500%, of which $550 million went to "general corporate purposes" — which for a company repurchasing $3.25 billion of stock a year is, in practical terms, the buyback. Interest expense in the June quarter was $183 million against $151 million a year earlier, up 21.2%, which is the cost of that structure showing up in the income statement.

Two things must be said about this and they point in opposite directions.

It is not distress and the negative equity is not a warning. An asset-light franchisor with no hotels on its balance sheet, generating $1.9 billion of free cash flow on $185 million of capital expenditure, can carry leverage that a hotel owner could not. Net debt of roughly 4.2 times EBITDA is high but not unusual for this model, and there is no covenant issue disclosed anywhere in the archive.

But it means roughly half the earnings growth is not operating growth. Consensus wants 15.1% adjusted EPS growth in FY2027 on 8.5% revenue growth. A 4.2% annual reduction in the share count supplies a large part of that gap. A reader paying 30 times forward earnings should know that the growth being multiplied is part RevPAR, part net unit growth and part financial engineering — and that the third component requires continued access to debt markets at reasonable rates.

3a. What today's price assumes (the inversion)

At $312.07 — 34.5x FY2026 consensus and 30.0x FY2027 — the price embeds:

  • Adjusted EPS of $9.039 in FY2026, up 12.0%. (Consensus, 14 analysts.) The first half delivered $4.30 ($2.01 plus $2.29), leaving $4.74 — and the September consensus of $2.38 implies a December quarter of $2.36, against $2.08 a year earlier. Achievable.
  • Adjusted EPS of $10.402 in FY2027, up 15.1%, on revenue growth of 8.5%. (Consensus, 15 analysts.) The 6.6-point gap between revenue and earnings growth is buyback and operating leverage, and the buyback component requires roughly $3 billion a year of continued repurchase against $1.9 billion of free cash flow.
  • System-wide RevPAR growth continuing near 4%. (Our derivation; the filing supplies +3.9%.) This is the most fragile assumption in the price, and it is fragile in a specific way: the two strongest regions were helped by a World Cup and a Winter Olympics, and two of five regions are currently impaired.
  • The market keeps paying 28-32x forward earnings. (Our number.) At 26x FY2027E the stock is $270; at 34x it is $354. Hilton has generally traded in the high twenties to mid thirties, so the current multiple is mid-range for this business — which is why the base sits close to the price.
  • Continued access to debt markets at reasonable rates. (Our number.) Shareholders' equity is MINUS $5,388 million and net debt is roughly 4.2x EBITDA. The May 2026 issue priced at 5.500%. If that cost rises materially, the buyback that supplies a large part of the earnings growth becomes more expensive.

3b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: adjusted EPS growth (+15.1%, from FY2026E $9.039 to FY2027E $10.402) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 30.0x toward roughly 29.0x) + shareholder yield (+4.8% — a 0.19% dividend plus roughly 4.63% of buyback).

Our base assumes very slight multiple compression and the reason is the composition of the growth rather than its rate. A company growing earnings 15% on revenue growth of 8.5%, with the difference supplied by debt-funded buyback against negative equity, should not carry the same multiple as one growing 15% organically. That said, 30x is mid-range for Hilton's own history and we are not forecasting a de-rating — only declining to underwrite an expansion.

Note the shape: roughly three quarters of the expected return is earnings growth and one quarter is capital returned, and of the earnings growth roughly a third is itself the buyback. Strip out the financial engineering and the underlying business is compounding at high single digits, which at 30x is a full price.
