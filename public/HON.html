The Overview

Honeywell used to be a conglomerate: aeroplane parts, chemicals, building controls, factory automation. It has now broken itself up.

On 29 June it handed its aerospace business to shareholders as a separate company called Honeywell Aerospace, giving one Aerospace share for every two Honeywell shares. One minute later it combined every two remaining Honeywell shares into one — a reverse split. It had already separated its advanced materials business.

Separately, a quantum-computing company Honeywell had built, called Quantinuum, went public. Honeywell no longer controls it, so accounting rules made it recognise a $6.6 billion paper gain. It still owns a large stake, worth $7,525 million at the listing — about a tenth of Honeywell's entire stock-market value.

Here is the practical problem for anyone trying to value this. The most recent set of accounts, published on 23 July, still includes the aerospace business. The first accounts describing the company that actually exists today will be published on 22 October. Until then nobody outside the company knows how much debt it carries, what dividend it will pay, or how much cash it generates.

We can, however, work out what the surviving business earns, because the accounts report each division separately. In the three months to June the three remaining divisions sold $5,182 million of products and services and made $1,171 million of divisional profit — a 22.6% margin, which is very good. Sales grew 3.4%.

But look at why sales grew. Volumes were flat — zero, and slightly negative over six months. All the growth was price increases put through to cover inflation. And one of the three divisions, industrial automation, is shrinking.

At $248.79 the shares cost about 30 times this year's expected earnings and 25 times next year's. The stock closed at its highest price of the past year on the day this was written, and its momentum indicator is in overbought territory.

Our estimate of fair value is $241, about 3% below the price. Analysts average $264.50.

We are marking this "Watch" rather than "Hold" for one reason: on 22 October the numbers that would let anyone properly value this company arrive. Buying before they do is buying something you cannot see.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
