The Overview

Robinhood is the app that made stock trading free and easy, and it is now much more than that: it also runs a large crypto business, owns a global crypto exchange (Bitstamp), custodies money for financial advisers, offers retirement accounts and a credit card, and is building prediction markets and its own blockchain.

The most recent quarter, reported six days ago, was good. Revenue grew 32%. Trading revenue grew 44%.

But there is an important catch in the headline. The company reported earnings of $0.62 per share against an expectation of $0.44 — a 41% beat, which is enormous. Reading the actual filing shows that $129 million of that profit came from a one-off accounting gain when Robinhood spun out and stopped consolidating an investment fund it had launched. Take that away and the quarter earned about $0.50 — still a beat, but 14% rather than 41%. That distinction matters and almost nobody makes it.

Something unusual: our knowledge base of expert claims contains 81 separate opinions about this company from 18 different independent sources, more than any other company we have researched. Fifty-four are positive. They are not vague. One source published five detailed claims in March arguing that Robinhood is positioning itself to be the default home for the $70-90 trillion of wealth that will pass from older to younger Americans. Another said in June, "I'm not selling Robinhood for 10 years." A third said in July that if you own Ethereum you should sell it and buy Robinhood shares instead, because Robinhood collects about 90% of the fees while the blockchain itself collects under 1%.

The bears are also good and we quote them: one argues that self-custody crypto wallets will eventually make brokers like Robinhood unnecessary; another calls it "a transactional casino-style business."

The shares cost $93.51 — 39% below their high of the past year, and at a level that technical measures classify as oversold. Curiously, the lowest price target published by any Wall Street analyst is $100, which is above the current price.

Our own estimate of fair value is $110, about 18% above the price. In a bad case the shares fall to about $61; in a good case they reach about $150. That is 1.74 dollars of possible gain per dollar of possible loss. Our bar for recommending a purchase is two to one. So: Watch — with a specific price, $85, written down at which it would become a Buy.

Two data notes. Robinhood's shares come in two classes and the market value figure correctly counts both — we checked, and it is right, which is not always the case. But the rest of the vendor's file for this company is the worst in this batch: it reports the June quarter's revenue as $535 million when the actual filing says $1,308 million, and shows a depreciation charge of minus $23 million, which is not a thing that exists.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
