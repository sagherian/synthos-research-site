The Overview

Hewlett Packard Enterprise builds the machines that go inside data centres — the computers, the storage, and now the networking gear that connects them.

For most of the last decade it was a dull business. It bought chips from other companies, put them in metal boxes, and sold them at a modest markup. Then two things happened.

First, artificial intelligence created enormous demand for those boxes. Companies building AI systems need racks of computers, and someone has to assemble and sell them.

Second, in July 2025 HPE bought Juniper Networks for $13.6 billion. Juniper makes networking equipment. That matters because networking is a much better business than assembling servers: last quarter HPE's networking division kept 21.6 cents of every dollar of revenue as operating profit, while the server division kept 12.4 cents.

The results have been dramatic. In the three months to the end of April, revenue was up 40% on the year before. Networking revenue was up 148%. Profit from the operating divisions more than doubled. And the company went from burning $1.7 billion of cash in the first half of last year to generating $1.6 billion in the first half of this one.

The share price has responded — enormously. The shares are up 164% in a year and 82% in the last three months alone. They rose another 4.3% on the day this report was written. At $52.39 the stock trades 76% above its own average price over the past 200 days.

Is that too much? On the numbers analysts use, no: you pay about 13 times what the company is expected to earn next year, which is cheap for a company growing this fast. But three things deserve stating plainly.

One. Those "earnings" exclude some large real costs — mainly the write-off of what HPE paid for Juniper, and shares given to employees. On strict accounting rules, last quarter's profit was about 45 cents a share, not 79 cents. The gap is 77%.

Two. In September 2024 HPE raised $1.5 billion by selling a special kind of preferred share. Those shares automatically turn into ordinary shares on 1 September 2027 — at least 76 million of them, about 5.75% more shares than exist today. Everyone's slice gets smaller. Our data file records this as zero.

Three. For analysts' full-year forecast to be right, HPE has to sell about $25 billion in the next six months after selling $20 billion in the last six. That is a 25% jump, and it has not happened yet. We find out on 2 September, four weeks from now.

Our estimate of fair value is $60, about 14% above the price. Analysts on average say $69.27 — and remarkably, the lowest analyst target, $62, is already above today's price.

Our conclusion is to wait for the 2 September results rather than buy a stock that has risen 82% in three months. That is not a judgment that the business is bad. It is a judgment about when to act.

1. What the business is now, and the acquisition that changed it

Hewlett Packard Enterprise is an enterprise technology company headquartered in Spring, Texas, with 67,000 employees, run by president and chief executive Antonio Fabio Neri. Its fiscal year ends 31 October.

The segment structure changed on 2025-11-01, and the vendor payload still reports the old one

From the 10-Q, verbatim:

> "Effective November 1, 2025, HPE implemented an organizational change by (i) merging the Server, Hybrid Cloud, and Financial Services business segments into a new segment named Cloud & Artificial Intelligence ("AI") and (ii) transferring the Telco and Instant On businesses from the Networking segment to the Corporate Investments and Other segment. As a result, the Company's organizational structure consists of the following segments: (i) Cloud & AI; (ii) Networking; and (iii) Corporate Investments and Other. The Company has reflected these changes to its segment information retrospectively."

The vendor's seg_prod for FY2025 reports the OLD five-segment structure — Server Segment, Hybrid Cloud, Networking, Financial Services, Corporate Investments. It is not wrong for the year it labels, but it cannot be reconciled to anything the company now reports, and any comparison built across it will be comparing different things. Four things to take from these two tables, in order of importance.

First, the margin arithmetic is the whole investment case. Networking earns 21.6% and Cloud & AI 12.4%. Networking is 25.2% of quarterly revenue but 38.1% of segment earnings. Every point of revenue mix that moves from Cloud & AI to Networking is worth roughly nine points of incremental operating margin on that dollar. Second, the Networking growth is acquisition, and it should not be read as organic. Juniper closed 2025-07-02, so the year-ago quarter contains none of it. A 148% increase against a base that excludes a $13.6 billion acquisition is arithmetic, not momentum. The first genuinely comparable quarter will be the one reporting on 2026-09-02, which includes Juniper on both sides.

Third, the organic engine grew 9.8% in the half. Cloud & AI — the old Server, Hybrid Cloud and Financial Services businesses, still 70.3% of quarterly revenue — rose 22.9% in the quarter but only 9.8% across the six months. That is respectable and it is not the 40% headline.

Fourth, and genuinely good: Cloud & AI margin nearly doubled. From 6.6% to 12.4% in the quarter and 7.5% to 11.4% in the half. The server business became roughly twice as profitable per dollar of revenue in twelve months, which is what pricing power in a supply-constrained AI-systems market looks like. It is also 11-12%, which is what assembling other people's silicon pays.
