The Overview

Every jet engine contains a set of blades that spin inside a chamber hotter than the melting point of the metal they are made from. Making those blades — as single crystals of nickel alloy, hollow, with internal cooling passages — is one of the hardest routine manufacturing tasks in the world. Howmet is one of a very small number of companies that can do it, and it also makes the specialised bolts that hold aircraft together and the forged aluminium wheels on heavy trucks.

Business is very good. In the three months to March, sales of engine parts rose 29%, and the profit margin on that division rose from about 33 cents in the dollar to 36.6 cents. Total company sales rose 19%. The company has beaten analysts' profit forecasts five quarters in a row.

The reason this business is attractive over decades rather than years is worth understanding. Engine makers sell the engine itself at roughly break-even and make their money over the next twenty or thirty years selling replacement parts — which, according to an industry analysis in our research base, are sold to airlines at three to four times the price charged to the aircraft manufacturer, at around 60% gross margins, and are worth three to five times the original engine over its life. Howmet supplies parts into both halves of that. Every engine it helps build today is an annuity for decades.

So the company is excellent. Here is the problem.

The shares cost $288.20, which is within half a percent of the highest price they have ever reached, after rising 56% in a year while the wider market rose 24%. At that price you are paying:

  • about 70 times last year's profits,
  • about 47 times what analysts expect for 2027,
  • 13.6 times annual sales,
  • and 80 times the spare cash the company actually generated last year — which is another way of saying the cash return on the price you pay is 1.24% a year.

The last figure is the most telling. Howmet is spending heavily to build new factory capacity — capital spending is more than twice its depreciation charge — which is exactly the right thing to do when demand is strong, but it means very little cash comes back to shareholders while it is happening.

Even the analysts think the run is nearly over: their average target is $299.18, only 3.8% above the price, the smallest gap of any company in this batch. Our own estimate is $287 — slightly below the current price.
