The Overview

Interactive Brokers is a stockbroker, but an unusual one. It employs 3,182 people — a few thousand — and looks after $203 billion of assets. Almost everything it does is done by software written in-house over nearly fifty years. That is why 77 cents of every dollar of revenue becomes pre-tax profit, which is a margin almost no business of any kind achieves.

It makes money two ways. It charges a small commission when customers trade — that was $673 million last quarter, up 30%. And it earns interest on the cash customers leave with it and on the money it lends them to buy shares — that was $1,057 million, up 23%. Everything else is small.

Last quarter every line grew at once, revenue rose 28% and profit per share rose 35%.

Two things about the published data are badly wrong and both would mislead anyone using a screen.

The revenue figure is roughly 70% too big. The data file reports $10.2 billion for last year. That counts all the interest the company receives without subtracting the interest it pays out. The company's own figure — what analysts forecast and what the company reports — is $6.1 billion.

The company is not what it appears. The shares you buy, "Interactive Brokers Group, Inc.", are a holding company that owns just 26.3% of the actual business. The other 73.7% belongs to the founder, Thomas Peterffy, together with management and employees, through a private vehicle. Last quarter the group made $1,338 million; only $312 million of that belonged to public shareholders. The arithmetic still works — the share price and the earnings per share are both measured on the same 26.3% slice — but the market-capitalisation number in the data file is measured on the whole thing, and mixing the two produces nonsense in any ratio except the price-to-earnings ratio.

The shares are $88.63, down 9% from their high, and by one standard technical measure they are oversold — the only stock in this group of twelve that is. Over the past year they are up 39% against the market's 24%.

Our estimate of fair value is $99, about 12% higher. The average analyst says $108, and — unusually — the lowest of the 19 analysts covering it says $98, which is above today's price. A member of the board has bought a small parcel of shares in the open market in each of the last three months; the most recent was filed on the day of this report.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Over-capitalised and extraordinarily profitable — but more than half the revenue is interest income, and public shareholders own a quarter of the business and none of the votes.
  • Growth Quality 9/10. Every revenue line growing simultaneously, costs growing at half the rate of revenue.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. A genuine compounding machine with prediction markets and crypto as options — though the founder himself calls the prediction-markets business a loss-leader today.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $99 against a current price of $88.63 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Three observations.

First, the cost base is almost fixed and almost trivially small. $440 million of quarterly expense supports $1,896 million of net revenue. Employee compensation of $182 million for 3,182 people is roughly $229,000 a head annualised — competitive pay for a technology firm — and it grew 12% against 28.1% revenue growth. Second, the one line that grew faster than everything else is execution, clearing and distribution fees, up 22%, and the release explains why: "driven by a $19 million increase in regulatory fees, as the SEC Section 31 transaction fee rate increased on April 4, 2026; partially offset by greater capture of liquidity rebates from certain exchanges due to higher trading volumes." A regulatory pass-through, not a cost problem.

Third, and this is the only genuinely negative number in the release: customer bad debt expense rose from $1 million to $10 million year on year, and from $2 million to $11 million for the half. In absolute terms it is 0.5% of quarterly net revenues and irrelevant.
