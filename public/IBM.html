The Overview

IBM sells three things to large organisations: software that lets them run their systems across their own computers and other people's clouds at the same time; consultants who install and run those systems; and the big computers themselves, including the mainframes that still process a large share of the world's bank transactions and airline bookings.

The software half is the good half. It grew 5% last quarter and keeps about 32 cents of profit on every dollar. It includes Red Hat, which IBM bought in 2019, and three more recent purchases, the largest of which — a data-streaming company called Confluent — cost about $11.5 billion in cash in March this year.

The problem is everything else. Last quarter, the consulting business grew by two-tenths of one percent. The mainframe line fell 42%, because these machines sell in waves and this wave has passed. Overall the company grew about 1%, which was less than analysts expected, and IBM's own finance chief said the company hit "revenue headwinds late in the second quarter."

That is why the shares, at $235.15, are 29% below where they were a year ago at their best, and why they have fallen 25% in six months while the market rose 11%. In the last two weeks they have bounced about 14% off the bottom, including a 3.9% rise today.

Two things you should know that the standard data does not tell you. First, the widely-reported debt figure for IBM is about $5.9 billion too high, because it treats office and equipment leases as borrowings and then counts part of them twice. The company's own filing says total debt is $62.0 billion. Second, IBM's 2025 profit was flattered: it actually paid negative tax that year — a $242 million refund rather than a charge — and about $1.3 billion of the fourth-quarter profit came from that one item. It has not repeated in 2026.

The shares cost about 19 times what analysts expect the company to earn this year. Our estimate of value is $250. The average analyst says $256. Those are close enough to each other, and to the price, that there is not much of an argument here either way — which is itself the answer.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Huge cash generation and a very low share-price volatility — offset by real debt at 3.1x cash earnings, and a balance sheet that is 58% goodwill and intangibles.
  • Growth Quality 4/10. One percent revenue growth last quarter. The software part is fine; the rest is not.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. Quantum computing and enterprise AI are genuine possibilities. IBM's record of converting such possibilities into revenue is poor.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $250 against a current price of $235.15 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Correction 1 — the vendor's total debt is $5,894M too high, and the mechanism is exact. The 10-Q states plainly: "Total debt of $61,987 million at June 30, 2026 increased $727 million compared to" December, and the debt table gives $61,260M at 2025-12-31. The vendor reports $67,154M. The difference decomposes without a remainder:

> Vendor shortTermDebt $7,224M = filing short-term debt $6,424M + current operating lease liability $800M

> Vendor longTermDebt $57,383M = filing long-term debt $54,836M + non-current operating lease liability $2,547M

> Vendor capitalLeaseObligations $2,547M = the same non-current operating lease liability, counted a second time

> Vendor totalDebt = $7,224M + $57,383M + $2,547M = $67,154M

> Overstatement = $800M + $2,547M + $2,547M = $5,894M

Treating operating leases as debt is a defensible convention. Counting $2,547M of them twice is not. The vendor reports total debt of $67,154M; the 10-Q shows $61,260M at the same date and $61,987M at 2026-06-30; we use $61,987M.

Correction 2 — netDebt omits marketable securities, and is also seven months stale. The vendor's netDebt of $53,513M is totalDebt less cash and equivalents only, ignoring $830M of marketable securities. Corrected to the same date on the filing's debt figure, net debt at 2025-12-31 was $61,260M − $14,471M = $46,789M — the vendor overstates it by $6,724M, or 14.4%. The current figure at 2026-06-30 is $61,987M − $8,178M = $53,809M. So the vendor's number happens to be within 0.6% of the right answer, for the wrong date, by two offsetting errors. We state this plainly because a reader checking only the headline would conclude the field was fine.

5b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Our base assumes mild compression and the reason is growth, not risk. Today the market pays 19.1x on the next year. If in two years it pays 17.5x on the then-next year, that is a genuine de-rating of about 8%.
