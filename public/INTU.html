The Overview

Intuit sells TurboTax, which prepares tax returns; QuickBooks, which does small-business bookkeeping; Credit Karma, which shows people their credit scores and sells them loans; and Mailchimp, which sends marketing emails. It is one of the most profitable software companies in America — 81 cents of every revenue dollar is gross profit, and it generated $7.6 billion of spare cash last year.

Twelve months ago the shares traded at $785. Today they trade at $324. The company has lost nearly three-fifths of its value while the stock market rose 24%.

Nothing in the accounts explains this. Revenue for the first nine months of the current financial year was up 14%. The most recent quarter beat what analysts expected.

What has happened is that investors have decided artificial intelligence will do this work instead. If a machine can prepare your tax return or keep your books for almost nothing, then a subscription to software that does the same thing is worth much less than it used to be. That is not a silly idea, and it has taken down the whole business-software sector, not just Intuit.

Our knowledge base has both sides of the argument, from credible and recent sources.

Against Intuit, and it is the sharper claim: tax and accounting software companies "get gutted as AI does the same work at ~1% cost."

For Intuit: nobody actually leaves QuickBooks, because small-business owners log in to it every day — the software is not just the calculation, it is the place where the business lives. And TurboTax's real skill is not knowing the tax code (which is public) but knowing which questions to ask you.

Management, meanwhile, has done two very large things this year that the data feed does not know about. In May it announced it would cut about 17% of its staff — roughly 3,100 people — while revenue was still growing 14%. And it authorised another $8 billion of share buybacks, which together with what was left is about 11% of the entire company.

You now pay about 12 times what analysts expect Intuit to earn next year, for a business they think will grow profits about 15% a year, with an 8.6% cash yield.

We think fair value is around $383 against $324. But the honest answer is that this is not a normal valuation problem. Either the machine takes the business, in which case the shares are worth perhaps $150, or it does not, in which case they are worth $550 or more. There is no version of the future in which Intuit is worth exactly the average of those.

The company reports on 25 August and will give its first forecast for next year. That is when this gets decided, and we are waiting for it.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
