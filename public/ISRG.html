The Overview

When a surgeon performs keyhole surgery with a robot, the robot is almost certainly a da Vinci, made by Intuitive Surgical. The surgeon sits at a console a few feet away and controls instruments inside the patient through small incisions.

The important thing to understand about the company is that it does not really make its money selling robots. It makes its money on what happens afterwards. Roughly three-quarters of its revenue comes from the instruments consumed in each operation and from service contracts on machines already installed. Every hospital that buys a system becomes a recurring customer for a decade or more, and the more operations that hospital performs, the more Intuitive earns.

So the number that matters is how many operations are done on da Vinci systems worldwide. Last quarter it was about 15% more than a year earlier. That is a very good number. It is also lower than the year before, when it was about 17%, and in America specifically it slowed from 14% to 12%.

That slowdown is the whole story of the share price. A year ago investors were paying more than sixty times earnings for this company, on the assumption that growth would stay very high. When growth stepped down, the price they were willing to pay stepped down much further. The shares have fallen 38% from their high and are down 24% over twelve months, while the overall market rose 24%.

Meanwhile the company itself has done fine. Revenue rose 20.5% last year. Profits beat what analysts expected in each of the last five quarters, by between 10% and 21%. It has about $9 billion of cash and essentially no debt. It started buying back its own shares again.

The shares cost $368.265. Analysts expect about $12.08 of earnings per share in 2027, so you are paying about 30 times. For a business with these characteristics that is not expensive — but it is not obviously cheap either, and the average analyst target of $491 is a long way above where the stock actually trades, which usually means analysts are behind the market rather than ahead of it.

Our estimate of fair value is $399, about 8% above the price.

The problem is the trend. The shares are below both their three-month and their twelve-month average prices, and the twelve-month average is 23% above where they trade now. Falling stocks in this situation often keep falling for a while, and there is no company news due for eleven weeks.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. No debt at all and $9 billion of cash, against a share price in a well-established decline.
  • Growth Quality 8/10. Revenue up 20.5% and procedures up 15%, but every growth rate is lower than the year before.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. A genuine compounding installed base, with the international rollout barely started and competitors finally arriving.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $399 against a current price of $368.26 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

A surgeon trained on da Vinci, in a hospital that has built its theatre workflow around it, with a decade of outcome data behind those procedures, does not change platform because a competitor's list price is lower. That is why 75.4% of revenue recurs, and it is the single most durable feature of any business in this batch.

Three observations, and the third is the one that matters most.

First, the deceleration is uniform. Every geography slowed by two to three points. This is not a single market failing; it is a global growth rate stepping down.

Second, the United States driver has changed. Growth is now coming from general surgery — cholecystectomy, hernia repair, appendectomy — rather than from urology and gynaecology, which were the original franchises. That is what a maturing adoption curve looks like: the high-value, high-penetration procedures are done, and growth moves to higher-volume, lower-complexity ones. The bariatric decline, on 2% of procedures, is a small negative. And the company names a policy driver — the expiration of enhanced ACA premium subsidies — whose continuing effect it says "remains uncertain."

Third, and this is the point: the outside-United-States rate of approximately 20% is running on an installed base of just 136 da Vinci 5 systems. European certification arrived in July 2025 and Japanese clearance in June 2025. The 32.3% of revenue that grows at 20% is doing so with the current-generation platform barely deployed. If the international rollout accelerates, the blended rate stabilises without the United States improving at all.

Management's own full-year guidance — management voice, half-weighted, and sourced from the knowledge base because the earnings 8-Ks in this archive are cover documents only: worldwide da Vinci procedure growth of approximately 13.5% to 15.5% for 2026. The June quarter came in at approximately 15%, at the top of that range.
