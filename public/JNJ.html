The Overview

Johnson & Johnson makes prescription medicines and medical devices. Roughly two thirds of its sales — $60.4 billion of $94.2 billion last year — are drugs: cancer drugs, drugs for autoimmune diseases like psoriasis and Crohn's, psychiatric drugs, and drugs for a rare lung-pressure disease. The other third is surgical equipment, artificial hips and knees, heart devices and contact lenses.

The share price has risen 52% in the last year while the wider market rose 24%. That is a very large gap for a company whose sales grew only 6%. So the interesting question is not "how fast is it growing" — it is "why did the market suddenly decide it was worth more."

The answer is a lawsuit. For years, J&J has faced tens of thousands of claims from people who say its baby powder caused cancer. It tried three separate times to push the liability into bankruptcy shells, and all three attempts were thrown out. Roughly 76,000 people in the United States still have claims. That uncertainty — nobody knowing whether the final bill would be $5 billion or $50 billion — is why the shares traded cheaply for a decade.

On 28 July, the company told the market it had struck a deal. It has agreed terms with the lawyers leading the main federal case and the related state cases: J&J will commit $5.5 billion, will pay no more than $3 billion of it in 2027, and nothing more before 2028. The catch is that at least 95% of the remaining claimants have to sign up, and that has not happened yet.

That is the re-rating. It is real, and it is mostly done.

Now the parts that are less comfortable. The company only has $3.7 billion set aside for talc. The deal commits $5.5 billion. That difference has to show up as a charge, probably at the next results.

The day after the settlement, J&J spent money. It bought a biotech called Firefly Bio and signed a large collaboration with another called Sail. In its own filing it said these deals will cut its adjusted profit per share by about $0.64 this year and $1.36 next year. To put that in context, analysts currently expect about $12.87 of adjusted earnings per share next year — so $1.36 is more than a tenth of it. We cannot tell from our data whether analysts have taken this into account yet.

And today, a third filing: the executive who runs the entire drug business — nearly two thirds of the company — is retiring on 1 September.

Meanwhile the underlying business is fine but slow. Cancer drugs are growing fast, over 20% last year. Psychiatric drugs are growing. But the company's second-biggest product, Stelara, is losing its patent protection and being copied — and it was 6.5% of everything the company sells. The encouraging detail is that the damage is shrinking: Stelara knocked 6.2% off sales growth last year and only 4.6% in the most recent quarter.

The dividend is the reason many people own this. It has been increased every year for 63 consecutive years, and was raised again in April 2026 to $1.34 a quarter, which is $5.36 a year, or a 2.10% yield at today's price.

Our judgement: the good news has been announced and the shares have already gone up 52%. Analysts' single most pessimistic price target is $255, which is where the stock closed today. We think it is worth about $270 — about 6% more. That is not enough of a discount to buy aggressively after a run like this. Hold.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

12. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Hold.

A very good business, a genuinely improved risk profile, and a price that has already paid for both.

What has actually changed, and it is important. On 2026-07-28 Johnson & Johnson announced a comprehensive resolution of the ovarian talc litigation with the leadership of the federal MDL and the related state proceedings — a $5.5 billion commitment, a first payment of no more than $3 billion in 2027, no further payments before 2028, conditioned on at least 95% participation. For a company that had three Chapter 11 vehicles dismissed, reversed $7.0 billion of reserve in a single quarter, and still faces approximately 76,000 US plaintiffs, that is the removal of the item that has capped this multiple for a decade. It is the explanation for a 52.4% twelve-month return against a 24.3% index, and for a stock that trades at 19.8x forward when it spent most of the last decade in the mid-teens.
