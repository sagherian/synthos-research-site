The Overview

KKR is one of the world's biggest private-investment firms. It raises money from pension funds, insurers and increasingly from ordinary wealthy individuals, and invests it in companies, property, infrastructure and loans. It charges a management fee on the money it holds — $744 billion of it — and takes a share of the profits when investments are sold well.

Two things make KKR unusual among its peers. It owns an insurance company, Global Atlantic, which brings $219 billion of money that never has to be given back — permanent capital, which is far better than money that investors can withdraw. And it owns some businesses outright, collecting their dividends.

The shares have had a very bad year. They are down 24% while the American stock market rose 24% — a gap of nearly fifty percentage points — and at the worst point they had fallen 35% from their peak. The reason is not that KKR is doing badly. Profits per share, measured the way the company and analysts measure them, have grown, and the most recent quarterly result beat expectations by 14%.

The reason is fear about private credit — the business of lending money to companies outside the banking system. Several independent commentators in our research library believe this market is in a bubble, and one makes a specific and worrying observation: the same loan is valued at 82 cents by Blackstone, 70 cents by Apollo, and 91 cents by KKR. If that is typical, KKR is the most optimistic valuer in its industry, and has the furthest to fall if reality is less kind.

Against that fear sits the clearest signal in the file. In February and March, when the shares were between $88 and $103, both of KKR's chief executives and three of its directors bought about $23 million of the stock with their own money. Every one of those purchases was at a price below today's $108.21. People who see the loan marks bought.

At $108.21 the shares cost about 15 times what analysts expect the company to earn in 2027. That is cheap for a business of this quality. Our estimate of fair value is $125, about 15% above the price. The average analyst says $133.67.

Two honest warnings. First, KKR pays almost nothing back to shareholders — a 0.7% dividend and a token buyback — so if the share price does not rise, you earn nothing while you wait. Second, the standard financial data for this company is close to unusable: the numbers you would normally check, like debt and book value, are meaningless here because KKR's accounts include $410 billion of assets that mostly belong to other people. We have thrown almost all of it out and explained why.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is — three segments and a very large balance sheet

KKR & Co. Inc. is a Delaware corporation with approximately 5,043 employees (the 10-K gives roughly 4,200 across asset management plus approximately 800 in a subsidiary organisation), 36 offices worldwide, and Joseph Y. Bae and Scott C. Nuttall as co-chief executives. Henry R. Kravis and George R. Roberts remain on the board, which was re-elected on 2026-05-29 by KKR Management LLP under Section 3.02 of the bylaws rather than by public shareholders.

Scale, from the 10-K: "As of December 31, 2025, we managed $744 billion of assets under management, of which $219 billion comes from Global Atlantic."

Three reportable segments, from the March 2026 10-Q, and the chief operating decision makers are the co-CEOs:

Note the structure carefully, because it is the reason every consolidated ratio in the vendor file is wrong. The Asset Management segment charges fees to the Insurance and Strategic Holdings segments, which are themselves consolidated. Global Atlantic's insurance liabilities, and the debt of consolidated collateralised financing entities holding third-party money, sit on the same balance sheet as KKR's own corporate obligations. The result is $410 billion of total assets and $81.6 billion of total equity of which $50.7 billion — 62% — belongs to noncontrolling interests.

The 10-Q says so in terms that settle the matter:

> "consolidated CFEs issue debt securities to third-party investors which are collateralized by assets held by the CFE. Debt securities issued by CFEs are supported solely by the assets held at the CFEs and are not collateralized by assets of any other KKR entity... The CFE's debt obligations are non-recourse to KKR beyond the assets of the CFE."

And separately:

> "investments and other assets held directly by majority-owned consolidated levered investment vehicles and other entities have been funded with borrowings that are collateralized by the investments and assets they own. These borrowings are non-recourse to KKR beyond the investments or assets serving as collateral or the capital that KKR has committed to fund such investment vehicles."

A vendor netDebt of $54.76 billion and an enterpriseValueTTM of $142.7 billion are therefore not merely imprecise for this company — they are measuring a quantity that does not exist. Section 8 sets out the full rejection.

The one part of the structure that is genuinely new and genuinely interesting is the retail channel. From the 10-Q: "March 31, 2026, total K-Series AUM was $38 billion, which has grown significantly over the past three years." That is the single cleanest quantitative measure of the structural distribution thesis in this dive, and it appears nowhere in the vendor payload.
