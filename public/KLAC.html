The Overview

Making a modern computer chip involves several hundred separate manufacturing steps performed on a silicon wafer the size of a dinner plate, and a single speck of contamination in any of them can ruin the whole thing. KLA makes the machines that look for those specks. Its tools inspect and measure the wafer between the manufacturing steps, so the factory can find out that something has gone wrong while it can still be fixed, rather than at the end when thousands of chips are already scrap.

That is a very good business to be in for three reasons. First, nobody wants to save money on the equipment that tells them whether their $10 billion factory is working — so KLA can charge a lot, and it does: it keeps about 61 cents of every revenue dollar as gross profit and about 42 cents as operating profit, which is unusually high for a company that builds physical machines.

Second, once one of its tools is installed in a factory it stays there for a decade or more, and KLA sells a service contract to keep it running. Those contracts are now about 22% of the company's revenue, and they behave like a subscription. In the last industry downturn, when KLA's overall sales fell 6.5%, the service revenue still went up 10%. That is why this company falls less than its neighbours when the cycle turns.

Third, there are only a handful of companies in the world capable of making this equipment at all, and KLA is the one for inspection and measurement specifically. Its own annual report lists its competitors and does not list Lam Research among them, because Lam does a different job in the same factory.

So it is a very good business. The problem is what it costs. The shares are $195.45. Last year the company earned $3.66 a share, so you are paying about 53 times what it actually made. Analysts expect earnings of about $5.48 a share next year and $6.62 the year after, which would bring that down to about 36 times and then 30 times — but only if sales grow 32% next year, which is a very large jump, and only if profit margins widen at the same time. The company has already told investors that a shortage of memory chips it buys for its own products is pushing its costs up and squeezing its margins through the end of this year.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $212. They cost $195.45. That is an 8.5% gap, and the average analyst thinks $225, which is higher than we do. When our own number is close to the price and below the crowd's, the correct answer is usually to wait rather than to buy. Today the shares jumped 6.95% along with the whole semiconductor sector, so you would also be paying for a one-day move.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Fortress finances — $985M of net debt against $6.1B of annual cash earnings — attached to a share price with no valuation floor and a 35% fall already inside the last year.
  • Growth Quality 8/10. Revenue up 11.7% last year and up 96% over five years, with the recurring service piece growing even in the bad year.
  • Exponential Potential 6/10. Every new generation of chip needs more measuring than the last, which is a real and durable tailwind — but KLA can only grow as fast as its five or six customers decide to spend.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $212 against a current price of $195.45 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

KLA describes itself in the 10-K as "a leading supplier of process control and yield management solutions and services for the semiconductor and related electronics industries." It was formed as KLA-Tencor in April 1997 through the merger of KLA Instruments and Tencor Instruments, businesses that began operating in 1975 and 1976. Chief executive Richard Wallace; approximately 15,200 employees (the FY2025 10-K says roughly 15,000 full-time plus 200 part-time and temporary, across 18 major regions).

The workforce split is itself a disclosure about the business model: approximately 27% in research and development, 28% in customer service, 20% in manufacturing, 5% in sales and marketing. The service organisation is the single largest function. That is the installed-base annuity being staffed.

The three reportable segments (from the 10-K, Note 18)

  • Semiconductor Process Control — inspection, metrology and data-analytics products plus related service, for integrated-circuit, wafer, reticle and chemical/materials manufacturers.
  • Specialty Semiconductor Process — advanced vacuum deposition and etching tools for MEMS, radio-frequency communication chips and automotive/industrial power semiconductors.
  • PCB and Component Inspection — inspection, test, measurement and direct imaging for printed circuit boards, advanced packaging and MEMS. The Display business was exited: in March 2024 KLA ended manufacturing of most Display products, continuing service only.

And the six product categories — which are NOT the same thing

This is the single most important disclosure-structure point in the file, and the vendor gets it half right. The seg_prod block supplies six lines. Two of them (Specialty Semiconductor Process, PCB And Component Inspection) are reportable segment names; four (Defect Inspection, Patterning, Service, Other Revenue) are product categories. The 10-K keeps these in two separate tables and states at Note 18 that "Wafer Inspection and Patterning products are offered in the Semiconductor Process Control segment. Services are offered in multiple segments."

So the vendor has flattened KLA's major-product-category table, which happens to include two categories whose names coincide with two of the three reportable segments. It is not a segment table, and read as one it would imply KLA has six segments when it has three. The vendor also relabels the largest line: what it calls Defect Inspection the 10-K calls Wafer Inspection.

Having said that, the arithmetic is clean and we verified it. The six lines sum to $12,156.162M for FY2025 against the 10-K's stated total revenue of $12,156.2 million — an exact match — and to $9,812.247M for FY2024 against the reported $9.812B. Three things follow from that table and they are the whole quality case.

First, gross margin does not move. Six fiscal years, a 33% up year, a 6.5% down year and a 24% recovery year, and gross margin has stayed inside a 59.8% to 61.3% band. A 150 basis point range across a full cycle in a business that ships heavy capital equipment is not normal. It is the signature of pricing power that does not require the customer to be having a good year.

Second, operating margin troughed at 37.1%. In the year revenue fell 6.5%, KLA still converted more than a third of every revenue dollar to operating profit. The FY2024 net income of $2.762B was the worst year in this file and it was still a 28.1% net margin.

Third, and most importantly: service revenue grew 10.0% in the down year. From $2.117B to $2.330B while total revenue fell $684M. The 10-K explains why — service revenue is "generated largely from recurring 'subscription-like' contracts" and is a function of the installed base and its utilisation, not of new tool orders. The 10-Q reports service revenue up 16% year on year in both the March 2026 quarter and the nine months to March, and approximately 23% of total revenues in that quarter, up from approximately 22% for FY2025.

This is the mechanical explanation for why KLA should carry a premium multiple to the rest of the wafer-fab-equipment complex, and it is the one thing in this dive that is not in dispute. Roughly a quarter of the company is an annuity attached to tools already bolted to fab floors, growing at mid-teens rates, and it does not care about the order cycle. The other three-quarters is the most defensible product category in the industry.

The point of this section is to establish that the business quality is real — so that when the rest of the dive says the price is too high, that is not a disguised argument that the business is bad. It is not. It is an argument about what has already been paid for it.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+20.8%, from FY2027E $5.483 to FY2028E $6.625) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 35.6x to 32x on the respective forward year, −10.1%) + shareholder yield (+1.37%) ≈ +8.5% to +12%.

Our base case assumes multiple COMPRESSION, and we should be explicit about why — because it is not a rolldown artefact. Today the market pays 35.6x on the next fiscal year's estimate. In twelve months, if it pays 32x on the then-next fiscal year's estimate, that is a genuine de-rating of 10%, not the mechanical effect of the calendar advancing. We assume it because growth is decelerating hard on the consensus path itself: EPS +45.8% in FY2027, +20.8% in FY2028, +13.2% in FY2029. A multiple appropriate to 46% growth is not appropriate to 13% growth, and the market normally starts discounting the deceleration a year ahead of it.

Almost all of the expected return is earnings growth, and the multiple is fighting it. That is the opposite of the Markel setup in this batch, where compounding alone got you to fair value with no re-rating. Here, if the multiple simply HELD at 35.6x on FY2028E the price would be $236 (+20.7%); if it compressed to 28x the price would be $185 (−5.3%). The entire spread between a good and a bad outcome sits in a multiple we do not control and have no edge on.

The bull case at $265 does not require multiple expansion at all — it holds today's 35.6x and applies it to the FY2028 consensus high of $7.401. That is a deliberately conservative construction: the bull case is an earnings beat, not a re-rating. The bear case at $147 is the mirror image: a de-rating to 24x applied to the FY2028 consensus low of $6.118.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch.

This is the highest-quality business in this batch and the one where we have the least to add.

What is genuinely excellent, and none of it is in dispute: a 61.3% gross margin that has not left a 150 basis point band in six fiscal years spanning a full cycle; a 41.7% operating margin that troughed at 37.1% in a down year; a service business that is 22-23% of revenue, grows at 15-16%, and grew 10.0% in the year total revenue fell 6.5%; a 35.7% return on invested capital; $3.767B of free cash flow on $375.9M of capital expenditure; a corrected net debt position of $985M against $6.055B of EBITDA, with no maturity before fiscal 2029 and an undrawn $1.50B revolver; and a structural position that the 10-K's own competitor list and the knowledge base's one useful structural claim both describe as belonging to a handful of irreplaceable suppliers.

What we are declining to pay for: 53.4x trailing earnings, 35.6x FY2027 consensus, 18.8x sales, 42.3x corrected trailing EBITDA. A base fair value of $212 that is 8.5% above spot and 5.8% below the street's $225.18. A forward case that requires 32.4% revenue growth in one fiscal year and 460 basis points of net-margin expansion by FY2028, against a company-disclosed gross-margin headwind already visible as two consecutive quarters of year-on-year decline. A knowledge-base lane of three claims, none of which is about this company. And an entry price that includes a 6.95% one-day gap.

The distinction that matters. We are not saying KLA is a bad business or that the FY2027 ramp will not happen. We are saying we have no independent basis for believing it more than the market does, no valuation cushion if we are wrong, and no company-specific catalyst for 85 days. In the Synthos frame, a name where our fair value sits below consensus and the conviction lane is empty is a Watch by construction, not by hesitation.
