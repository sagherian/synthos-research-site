The Overview

Making a modern computer chip involves printing patterns onto a silicon wafer, hundreds of times over, at a scale where a speck of dust ruins the whole thing. Fabricators need to know, after each step, whether anything went wrong. KLA makes the machines that check. It has done this for decades and there are very few alternatives.

You can see how few from the numbers. KLA sells physical equipment — heavy, complicated machines — and keeps 61 cents of every revenue dollar after the cost of building them. Companies that make things do not normally earn that. Companies that face real competition do not earn that. It is the arithmetic signature of a business customers cannot easily walk away from.

Last fiscal year, which ended in June, KLA sold $13.6 billion of equipment and services and earned $3.66 per share. Analysts think next year it will sell $18.0 billion — a 32% jump — because the world is building a great many chip factories. That is the whole bull case, and it is a good one.

Here is the problem. The shares cost $195.45. That is about 30 times what analysts expect the company to earn two fiscal years from now, and about 53 times what it actually earned last year. Put differently: if you bought the entire company today, the cash it generated last year would return 1.48% of your money. A savings account does better.

Two other things you should know. About 31% of KLA's sales ship to China, and the U.S. government controls what it is allowed to send there — the company itself says in its filings that those rules may "significantly harm" the business. And the stock has been a rollercoaster: up 120% over twelve months, then down 35% from its peak, then up 7% today alone.

In June, KLA split its stock ten-for-one — every share became ten, each worth a tenth. This changes nothing about what the company is worth; it changes every per-share number you will see quoted, which is why we flag it prominently.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is about $205, roughly 5% above the price. The average analyst says $225. Neither is far enough above $195 to be interesting, and the downside if the 32% revenue jump does not arrive is much larger than the upside if it does. So: watch, do not buy.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
