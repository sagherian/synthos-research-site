The Overview

Take air, cool it until it turns to liquid, and separate it into its component gases. Oxygen for steel mills and hospitals. Nitrogen for electronics factories and food freezing. Argon for welding. Linde does that at industrial scale, and it also makes hydrogen, helium and carbon dioxide, and it builds the plants that do the separating.

The reason this is a good business has nothing to do with the chemistry, which is a hundred years old. It is the contracts. When Linde's biggest customers need gas, Linde builds a plant on their site, runs a pipe over the fence, and signs a contract for ten to twenty years that obliges the customer to buy a minimum amount and allows Linde to raise the price with inflation. Once that plant is there, the customer cannot switch. At the end of June the company had about $64 billion of such contracted future purchases on its books, with some contracts running thirty years.

For the smaller customers, the economics are different but the result is the same: liquid gas is heavy and cheap, so trucking it far does not pay, which means whoever has the nearest plant wins. Geography does the work a patent would do elsewhere.

So sales grow slowly and reliably — about 5% a year. What has made shareholders money is that profits grow twice as fast. Linde raises prices a little, cuts costs a little, and buys back about 1.5% of its own shares a year, and those three things turn 5% sales growth into about 10% earnings growth. It has done that consistently enough that it raised its dividend again this year, to $1.60 a quarter.

The problem is the price. The shares are $484.64. The company earned about $17.24 a share on an adjusted basis over the last year, so you are paying about 28 times. Analysts expect about $17.90 this year and $21.55 by 2028. Paying 28 times earnings for a company growing sales 5% a year is a high price, and the market appears to agree: the shares have gone up 5.5% over the last year while the S&P 500 went up 24.3%, even though the company beat expectations every single quarter. Nothing went wrong with the business. The market simply decided it would pay less.

Our estimate of value is $539. The shares cost $484.64. That is an 11% gap, and the average analyst target is $558, higher than ours. When our number is below the crowd's and the gap is small, waiting is usually right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

5b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over a two-year horizon decomposes as: adjusted EPS growth (+20.5%, from FY2026E $17.895 to FY2028E $21.555) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 27.1x on the forward year to 25x, −7.7%) + shareholder yield (+2.8% a year, ~5.7% compounded) ≈ +18% to +19% total, or roughly +9% annualised.

Our base case assumes mild multiple compression and we should say why, because it is not a rolldown artefact. Today the market pays 27.1x on the next calendar year. Two years out, if it pays 25x on the then-next year, that is a genuine de-rating of about 8%. We assume it for one reason: the market has been de-rating this stock steadily for twelve months in the face of five consecutive beats, and nothing in the fundamentals has appeared to stop it. The twelve-month total return of +5.5% against +24.3% for the index, on earnings that grew about 10%, means the multiple contracted roughly 15% in a year. Assuming that process fully halts and reverses is a stronger assumption than assuming it decays.

Almost all of the expected return is earnings growth plus dividends, and the multiple is a small headwind. That is a healthier structure than a name needing re-rating. If the multiple simply HELD at 27.1x on FY2028E the price would be $584 (+20.5%); if it compressed to 20x the price would be $431 (−11.1%). The spread between those is the entire uncertainty and it is not resolvable from the data in this file.

The bull case at $652 requires no multiple expansion — it holds today's 27.1x forward multiple against the FY2028 consensus high of $23.292, plus a point of drift. The bear case at $418 is a de-rating to 21x against the FY2028 consensus low of $19.901, which is roughly where the stock traded at its 52-week low of $389.38 plus the two years of earnings growth since.
