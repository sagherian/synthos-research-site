The Overview

Lockheed Martin makes the most expensive military equipment in the world: the F-35 fighter, the Patriot and THAAD missiles that shoot down incoming rockets, submarines' combat systems, and satellites. Almost three-quarters of its sales go to the United States government, and most of the rest to allied governments.

Two things are true about it right now and they point in opposite directions.

The first is that its order book has never been fuller. At the end of June it had $230 billion of work already contracted — more than three years of sales — and that figure grew by $37 billion in just six months, mostly because of one very large missile-defence order. Sales outside the United States are growing fast: Europe up 14% and Asia up 25% in a year, as allies rearm.

The second is that Lockheed keeps losing large amounts of money on a small number of secret development projects. When a company agrees a fixed price for something that has never been built before, and it turns out to cost more, the company must recognise the entire expected loss immediately. Lockheed has now recognised roughly $3.3 billion of such losses across two classified programmes. In one quarter last year those charges cut its operating profit from a normal $2.3 billion to $748 million. Shareholders have sued, alleging the company was not straight with them about it. And the company says in its own filing that more losses may come.

There is also a balance-sheet oddity worth understanding. Lockheed has bought back so much of its own stock over the years that its accounting equity is only $6.7 billion against $60 billion of assets, and once you strip out goodwill the figure is negative. That is not a crisis — the business generates $8.8 billion of cash a year — but it means the company has no equity cushion, and it has just agreed to spend $3.45 billion buying another business partly with borrowed money.

Now the price. The shares are $589.13. They have risen 40% in the last twelve months, more than the whole stock market did, from a company whose share price normally barely moves at all. A standard momentum gauge reads 81 out of 100, which is the highest in this research batch and conventionally means very overbought.

Our estimate of fair value is $585 — slightly below the current price. The average analyst says $634, and the most pessimistic says $575, which is also below where the shares trade today.

So: a genuinely improving business, a genuinely worrying execution record, and a price that has already moved. The sensible response is to name the price we would want — around $500 — and wait.

  • Growth Quality 6/10. Real acceleration, off a base that did not grow at all between 2020 and 2023.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. No compounding mechanism of its own — and a credible argument that cheap drones are the exponential coming for its main product.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

> "As of June 28, 2026, our ending backlog was $230.4 billion. $230.4 billion is 3.07x trailing revenue of $77.0 billion. Thirty percent within twelve months is roughly $69 billion, against consensus FY2026 revenue of $80.8 billion — so the backlog covers roughly 86% of the next year's expected sales before a single new order is booked.

One caveat the company itself supplies, and it matters: "For our cost-reimbursable and fixed-priced-incentive contracts, the estimated consideration we expect to receive... may exceed the contractual award amount... As the risks on such contracts are successfully retired, the estimated consideration from customers may be reduced, resulting in a reduction of backlog without a corresponding recognition of sales." Backlog can fall without revenue rising. And an undefinitized contractual action — the source of $36.8 billion of the increase — is by definition a contract whose terms are not yet agreed. The number is real; its precision is not.

2. The execution problem, in the company's own numbers

This is the section that determines the verdict, and every figure in it is quoted from the 10-Q.

Aeronautics classified programme:

> "We have experienced significant performance issues on an existing classified program at our Aeronautics business segment. The initial phase is on a fixed-price incentive fee contract with fixed-price incentive fee options for additional phases... during the second quarter of 2025, we recognized additional reach-forward losses of $950 million across both phases of the program."

The drivers the company lists: "(1) software development performance degradation and integration findings observed over a continued period; (2) learnings in recent software and build experience on another program... (3) significant changes in test plan...; (4) safety-critical and other necessary design and engineering changes in response to certain observed performance degradation and a discrete event; and (5) complete schedule realignment."

> "As of June 28, 2026, cumulative losses recognized to date on this program remained at approximately $1.8 billion across both phases. As of June 28, 2026, $427 million of the losses remained accrued... we may need to record additional losses in future periods."

Missiles and Fire Control classified programme:

> "...a competitively bid classified contract, which includes a cost-reimbursable base contract for the initial phase and multiple fixed-price options for additional phases. We previously disclosed that the options may be exercised over the next several years and, if performed, we expect they would each be at a loss. Certain options have been exercised, and... we continue to believe it is probable that the remaining unexercised options will be exercised. As of June 28, 2026, cumulative losses recognized on the program remained at approximately $1.5 billion in total."

Read that carefully. The company expects future contract options to be exercised, and expects each of them to be at a loss, and regards their exercise as probable. That is a disclosed, quantified, forward-looking commitment to lose money — and it is not a hypothetical risk factor.

Canadian Maritime Helicopter Program: the final aircraft was delivered in 2025; the programme "has experienced performance issues and we have previously recorded losses" including $570 million in the June 2025 quarter; restructuring discussions "may be prolonged or unsuccessful, and could result in a contract termination."

United Launch Alliance: the 50/50 joint venture with Boeing. The 10-Q warns that Lockheed and Boeing "expect to provide additional financial support to ULA to support its liquidity or ongoing operations and could incur impairment and operating losses if the Vulcan Centaur rocket does not perform consistent with ULA's assumptions."

Securities litigation:

> "On July 28, 2025, a putative class action was filed—and subsequently amended on January 12, 2026—in United States District Court for the Southern District of New York against us and certain current and former members of our senior management... on behalf of persons and entities that purchased or otherwise acquired our securities between January 23, 2024 and July 21, 2025. Plaintiffs seek unspecified losses allegedly caused by alleged misstatements about certain classified programs in the Aeronautics and MFC business segments and F-35 program, which were allegedly revealed to be false when we announced estimated losses relating to certain of those programs."
