The Overview

Lowe's sells the things people need to build, fix and decorate houses: lumber, appliances, paint, plumbing, garden supplies, kitchen fittings. It has 1,759 large stores in the United States and is the second-biggest company of its kind after Home Depot.

Its problem is that Americans are not moving house. When mortgage rates rose from about 3% to over 6%, homeowners with cheap old mortgages stopped selling, because moving means giving up the cheap loan. Existing home sales are at ten-year lows. And people buy the most from Lowe's in the months just after they move.

You can see the effect directly in the company's own numbers. In the three months to 1 May, sales at stores open more than a year rose 0.6%. But the number of customer transactions fell 0.9% — fewer people came in and bought things. The 0.6% came entirely from each remaining customer spending 1.5% more, which is mostly price. For the full previous year the same pattern was worse: comparable sales up 0.2%, transactions down 2.8%.

Lowe's headline revenue looks much better than that — up 10.3% in the quarter — but that is because it bought two companies. In June 2025 it paid $1.3 billion for a firm that installs flooring and cabinets for home builders. In October 2025 it paid $8.8 billion for a distributor of building materials to professional contractors. Together, $10.1 billion — paid for by borrowing $7 billion.

That borrowing matters. Lowe's now owes roughly $42 billion net, about 3.4 times its annual cash earnings — a meaningful step up. Its accounting net worth is negative $9.9 billion, which sounds alarming but is simply what happens when a company buys back more of its own stock over the years than it has retained in profits. It works fine while the cash keeps coming; it leaves no cushion if it stops.

The strategy is sensible: Lowe's has always been weaker than Home Depot with professional builders and contractors, and buying its way into that market is a reasonable fix. The awkward part is the timing — both businesses it bought sell into new home construction, which is the weakest part of an already weak market.

Now the other side. The shares have fallen 24% from their high and have gone nowhere for a year while the wider market rose 24%. They generate about 6.3% of their price in spare cash each year. And unusually, all 51 analysts who cover the company think the shares are worth more than they cost — the most pessimistic says $232, which is still 6% above today's price of $218.08.

Our own estimate is $235, about 8% up. That is a real but modest gap, and the company reports its next set of results in fifteen days.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Strong cash generation; meaningfully more debt than a year ago, taken on at a bad moment.
  • Growth Quality 4/10. The growth was bought. The underlying customer count is shrinking.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. A large, mature retailer. Nothing here compounds by itself.

1. What the business is, and the disclosure that is missing

Lowe's describes itself in the 10-K as a Fortune 100 company and "the world's second largest home improvement retailer." Chief executive Marvin R. Ellison; approximately 276,000 employees per the vendor profile; Mooresville, North Carolina; NYSE-listed.

From the 10-K filed 2026-03-23:

> "As of January 30, 2026, Lowe's operated 1,759 home improvement stores and outlets in the United States, representing approximately 196 million square feet of retail selling space. In addition, Lowe's operated over 540 branch locations in the United States and Canada, which include our current year acquisitions of Foundation Building Materials (FBM) and Artisan Design Group (ADG)."

The stores average approximately 112,000 square feet of retail selling space plus 32,000 square feet of outdoor garden centre, and there are 16 Lowe's Outlet stores in a smaller format.

A disclosure limitation that must be stated before any number is used

Lowe's filings in this archive are prose-only. A direct count returns zero [TABLE] markers in the 10-K filed 2026-03-23 and zero in either 10-Q. No income statement, balance sheet, cash-flow statement or segment table is recoverable from the filed text. Numbers that appear in the narrative are recoverable — and several important ones are, including comparable sales, acquisition prices, debt terms and dividend rates — but the financial statements themselves are not.

The consequence, stated plainly: every balance-sheet, income-statement and cash-flow figure in this dive comes from the vendor payload, and the most recent one available there is the annual balance sheet at 30 January 2026 — three months before the latest 10-Q's balance-sheet date of 1 May 2026. We could not verify the vendor's balance sheet against a filing for this name, and we say so rather than implying we did. Where the filings do contain a number in prose, it is used and attributed; where they do not, the vendor is used and labelled.

2. The comp — the number that governs this dive

From the 10-K filed 2026-03-23, for fiscal 2025 (year ended 30 January 2026):

> "Net sales for fiscal 2025 increased 3.1% from fiscal 2024 to $86.3 billion. Comparable sales for fiscal 2025 increased 0.2%, consisting of a 3.0% increase in comparable average ticket, partially offset by a 2.8% decrease in comparable customer transactions. Net earnings for fiscal 2025 decreased 4.4% to $6.7 billion. Diluted earnings per common share decreased 3.1% in fiscal 2025 to $11.85 from $12.23 in fiscal 2024."

And the quarterly comp path within fiscal 2025, also from the 10-K: "a decline of 1.7% in the first quarter, an increase of 1.1% in the second quarter, an increase of 0.4% in the third quarter, and an increase of 1.3% in the fourth quarter."

From the 10-Q filed 2026-05-28, for the quarter ended 1 May 2026:

> "Net sales in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 improved 10.3% to $23.1 billion compared to net sales of $20.9 billion in the first quarter of fiscal 2025. Comparable sales for the first quarter of fiscal 2026 increased 0.6%, consisting of an increase in comparable average ticket of 1.5%, partially offset by a decrease of 0.9% in comparable customer transactions. Net earnings in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 remained consistent with the first quarter of fiscal 2025 at $1.6 billion. Diluted earnings per common share were $2.90 in the first quarter of fiscal 2026 compared to $2.92 in the first quarter of fiscal 2025."

Four things follow from those two passages and they are the whole operating case.

First, reported growth and real growth are different numbers. First-quarter revenue rose 10.3%; comparable sales rose 0.6%. The 9.7-point difference is the acquisitions. A reader looking only at the vendor's inc_q block — which shows $23,078M against $20,930M — would see a retailer accelerating to double-digit growth. It is not.

Second, transactions are falling. Minus 2.8% for the full year, minus 0.9% in the quarter. Fewer customers, each spending more. Since a substantial part of the ticket increase in a period of tariffs and building-materials inflation is price rather than mix, the honest reading is that unit demand is contracting.

Third, online is doing the work. The 10-Q states that online sales "positively impacted first quarter fiscal 2026 and fiscal 2025 comparable sales by approximately 185 basis points and 65 basis points, respectively", and the 10-K gives 105, 50 and 25 basis points for fiscal 2025, 2024 and 2023. Strip online out and first-quarter physical-store comps were approximately minus 1.25%. Online is growing fast and is the one genuinely encouraging operating datum in this file — but it is masking the store trend, not reversing it.

Fourth, the categories that work are the professional ones. The 10-Q names "Rough Plumbing, Lawn & Garden, and Appliances" as the leaders and attributes the strength to "continued growth with our Pro customer and online." Nine of thirteen categories comped positive in the first quarter against five of fourteen for fiscal 2025 — a genuine improvement, and the strategic rationale for the acquisitions in a single sentence.

Third, and this is the strategic risk the market may be under-weighting: both acquisitions increase exposure to new residential construction. ADG sells "to national, regional and local home builders and property managers"; FBM distributes building materials to professional contractors. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+10.0%, from FY2027E $13.43 to FY2028E $14.78) + multiple drift (mild COMPRESSION, from 16.2x to roughly 15.5x on the respective forward year, −4%) + shareholder yield (+2.4%, of which 2.2% is the dividend) ≈ +8% to +9%.

Our base assumes a small de-rating, and the reason is leverage rather than growth. Lowe's has historically earned a forward multiple in the high teens as an unlevered-feeling, buyback-driven compounder. It is now a 3.4x-levered company with negative book equity, falling interest coverage, and a quarter of its recent revenue growth purchased rather than earned. A modest discount to its own history is the appropriate treatment while that is true — and it reverses if the acquisitions prove accretive and leverage comes down.

Almost all of the expected return is earnings growth plus dividend; the multiple is a small headwind. If the multiple simply held at 16.2x on FY2028E the price would be $239 (+9.7%); at 14x it would be $207 (−5.1%); at 19x it would be $281 (+28.7%). The distribution is wide and roughly symmetric around the base, which is what a cyclical at a trough looks like.

The bull case at $306 requires the multiple to EXPAND to 22x on the FY2027 consensus high — and that is the fragile leg of the bull case, stated explicitly. It is not impossible: Lowe's has traded above 22x forward within the last five years. But it needs a housing inflection to justify it, and the bull case is therefore a bet on an external series, not on the company.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch.

What is genuinely good: free cash flow of $7,651 million at an 8.9% margin and a 6.3% yield; return on invested capital of 21.2%; a 24.1% drawdown that has already removed most of the valuation excess; a share count down 25% in five years, from 750 million to roughly 561 million; $4.6 billion of undrawn revolving credit and an intact $10.5 billion buyback authorisation; nine of thirteen product categories comping positive in the latest quarter against five of fourteen for the prior year; online adding approximately 185 basis points to the comp, nearly triple the prior year's contribution; a coherent strategic pivot into the professional-contractor channel where the company has always been weakest; and a street where all 51 price targets sit above the current price.

What we are declining to pay 16.2x forward earnings for: comparable sales of +0.6% with comparable customer transactions negative in each of the last two reported periods; reported revenue growth of 10.3% of which 9.7 points is acquisition; net leverage of 3.4x after $7.0 billion of new borrowing, on a balance sheet with negative $9,917 million of book equity and interest coverage that fell enough in one quarter to convert a $60 million operating-income increase into a $2 million pre-tax decrease; $9,853 million of goodwill and intangibles where there were none a year ago, on two businesses both levered to new residential construction at a housing trough; a buyback cut 95% in the year the acquisitions closed; and a knowledge-base lane whose two highest-conviction claims on the driver are bearish at 80, the fresher arguing that housing weakness is demand rather than rates.

The distinction that matters. We are not saying Lowe's is a bad company or that the acquisitions were a mistake — the Pro strategy is the right answer to the problem the company has, and the market it is buying into is genuinely large and fragmented. We are saying that the price is 16.2x a consensus EPS path that requires comps to inflect, that the inflection depends on an external housing series which the freshest independent evidence says is not inflecting, and that our own base returns 7.8% before a print that lands in fifteen days. The asymmetry — 40% up against 18% down — is the best in this batch, and it is the reason this is a Watch with a specific buy level rather than a dismissal.

Pre-registered KILL criteria — what would take this to Avoid:

  • Comparable sales turning negative on 2026-08-19 or thereafter, after four consecutive positive quarters.
  • Comparable customer transactions falling more than 2% in any quarter, which would mean the ticket-driven comp is masking accelerating unit decline.
  • Any impairment against the $9,853 million of goodwill and intangibles. The 10-Q states no impairment as of 1 May 2026; that is the baseline.
  • Net leverage above 3.75x, or a credit-rating outlook change — the 10-Q explicitly names ratings as a driver of borrowing cost.
  • Full-year guidance below roughly $12.00 of adjusted EPS, against the $12.48 consensus.
  • A break below the 52-week low of $201.92 on company-specific news.

Pre-registered FLIP conditions — what would take this to Buy — Tactical:

  • Price below approximately $200 (roughly 14.9x FY2027E, 13.5x FY2028E) with no deterioration in comps. At $200 the base case is +17.5% against a bear case only 11% lower, and the risk/reward becomes genuinely asymmetric. That is 8.3% below today's close and 1% below the 52-week low.
  • Comparable customer TRANSACTIONS turning positive on 2026-08-19. This is the single cleanest confirmation available and it is worth more than any comp headline, because it separates real demand recovery from price-driven ticket inflation.
  • Separate disclosure of Foundation Building Materials revenue and margin showing clear accretion, which would convert $8.8 billion of unexamined capital into a measurable return.
  • The buyback resuming at $3 billion a year or more against the untouched $10.5 billion authorisation, which would restore the mechanism that has carried EPS for five years.
  • Existing home sales inflecting above 4.5 million annualised, against the 4.09 million the knowledge base cites — the external series on which everything else depends.

Single biggest risk: the comp does not inflect, and the leverage taken on to compensate turns out to have been pro-cyclical. Lowe's response to a demand problem was to borrow $7 billion and buy $10.1 billion of businesses that sell into new residential construction — the one part of the housing market that is arguably weaker than the part it was already exposed to. If comparable transactions keep falling and existing home sales stay near 4.09 million, the company will be carrying 3.4x net leverage, $9,853 million of goodwill and intangibles, a suspended buyback and negative book equity into a flat revenue base. Consensus models EPS accelerating from +1.6% to +7.7% to +10.0% on the assumption that does not happen. At 16.2x forward earnings there is some cushion — this is not a 45x stock — but there is nothing in the balance sheet to absorb a second year of negative transactions, and the historical shock absorber, a $4-6 billion annual buyback, has been spent on the acquisitions.
