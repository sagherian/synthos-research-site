The Overview

Making a computer chip means building it up in hundreds of layers on a silicon wafer. Some steps add material; some steps carve it away. Lam Research makes the machines that do the adding (deposition) and the carving (etch), plus the machines that clean the wafer between steps. It sells those machines to about a dozen customers worldwide — Samsung and TSMC are the two biggest — and then earns a second, steadier stream of money servicing the machines it has already sold. That service business was $6.9 billion last year, about 38 cents of every dollar of revenue, and it grows every time another machine ships.

Lam is unusually tied to memory chips — the kind that store data rather than do the calculating. That matters enormously right now, because memory is in a boom. Micron, the big American memory maker, has gone from $9.3 billion of quarterly revenue to $41.5 billion in a little over a year and is now worth more than a trillion dollars. Memory companies that make that much money eventually spend it on new factories, and new factories mean new Lam machines. That is the whole bull case, and today the market voted for it: Lam rose 7.85%, more than any other equipment maker.

Here is the catch, and it is the reason this is a "watch" and not a "buy." A year ago you could have bought this company for about $96 a share. It now costs $317.74. In that year the company's profits per share went up by 39%. The price went up by 230%. The difference is the market simply deciding to pay far more for each dollar of profit — from about 23 dollars of price per dollar of annual profit to about 55. So most of the last year's gain was a change of opinion, not a change of business.

And the memory boom has not actually shown up in Lam's numbers yet in the way you might expect. Over the same period that Micron's revenue multiplied by four and a half, Lam's grew 42%. Lam's own quarterly filing says most of this year's growth came from foundry customers — the ones making processors — not from memory. Two other quiet warnings sit in the filings: customers' advance deposits have shrunk from $2.77 billion to $2.22 billion over two quarters, and China, which is subject to US export restrictions, is now about 37% of sales.

Meanwhile every company insider in our records has been selling — the chief executive sold 30,000 shares at $390 in early July, two directors sold in the $335 to $359 range — all at prices above where the stock trades today. The company itself bought back its own shares at roughly $91 and roughly $219 in its two most recent accelerated programmes, which is a long way below $318.

We think fair value is about $353, roughly 11% above the current price and slightly below what the average analyst thinks. In a bad scenario we get $205. In a good one, $430. That is an even bet, and an even bet is not a reason to act.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is — one segment, two revenue streams, three markets

Lam Research is, in the words of the FY2025 10-K, "a global supplier of innovative wafer fabrication equipment and services to the semiconductor industry." Incorporated 1980, headquartered in Fremont, California, listed since 1984. Chief executive Timothy Archer.

The 10-K states the company operates in ONE reportable business segment: manufacturing and servicing of wafer processing semiconductor manufacturing equipment. That matters for how the vendor data is read, and is addressed in Section 8.

Product lines (10-K Item 1): deposition — atomic layer deposition, chemical vapour deposition, electrochemical deposition (the ALTUS, VECTOR, SPEED, SABRE families) — and etch, including conductor etch, dielectric etch (the Vantex platform) and cryogenic low-temperature etch for high-aspect-ratio structures, plus wafer clean. The 10-K is specific that leading-edge memory devices have "especially challenging structures, such as extremely deep holes and trenches" and that its dielectric etch capability targets "3D NAND high aspect ratio hole, trench, contact, and capacitor cell applications." This is the technical basis of the memory leverage.

2. The single most important thing — you are buying the memory cycle at a multiple that has already tripled

Two facts, placed next to each other, are the whole dive.

Fact one: the memory supercycle is real and Lam is its most levered equipment expression. Micron closed today at $892.67, +7.62%, a $1.008 trillion market capitalisation, with quarterly revenue up from $9.3B to $41.5B across five quarters. Memory profits become memory capital expenditure become Lam orders. Lam's Korea revenue — Samsung and the memory complex — grew 43.6% in FY2025 to $4.128B. The March 2026 10-Q records memory strengthening across both DRAM and non-volatile memory. Today's price action ranked the four WFE names in exactly the order of their memory leverage: LRCX +7.85%, KLAC +6.95%, AMAT +5.48%, ASML +4.22%, against Micron's +7.62%. The market is pricing Lam as memory beta, and it is right to.

That is not a criticism of the company — the earnings genuinely grew 38.8% and the gross margin genuinely expanded 180 basis points. It is a statement about what is left to capture.

Why this matters more for a WFE name than for almost anything else. Wafer-fab equipment is a capital cycle. Historically these businesses trade at low multiples on peak earnings and high multiples on trough earnings, because the market correctly discounts the mean reversion. Lam's own history in this file demonstrates it: revenue fell 14.5% in FY2024, diluted EPS fell from $3.32 to $2.90, and the FY2024 estimate row in the vendor's own file was $15.591B of revenue against $14.905B delivered. At $317.74 the market is paying 55.2x trailing and 27.0x FY2028 consensus earnings — a growth multiple, on rising earnings, in a cyclical. That is a bet that this cycle is structurally different. It might be: the 10-K's argument that 3D scaling, multiple patterning and advanced packaging permanently increase the number of deposition and etch steps per wafer is a genuine argument for a higher through-cycle multiple. But it is a bet, and it is already in the price.

State it as plainly as possible: at 55x trailing, earnings can double and the stock can go up 11%. That is the arithmetic of buying a cyclical after it has been re-rated. The reason we assume compression rather than a hold is threefold — growth maturation (consensus itself decelerates to +10.7% revenue by FY2029), risk normalisation (a 12.1% effective tax rate and a 37% China mix are both fragile inputs to the forward EPS), and cycle position (equipment multiples contract as customer capital budgets peak).
