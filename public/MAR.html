The Overview

Marriott does not own many hotels. It owns the names on them — Marriott, Ritz-Carlton, Sheraton, Westin, W, Courtyard and about two dozen more — and it collects a fee from the people who do own the buildings, in exchange for the brand, the booking system and the loyalty programme that fills the rooms.

That is a very good arrangement. Marriott spent $282 million on equipment and technology in six months while collecting $1.8 billion of cash. There is almost nothing to buy and almost nothing to maintain.

What Marriott has done with that cash is buy its own shares — about $13.6 billion of them over four years, and another $2.2 billion in the first seven months of this year. It has bought so many that the accountants now record the company as having negative net worth: minus $3.8 billion. That is not a sign of distress; it is arithmetic. But it does mean the company borrows to buy stock, and in the first half of this year the buybacks were slightly larger than all the cash the business generated.

The results reported yesterday were fine and the shares have been falling anyway. Revenue per available room — the industry's core measure — rose 3.4% worldwide. But all of that came from charging more per night, not from filling more rooms. And outside America it went down 0.5%, because of the war in the Middle East. The company says plainly that the damage is continuing into the current quarter.

It also trimmed its forecast for how many new rooms it will add this year, took a $68 million write-down on a hotel, and made a $27 million legal provision.

So the shares are down 14% from their high and the momentum indicators are the weakest of any company in this research batch. You pay about 26 times what analysts expect Marriott to earn in 2027, for a business growing profits about 12% a year, part of which comes from the share buyback rather than from the hotels.

We think it is worth about $370 against $345. That is not enough to buy a cyclical business at the point in its cycle when it stops filling rooms. It is enough to put on a list.

  • Growth Quality 6/10. The algorithm works. Every component is currently decelerating.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. Credit-card and residential branding fees are pure brand rent and growing fastest.

It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. 1. The June 2026 quarter — the model, and the war

All figures from the 10-Q filed 2026-08-03, one day before this dive.

On the demand line, verbatim:

> "In the 2026 second quarter, worldwide RevPAR increased 3.4 percent, primarily driven by ADR growth of 3.5 percent. In the 2026 first half, worldwide RevPAR increased 3.8 percent, primarily driven by ADR growth of 3.3 percent. RevPAR growth was strong across all of our regions, except for Middle East & Africa."

> "In the U.S. & Canada, RevPAR increased 5.0 percent in the 2026 second quarter and 4.6 percent in the 2026 first half, reflecting strong demand across all brand tiers and customer segments, as well as demand from the World Cup in June 2026."

> "In our International regions, RevPAR decreased 0.5 percent in the 2026 second quarter and grew 2.0 percent in the 2026 first half. Performance was negatively impacted by the conflict in the Middle East, which resulted in a sharp decline in RevPAR in our Middle East & Africa region beginning in March 2026, with the impact from the conflict continuing into the third quarter. The continued operational and financial impact on our business depends on the duration and extent of travel disruption resulting from the conflict."

Three things must be read out of that.

First, worldwide RevPAR growth of 3.4% came from ADR growth of 3.5%, which means occupancy contributed approximately NOTHING. In the half, RevPAR +3.8% on ADR +3.3% implies roughly half a point from occupancy. Rate-led RevPAR is the late-cycle signature in lodging: hotels raise price while filling the same or fewer rooms, and the arithmetic reverses hard when demand turns because rate falls faster than it rose. This is the most important operating fact in the file and it is not in any headline.

Second, the U.S. figure is flattered by a one-off. The company itself names "demand from the World Cup in June 2026" as a driver of the +5.0%. That does not repeat in the September or December quarters.

Third, the international drag is dated, exogenous and — per the company — ongoing. It began in March 2026, it took the region's RevPAR sharply down, and "the impact from the conflict continu[es] into the third quarter." Note the symmetry with Marathon Petroleum in this same batch, whose June-quarter refining margin per barrel more than doubled because of the same conflict. One war, two names, opposite signs, both filing-verified.

Fee revenue and where the growth came from:

> "The increase in franchise fees in the 2026 second quarter and 2026 first half primarily reflected higher co-branded credit card fees ($73 million and $132 million, respectively) as well as higher revenue related to our franchised properties due to rooms growth ($30 million and $53 million, respectively), higher RevPAR, and other items. The increase in franchise fees in the 2026 first half also reflected higher residential branding fees ($32 million)."

> "The increase in incentive management fees... primarily reflected higher profits at managed hotels in the U.S. & Canada."

The single largest identified driver of fee growth in the quarter was the co-branded credit card programme, at $73 million — more than double the $30 million from rooms growth. That is a payments annuity, not a lodging business, and it is worth noticing that a hotel company's fastest-growing fee line has nothing to do with hotels.

And what went the other way:

> "Owned, leased, and other revenue, net of owned, leased, and other expense, decreased in the 2026 second quarter and 2026 first half primarily due to a property-related litigation accrual ($27 million for both the 2026 second quarter and 2026 first half)."

> "Depreciation, amortization, and other expenses increased... primarily due to the $68 million impairment charge."

> "U.S. & Canada segment profit decreased in the 2026 second quarter and 2026 first half, compared to the same periods in 2025, despite the higher net fee revenues, primarily due to the $68 million impairment charge..., lower cost reimbursement revenue, net of reimbursed expenses ($37 million and $109 million, respectively), and a property-related litigation accrual ($27 million...)."

Marriott's largest and best-performing region produced LOWER segment profit on HIGHER fee revenue. That is a clean statement of the quarter and it explains a share price 14.2% below its high after an earnings beat.

Over the long term, our centralized programs and services are not designed to impact our economics, either positively or negatively."

Three consequences.

priceToSalesRatioTTM of 3.35x is meaningless — on the fee-plus-owned basis of roughly $7.1 billion it is nearer 12.7x. Every margin ratio computed on reported revenue is likewise depressed by roughly a factor of four: netProfitMarginTTM of 9.62% is approximately 35% on the economic revenue base; operatingProfitMarginTTM of 15.8% is approximately 58%. This is not a vendor defect — Marriott reports it this way because accounting standards require gross presentation of principal-basis reimbursements — but it is the same reading trap as the USB, BNY, IBKR and COF gross-ups, and it is flagged here for the same reason.

Verdict

Watch.

The arithmetic. At $345.20 Marriott trades at 29.6x the FY2026 consensus of $11.669 and 26.5x FY2027's $13.048, for consensus earnings growth of 11.8% — of which roughly four points is the buyback. Our base fair value of $370 is 7.2% above spot with 1.75:1 asymmetry ($285 bear, $450 bull). The base requires a 7% multiple expansion and that is the fragile leg; if the multiple holds, fair value is roughly spot.

What is genuinely good. An asset-light franchise model with 1.17% capital intensity and 18.6% return on invested capital, a 629,000-room pipeline with 279,000 under construction, U.S. & Canada RevPAR +5.0%, and two fee lines growing faster than the hotels — co-branded credit card fees +$73M in the quarter and +$132M in the half, and residential branding fees +$32M in the half — both of which are pure brand rent. Capex and free cash flow, the fields most often corrupt in this feed, are verified correct. And the entry is the most technically oversold in the batch: RSI 33.7, 8.7% below the 50-day average, 14.2% off the high.

What keeps it at Watch rather than Buy.

First, RevPAR growth is entirely rate. Worldwide +3.4% on ADR +3.5% — occupancy contributed nothing — and the U.S. component was helped by "demand from the World Cup in June 2026", which does not repeat. A lodging cycle that has stopped adding occupancy is a lodging cycle in its later stages, and 26.5x is not a late-cycle multiple.

Second, the international drag is live and the company says so. "[T]he impact from the conflict continu[es] into the third quarter," and "[t]he continued operational and financial impact on our business depends on the duration and extent of travel disruption." International RevPAR was MINUS 0.5% and over half the development pipeline is outside the U.S. and Canada.

Third, the quarter's quality was poor beneath the beat. Revenue missed by 1.7%; U.S. & Canada segment profit FELL despite higher net fee revenues on a $68M impairment and a $27M litigation accrual; net rooms growth was trimmed to the low end; and the headline +20.4% adjusted EPS growth compares a charge-excluded figure to a gain-excluded one against GAAP growth of +2.2%.

Fourth, the capital structure is stretched by choice. Equity is MINUS $3,771M; first-half repurchases of $1,819M exceeded first-half operating cash flow of $1,806M, funded with $670M of net debt issuance; net debt is 3.67x EBITDA against a 4.5x covenant. The buyback is a third of the consensus EPS growth and it is a policy, not an entitlement.

Fifth, the knowledge base's one name-level claim says Airbnb threatens exactly Marriott's tiers.

Pre-registered KILL criteria — what would take this to Hold or Avoid:

  • Worldwide RevPAR growth below 1%, or negative, in any quarter.
  • Occupancy contributing negatively to RevPAR for two consecutive quarters — it contributed approximately zero in the June quarter.
  • Net rooms growth guided below 4.5%, after being trimmed to the low end of that range.
  • The buyback paused or materially slowed as leverage approaches the 4.5x Credit Facility covenant from 3.67x.
  • A second impairment, after the $68 million taken in the June quarter.
  • International RevPAR still negative in the December quarter, i.e. the Middle East drag outlasting the company's own third-quarter framing.

Pre-registered UPGRADE conditions — what would take this to Buy — Tactical:

  • A price below roughly $315, which is 24.1x FY2027E and restores asymmetry to better than 2.2:1 without requiring any multiple expansion. This is the operative trigger and it is a price.
  • Occupancy contributing positively to worldwide RevPAR, which would establish that the cycle has room.
  • International RevPAR back to positive, which reverses on a headline rather than on execution.
  • Separate disclosure and sizing of the co-branded credit card and residential branding fee streams, which would let roughly $200 million a year of pure brand rent be valued as such.
  • Net rooms growth restored to the top of the 4.5-5.0% range.

Buying oversold is not the same as buying cheap. Logged as a tracked Synthos call (Watch) as of 2026-08-04 at $345.20, with the fair-value anchors, kill criteria and the $315 trigger all gradeable.

Single biggest risk: rate-only RevPAR growth. Worldwide comparable RevPAR rose 3.4% in the June quarter on ADR growth of 3.5%, which means occupancy contributed nothing, and the strongest region's +5.0% was helped by a World Cup that will not recur. In lodging, rate is the last thing to go up and the first thing to come down: an operator can hold price while occupancy erodes for two or three quarters, and then discounts to fill rooms, at which point RevPAR falls faster than demand does. Marriott's fees are a percentage of that number. At 26.5x FY2027 consensus with negative book equity, 3.67x leverage against a 4.5x covenant, and a buyback that consumed more than the company's operating cash flow in the first half, the price assumes the cycle has further to run. The company's own disclosure says international demand is already contracting and will continue to in the current quarter.
