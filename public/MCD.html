The Overview

McDonald's does not really make most of its money selling hamburgers. It makes it by owning the property under about 45,000 restaurants and letting other people — franchisees — run them. Those operators pay McDonald's rent on the building and a royalty on every dollar they sell. Ninety-five out of every hundred McDonald's restaurants are run this way, and roughly nine out of every ten dollars of restaurant profit the company reports comes from that rent-and-royalty stream rather than from restaurants it runs itself.

That is a very good arrangement. The franchisee takes the risk of staffing, food costs and the daily grind. McDonald's takes a slice off the top, and because it owns the land and buildings — about 56% of the land and 80% of the buildings — it keeps control of the location forever. When a 20-year franchise agreement ends, the company keeps the real estate and simply signs a new one. This is why the company keeps 46 cents of every revenue dollar as operating profit, which is a remarkable number for anything to do with food.

So why is the share price down? Last year the shares fell 11%, while the American stock market as a whole rose 24%. The shares are 21% below their high of the last year and sit barely 2% above their low.

The reason is a worry, not a fact. McDonald's customers are, on average, less well-off than most companies' customers. When money is tight for people at the lower end of the income scale, they cut back — and there is a live argument about whether McDonald's wins from that (people leave more expensive restaurants and come to McDonald's instead) or loses from it (people just eat at home). The company's own filings do not settle it, and there is one detail that is genuinely worrying. When McDonald's reports that sales at existing US restaurants grew, it says every single time that the growth came from a higher average bill — not from more customers. It never once claims in these documents that more people came through the door. Growing sales by raising prices works until it doesn't.

There is a second thing worth understanding, because it looks alarming and mostly isn't. McDonald's has negative shareholders' equity — the accounting value of the company is below zero, about minus $1.8 billion. That is not a sign of distress. It is what happens when a company spends decades buying back its own shares, funded partly with borrowing: it has spent $79.3 billion buying back stock over its life. The practical consequence is that any measure that divides by the book value of the company — price-to-book, return on equity, debt-to-equity — produces a meaningless or backwards number. We do not use any of them in this report, and the automated rating system the data vendor supplies gives McDonald's the worst possible score on all three, which is why it grades the company C+. That grade tells you nothing.

What we can say about the debt is straightforward: the company owes about $40.1 billion of borrowings plus $14.8 billion of lease obligations, against annual cash earnings of about $14.8 billion. That is roughly three-and-a-half years of earnings — real leverage for a company people describe as safe, though it is comfortably serviced and rated investment grade.

The dividend is the genuine attraction. McDonald's has raised its dividend every year for 50 consecutive years, currently $1.86 a quarter, or $7.44 a year — a 2.8% yield. But note the strain: in 2025, dividends took 71% of the company's spare cash, and dividends plus share buybacks took 99.8% of it. There is nothing left over, and the company plans to spend MORE on building restaurants over the next two years.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $281. They cost $268.34. That is a gap of under 5%. The average professional analyst thinks $335 — and not one of the 62 analysts covering this company has a target below today's price, which is unusual and, if anything, a warning that the optimism is crowded. When our number is far below the crowd's and the gap to today's price is small, the correct answer is to wait.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

McDonald's "franchises and owns and operates McDonald's restaurants, which serve a locally relevant menu of quality food and beverages in communities across more than 100 countries." Of the 45,356 restaurants at year-end 2025, approximately 95% were franchised — and at 2026-03-31 the count was 45,699, still approximately 95% franchised. Chief executive Christopher J. Kempczinski; over 150,000 Company employees, approximately 70% based outside the US, plus "over two million individuals who work in McDonald's franchised restaurants around the world" who are not McDonald's employees. Headquarters Chicago; NYSE-listed; a Dow Jones Industrial Average member since 1985; approximately 5,100,000 shareholders of record and beneficial owners as of 2026-01-31.

The three reportable segments (10-Q, Segment Information note)

  • U.S. — the largest single market, 95% franchised as of 2026-03-31.
  • International Operated Markets — markets where the Company owns, operates and franchises restaurants, "including Australia, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Poland, Spain and the U.K." — 89% franchised.
  • International Developmental Licensed Markets & Corporate — "primarily developmental licensee and affiliate markets... including equity method investments in China and Japan, as well as Corporate activities", comprising over 75 countries — 99% franchised.

The chief operating decision makers are the CEO and the Global CFO jointly, and segment performance is evaluated on one measure: operating income.

The revenue cut that matters more — and that the vendor does not supply

This is the single most important disclosure-structure point in the file. McDonald's revenue splits two completely different ways, and only one of them is in the vendor payload:

1. By segment / geography — US, International Operated Markets, International Developmental Licensed Markets & Corporate. This is what seg_prod and seg_geo both contain.

2. By ownership structure — Franchised revenues (rent + royalties + initial fees) versus Company-owned and operated sales versus Other revenues (technology-platform recovery fees and brand licensing). This cut is in NEITHER vendor field, and the 10-K table that carries it is a numeric table, which this extraction strips. The dollar split is therefore NOT AVAILABLE to this dive from any source, and no figure for it appears anywhere below.

Why that matters more than any other missing number here: the ownership mix is what drives the margin. The 10-K is explicit — "The contributions to operating margin differ by segment due to each segment's ownership structure, primarily due to the relative percentage of franchised versus Company-owned and operated restaurants." We can bound it qualitatively from prose the extraction did keep:

  • "Franchised margins represented approximately 90% of restaurant margin dollars" (10-K, 2025) and "more than 90%" (10-Q, Q1 2026).
  • "Total restaurant margins increased 5% (3% in constant currencies)" in 2025.
  • "Company-owned and operated margins reflected the impact of ongoing inflationary cost pressures in the U.S. and the International Operated Markets. The U.S. was impacted by negative sales performance for the year."

Read that last sentence carefully. Company-operated sales in the US went DOWN in 2025 — the 10-K says it twice, once under Revenues ("Revenue growth in the U.S. was driven by positive franchised sales performance, partly offset by negative Company-owned and operated sales performance") and once under Restaurant Margins. That is the 5% of US restaurants McDonald's runs itself, and it is the only part of the estate where the company sees the customer's wallet directly.

This is not an accusation. It is a reading of the file, and the file is precise about the distinction. McDonald's defines the terms itself: "Comparable sales and comparable guest counts are compared to the same period in the prior year and represent sales and transactions, respectively", and "Comparable sales are driven by changes in guest counts and average check, the latter of which is affected by changes in pricing and product mix." The company measures guest counts. It has a defined term for them. It reports comparable sales and it explains them by check.

And it says what it wants to happen, in the future tense: the strategy is "supported by a strong global senior leadership team aimed at executing against the MCD growth pillars, further developing our three platforms and driving long-term growth through increasing guest counts and growing industry market share." Increasing guest counts is the goal, stated in both the 10-K and the 10-Q. It is not reported as an outcome anywhere in either.

Three things follow.

First, the improvement in the US comp sequence is real and should not be dismissed. +0.5% for nine months of 2025 to +3.9% in the March 2026 quarter is a genuine four-hundred-basis-point acceleration, and it happened while the market was marking the stock down 15.8% over six months. Something is working. The 10-K describes what: "a marketing strategy that highlights value at every tier of the menu, as providing delicious and affordable menu options remains a cornerstone of the McDonald's brand. This includes everyday low-price options on our menu, affordable meal bundles, limited-time deals and personalized value and digital offers available in our mobile app."

Second, the company itself concedes the environment. In both the 10-K and the Q3 10-Q, verbatim: "the current industry-wide challenges associated with more discerning consumer spending." That is management's own characterisation of demand, and we half-weight it as management voice — but it points the same way as the bearish half of our knowledge-base lane, which is the more notable fact.

Third, the one place the company sees the customer directly, it lost ground. The 5% of US restaurants McDonald's operates itself had negative sales performance in 2025, disclosed twice. Franchised sales in the US grew; the company's own stores did not. That is not conclusive — company-operated units are not a representative sample and the estate is being refranchised — but it is the only unmediated read on the US customer in the entire file, and it is the wrong sign.

Why this section is the hinge of the whole dive. If US comps are check-led because McDonald's has pricing power over a loyal customer, this is a 21%-off entry into a defensive compounder and the street's $335 target is reasonable. If US comps are check-led because the company is raising prices into flat-to-falling traffic, then the comp line is borrowing from the future and the de-rating is early, not wrong. The file does not resolve it. Neither do we. And a name whose central question is unresolved by its own disclosure does not get a Buy.

Net debt of $53.771 billion against $14.832 billion of trailing EBITDA is 3.63x. That is not a fortress. For comparison: KLA, in the same batch, carries 0.16x. McDonald's is a levered company that is described as a defensive one, and both descriptions are correct at once — the leverage is serviceable because the earnings do not move (beta 0.419, interest coverage 7.86x, ratings BBB+ and Baa1) rather than because it is small.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth on the rolling forward line (+7.0%, from FY2027E $14.072 to FY2028E $15.055) + multiple drift (−2.2%, from today's 19.07x to 18.66x on the then-forward year) + dividend yield (+2.77%) ≈ +7.6%. Add roughly 1.1% of buyback and the shareholder-yield-inclusive figure is nearer +8.7%.

Our base case assumes NO re-rating, and in fact a whisker of further compression on the roll. We should be explicit about why.

The case for expansion, which we are declining to underwrite: the multiple has already fallen from roughly 23.5x to 20.8x on the FY2026 line in twelve months, and mean reversion in a business whose earnings did not deteriorate is a perfectly respectable argument. The street is making exactly this argument — a $335.47 consensus target is 22.1x FY2028E, i.e. it assumes the multiple recovers most of what it lost.

The case for holding or compressing, which is ours: the growth rate does not support a higher multiple, and the return on capital is falling. Consensus EPS growth on the credible lines is +8.0%, +9.1%, +7.0% for 2026, 2027, 2028 — a business growing high single digits. Company-disclosed ROIC has fallen 25.2% → 21.8% → 20.3%. And the free cash flow that funds the dividend is already fully committed at 99.8% of returns, with capital expenditure guided up in both 2026 and 2027. A high-single-digit grower with falling incremental returns and no free cash flow slack is a 17-20x business, not a 23-25x business, and the market spent the last twelve months deciding the same thing.

Almost all of the modelled return is EPS growth plus the dividend, and the multiple is a mild headwind. If the multiple simply HELD at today's 19.07x on FY2028E, the price would be $287 (+7.0%); if it re-rated to 22x it would be $331 (+23.5%); if it compressed to 16x it would be $241 (−10.2%). That $90 spread on a business whose earnings are among the most predictable in the S&P 500 is the whole risk here, and it is not a risk we have any edge on.

Where MCD fits in the Synthos Framework Portfolio. The defensive consumer / dividend-compounder sleeve, at 0% today with a 2-3% target on a fill near $240 or a guest-count disclosure. On the batch-construction question: McDonald's is the closest thing in this cohort to a bond substitute with an equity coupon — 0.419 beta, 2.8% yield, 50 years of increases, 46% operating margin — and its value in a portfolio is precisely that it does not correlate with the semiconductor complex that dominates the rest of the batch. The argument FOR owning it is that a 21% drawdown in a business that grew earnings 5% is the sort of dislocation that a patient book exists to buy. The argument AGAINST is that the drawdown has a reason, the reason is unresolved, and the entry today pays only 4.7% to our own number. That tension is exactly why this is a Watch rather than a Hold: we want the exposure, at a price, and we have named the price. Logged as a tracked Synthos call (Watch) as of 2026-08-04 at $268.34.

Single biggest risk: that US comparable sales are price, not people. Every US comp disclosed in this archive — +0.5%, +2.4%, +2.1%, +3.9% — is attributed by the company to check growth. None is attributed to guest counts, and the 10-K names "increasing guest counts" as a strategic goal in the future tense. The knowledge base's most-skilled voice claims that "Chipotle, McDonald's, Walmart confirm double-digit low-end traffic declines." The company's own language concedes "the current industry-wide challenges associated with more discerning consumer spending." If pricing power against a squeezed low-income customer is exhausted, comps turn negative on flat-to-falling traffic, and there is no volume growth underneath to catch it — because there never was any. At 19.1x FY2027 consensus with a fully-committed cash flow and a multiple already compressing, a negative US comp does not produce a proportionate price decline; it produces a de-rating on top of it, which is what the $221 bear case is.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that a 46.1% operating margin merely holds while unit growth accelerates. Every other assumption in Section 6a is a volume or arithmetic assumption and is observable quarter by quarter. This one is a margin assumption made while capital expenditure rises 43% over two years to $3.7-3.9 billion, while approximately 2,600 restaurants a year open into a target of 50,000 by end-2027, while company-operated US sales are already negative and reflecting "ongoing inflationary cost pressures", and while return on invested capital has fallen 490 basis points in three years. Margin has held so far, and the franchised annuity is the reason it should keep holding. But it is being asked to hold through the fastest unit build in company history, funded by a cash flow with no slack in it. If operating margin slips to 44% on the FY2027 consensus revenue of $30.005 billion, operating income falls roughly $630 million, EPS falls to roughly $13.40, and at 19x that is $255 — 5% below today's price.
