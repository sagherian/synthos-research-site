The Overview

McDonald's does not really sell hamburgers. Independent business owners sell the hamburgers; McDonald's owns the brand and, very often, the land underneath, and takes a cut of the sales plus rent. That is why a company with $27 billion of revenue keeps 46 cents of operating profit on every dollar — the franchisees carry the staff, the equipment and the risk.

The business is fine. Last year sales at existing restaurants rose 3.1% worldwide. Every region was positive. The company generated $7.2 billion of spare cash and handed essentially all of it back to shareholders as dividends and buybacks.

But there is a catch in the American numbers, and the company itself puts it in the annual report: US sales rose 2.1% "benefiting from average check growth." Translated: people spent more per visit. It does not say more people visited. An independent analyst in our research base — the single most highly weighted commentator we track — has been arguing since late 2025 that lower-income customers are cutting back sharply, and named McDonald's specifically in May 2026 as a stock near its lows signalling a weakening economy. He has been right. The shares are down 11% over twelve months while the stock market is up 24%.

Two things happened this morning, both in filings dated today.

One: the company reported its April-to-June results. Profit per share of $3.38 beat what analysts expected. Sales of $7,099 million came in slightly below the $7,129 million expected — a small miss, but the first in five quarters.

Two: the executive in charge of McDonald's United States — the biggest part of the company — gave one day's notice and left the same day. He is being replaced by a 26-year company veteran. The filing gives no reason.

The price is $268.34. That is about 19 times what the company is expected to earn in 2027 — cheap by its own recent history, since buyers paid $341 for the same expected earnings within the last year. It pays a 2.74% dividend and buys back roughly another 1.1% of itself annually.

Our estimate of fair value is $293, about 9% above the price. Analysts on average say $335. If we are right, the downside to a genuine consumer recession is about 15% and the upside is about 30% — a favourable ratio.

We are still saying watch, not buy. Three reasons: 9% is not a lot of upside for the trouble; the most reliable bear on this name has been right all year and nothing in today's numbers proves him wrong; and a company does not usually change the head of its biggest division on an hour's notice when things are going well.

  • Exponential Potential 2/10. None. A superb toll on a saturated market.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Franchised margins are 90.7% of the $15,352M restaurant-level total, and company-operated margins are FALLING — $1,517M, $1,447M, $1,422M across three years. The franchise engine is doing all the work and more. Comparable sales, fiscal 2025, from the 10-K verbatim:

> "In 2025, global comparable sales increased 3.1%. Comparable sales in the U.S. increased 2.1%, benefiting from average check growth. Comparable sales in the International Operated Markets increased 3.2%, reflecting positive comparable sales in nearly all markets, led by Germany and Australia. Read the second sentence carefully. The company attributes the US result to average check growth and does not claim traffic growth. That single clause is the pivot of the bear case in Section 7, and it is the company's own language.

Restructuring is recurring rather than one-off. Fiscal 2025 carried "net pre-tax charges of $229 million, or $0.25 per share, primarily related to restructuring charges associated with the Company's internal effort to modernize ways of working (Accelerating the Organization)", and fiscal 2024 carried $221 million, also $0.25 per share, for the same programme, plus $70 million ($0.08) for the sale of the South Korea business and the acquisition of the Israel business. Two consecutive years at exactly $0.25 per share of "restructuring" is a run-rate cost being presented as an adjustment, and this dive treats it as such: the GAAP EPS of $11.95 is the honest trailing figure and the adjusted $12.20 is the one the estimates are struck on.
