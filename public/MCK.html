The Overview

Almost every prescription drug sold in America passes through one of three companies. McKesson is the largest. It buys drugs from the manufacturers and delivers them to pharmacies, hospitals and doctors' offices, and it keeps a very thin slice — about three and a half cents on every dollar of goods, before costs.

That sounds like a bad business until you look at how the money moves. McKesson gets paid by its customers in about 25 days. It pays its suppliers in about 56 days. So for a month, it is holding other people's money. It needs almost no capital of its own, and as a result it earns 31 cents of profit per year on every dollar of capital actually invested — a figure most software companies would be pleased with.

Last year it moved $403 billion of product, up 12%, and earned $39.15 per share. It also bought back $4.8 billion of its own stock, which is about 5% of the entire company in a single year, so each remaining share owns more of the business than it did.

Two things are happening now that the standard financial data does not show.

The first is that McKesson is splitting itself up. It has decided to separate its medical-supplies business — gloves, syringes, lab equipment, sold to doctors' offices and nursing homes — into a separate company. It has already sold a 13% stake in that business to the investment firm Apollo for about $1.25 billion, and in June it borrowed $2.25 billion against it. Businesses do not get financed that way unless they are being prepared to leave. What remains would be a cleaner, faster-growing company focused on drugs, cancer care and healthcare software.

The second is that the shares have gone nowhere for six months — down 2%, while the market rose 11% — so the price is now about 19 times next year's expected profit, for a company expected to grow those profits 13-14% a year.

Our estimate of fair value is $930, about 12% above the $830.43 price. The average analyst says $1,006, and the most pessimistic analyst says $875 — which is still above today's price. We are deliberately more cautious than all of them.

There is one very important piece of timing: McKesson announces its latest quarterly results tomorrow. Nothing in this document can know what they will say. Our rating is a Buy, but with an explicit condition attached — do not buy before that announcement.

The main things that could go wrong: CVS, the pharmacy chain, is 24% of everything McKesson sells, and losing it would be catastrophic. The company has bought back so much stock that its accounting net worth is negative $2.2 billion — harmless while it generates $6 billion of cash a year, dangerous if that ever stops. And it remains a defendant in opioid litigation, which never fully goes away.

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced.
