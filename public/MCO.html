The Overview

When a company or a government borrows money by issuing a bond, investors want an independent opinion on how likely it is to be repaid. Two firms provide most of those opinions worldwide: Moody's and S&P Global. Moody's rates roughly 40% of the world's debt.

This is an unusually good business, and the reason is simple. A rating from Moody's typically lowers a borrower's interest cost by more than the rating fee. So issuers pay, and they keep paying, and a new entrant cannot easily persuade investors to accept an unfamiliar opinion. Our knowledge base contains five separate expert claims about this company and every one is positive. One calls it "an unassailable protocol-network-effect moat" and says it is "ownable for 30+ years."

Moody's also owns a second business selling credit data, research and risk software. It is about as large as the ratings business by the vendor's labelling and it is growing much more slowly — 4% in the most recent quarter.

The recent quarter looked spectacular: earnings per share of $5.02 against $3.20 a year earlier, up 57%. But reading the actual filing shows the company sold a business during the quarter — MA Regulatory Solutions — and booked a gain on it. Strip the below-the-line items and the operating profit grew 24%. Still very good; not 57%.

The shares cost $483.67, which is about 25.6 times what analysts expect Moody's to earn in 2027. Those same analysts expect earnings to grow about 12% a year. Paying 25.6 times for 12% growth is a full price for a great business.

Our estimate of fair value is $491 — essentially where the shares already trade. In a bad case they fall to about $360; in a good case they reach about $619. That is roughly one dollar of possible gain per dollar of possible loss. So: Hold.

There is one thing worth knowing about the risk. This business does not lose customers; it loses volume. In 2022, when interest rates rose and companies stopped issuing bonds, Moody's revenue fell 12% and its profit fell 38% — in a single year, with the moat entirely intact. That is the shape of the downside.

Two data notes. The vendor's file reports Moody's United States revenue as $472 million. The correct figure is about $4,171 million — nearly nine times larger — because the file has mislabelled a residual line. And the file's own reports for this company contain no financial tables at all, so we could not check its capital-spending figure against the original documents; the number it implies has fallen 75% in a year, which we flag without being able to resolve.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Why it is a Hold and not a Buy, in three items.

First, the arithmetic. At $483.67 the shares are 28.6x FY2026 consensus and 25.6x FY2027's $18.90 for 11.7% forecast EPS growth — a PEG near 2.2. Base fair value $491, 1.6% above spot; bear $360; bull $619; asymmetry 1.09:1.

Second, the most recent quarter is not what the headline says. Diluted EPS of $5.02 against $3.20 is +56.9%. Operating income grew 24.1%. The 10-Q attributes the difference to "the gain on the divestiture of the MA Regulatory Solutions business", and the residual between operating and pre-tax income swung $216 million favourably. The vendor payload contains no trace of the transaction.

Third, the thing that is supposed to de-risk the franchise is not doing so. Moody's Analytics grew 4% reported (8% organic constant currency) while the group grew 15.1%, and the company has just divested part of it. The knowledge base's own base case is that Analytics "smooths cyclicality" by lifting recurring revenue to 68%. At 4% growth it is diluting rather than smoothing, which means the earnings remain levered to debt issuance — and FY2022 shows what that means: revenue −12.1%, net income −37.9%, with the moat intact.

And the honest counterweight. The knowledge base's moat argument is correct, and a business with genuine terminal-value certainty deserves to be owned rather than traded. A reader who already holds this should hold it: the franchise is not impaired, the buyback is retiring 2% of the shares a year, and paying 25.6x for a duopoly is not a mistake — it is simply not an opportunity. A reader who does not hold it is being asked to accept a 1.09:1 payoff for the privilege.
