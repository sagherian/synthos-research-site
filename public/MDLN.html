The Overview

Medline makes and distributes the everyday physical supplies a hospital runs on — gloves, gowns, drapes, wound dressings, surgical trays, sanitiser, incontinence products, basic instruments — and also runs the warehouses and delivery networks that get them there. It employs 45,000 people and sells about $29 billion a year. It was owned by private-equity firms and was floated on the stock market in December 2025.

Two things are going well. Sales are growing about 10% a year, which is genuinely good for a business of this size. And the company used the flotation money to pay down debt: it cut borrowings by about $6 billion, and its quarterly interest bill has dropped from $233 million to $165 million. That saving — roughly $270 million a year — goes straight to profit without the business having to do anything more.

Three things are going badly, and together they are why we are not buying. First, the profit margin on what it sells is shrinking: a year ago it kept 24.8 cents of gross profit per dollar of sales, and in the most recent quarter it kept 22.6 cents. On $29 billion of sales, that difference is about $640 million — more than the company's entire annual profit. Second, one of the private-equity owners sold about $803 million of shares in May at $36.54, roughly 10% below today's price, and still holds around 100 million shares that may come to market. Third, the company discloses remarkably little: the data contains no breakdown of sales by product or by country at all, and the shares have not traded long enough to have a one-year track record.

Our estimate of fair value is $41. The shares cost $40.47. Results are announced tomorrow. That is not a buy.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

1. Data integrity — what we rejected, and what is simply missing

REJECTED — the FY2025 annual EBITDA figure

inc_a for FY2025 reports ebitda: 1,952,000,000 against operatingIncome: 2,212,000,000. EBITDA cannot be below operating income — depreciation and amortisation are added, not subtracted. Two further checks confirm the corruption:

  • The quarterly EBITDA figures for FY25 sum to $2,849M ($799M + $842M + $826M + $382M), not $1,952M.
  • FY2024 reported EBITDA of $3,532M on lower revenue and near-identical operating income ($2,225M). A fall to $1,952M would be inexplicable.

We use $2,849M as the FY25 EBITDA reference and label it as the quarterly sum.

REJECTED — the Q4'25 quarterly EBITDA figure

inc_q for 2025-12-31 reports ebitda: 382,000,000 against operatingIncome: 425,000,000 — the same impossibility at quarterly level, and starkly out of line with the surrounding quarters ($799M, $842M, $826M, and $681M in March 2026). Rejected. Note that the $2,849M quarterly sum above therefore itself contains one bad quarter and is likely understated; a cleaner estimate using the March 2026 quarter's $681M in place of the corrupt $382M gives roughly $3,148M, which is exactly the FY26E consensus EBITDA. We use $2,849M conservatively and flag the likely understatement.

REJECTED — the trailing capital-expenditure and free-cash-flow metrics

km_ttm reports capexToRevenueTTM: 0.00017158 (0.017% of revenue) and capexToDepreciationTTM: 0.0075 — implying trailing capital expenditure of roughly $5M against $447M actually reported in FY25 and $364M in FY24. The trailing capex field is corrupt, and the consequence is that priceToFreeCashFlowRatioTTM (22.4x), freeCashFlowYieldTTM (4.46%), freeCashFlowPerShareTTM ($1.862) and evToFreeCashFlowTTM (29.3x) are ALL OVERSTATED and are discarded.

Our recomputation from cf_a FY25: operating cash flow $1,744M less capital expenditure $447M = free cash flow $1,297M, a 3.79% free-cash-flow yield on the $34.22B market capitalisation — not the 4.46% the vendor reports. Labelled as our computation.

REJECTED — the reported beta

profile.beta reads −0.41062886. A negative beta for a medical-supply distributor is not an economic fact; it is a regression artifact from under eight months of price history since the 2025-12-17 listing. Corroborating evidence of insufficient history: tech.sma200 is null and tech.ret_12m is null. The beta is unusable and no risk statement in this dive relies on it.

FLAGGED — stock-based compensation reported as zero in every year

cf_a reports stockBasedCompensation: 0 for FY22, FY23, FY24 and FY25. For a newly public company with 45,000 employees this is implausible; it is more likely an artifact of the pre-IPO private-company reporting basis. Flagged; it means the reported free cash flow of $1,297M may be flattered by an undisclosed non-cash compensation charge, and we cannot size it.

FLAGGED — the FY2025 equity presentation is not comparable to prior years

bal_a FY2025 shows totalEquity: 19,286M comprising totalStockholdersEquity: 10,737M and minorityInterest: 8,549M. FY2024 and FY2023 both report zero minority interest. The non-controlling interest appears for the first time in FY25, reflecting the IPO reorganisation into what appears to be an Up-C-style structure. FY25 equity is therefore not comparable with FY24, and price-to-book metrics across the transition are meaningless.

FLAGGED — share count inconsistency

inc_q Q1'26 gives 819.0M basic and 825.0M diluted shares. The quote's market capitalisation of $34,221.7M at $40.47 implies 845.6M shares — a 2.5% discrepancy. km_ttm.revenuePerShareTTM of $35.580 against trailing revenue of $29,140M implies 819.0M. We use 825M diluted for per-share arithmetic and flag the spread, noting that with $8,549M of non-controlling interest on the balance sheet the true economic count may be higher than either figure.

SEVERE GAP — seg_prod and seg_geo are both entirely empty

There is no product revenue split and no geographic revenue split for a $29.1 billion-revenue business — not for FY25, not for any year. The profile describes two divisions (Medline Brand and Supply Chain Solutions) and three Medline Brand categories (Frontline Care, Surgical Solutions, and a third), and none of them appear in the data with a revenue figure attached.

This is the most consequential gap in the file. It means we cannot determine whether the gross-margin compression documented in §3 is concentrated in manufactured products or in distribution, whether it is domestic or import-driven, or whether it is mix or price. We flag explicitly that the central operating question in this dive cannot be answered from this data, and no claim below rests on a segment split.

Any investor relying on the adjusted series here is relying on an adjustment equal in size to the reported profit.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over a two-year horizon: EBITDA growth (+8% annually, consensus) + deleveraging transfer + multiple drift + shareholder yield (zero — no dividend, no buyback).

The deleveraging transfer is the distinctive feature of this bridge and deserves explicit treatment. At an enterprise value of $44.84B and net debt of $10.62B, equity is 76.3% of the capital structure. Each $1 of debt repaid transfers $1 of enterprise value to equity holders at a constant EV/EBITDA multiple. With free cash flow of roughly $1.3B annually, that is potentially ~$1.30 per share per year of value transfer, or 3.2% — if the cash goes to debt rather than to working capital. The three-year record of flat free cash flow against 22.4% revenue growth is the reason to be sceptical that it will.

Our base case assumes modest multiple COMPRESSION: we apply 13.0x FY27E EBITDA against the 13.2x the market pays for FY27E today. The justification is that a business with a decelerating top line (+9.3% to +7.4% on consensus), a compressing gross margin, 3.4x leverage and 2.6x interest coverage is not a candidate for re-rating, and that 14.2x FY26E is already a full multiple for a distributor.

So the base-case return of roughly +1% is: ~8% of EBITDA growth plus a ~3% deleveraging transfer, almost entirely offset by a modest de-rate and by the fact that the starting price already reflects both. There is no yield leg (no dividend, no buyback). The bull case at $51 explicitly requires multiple EXPANSION to 15.5x — which needs gross margin to recover and the sponsor overhang to clear. That is the fragile leg and it requires two independent things to go right.

9. Moat and competitive position

Medline's advantage is vertical integration at scale: it manufactures and sources its own branded products and runs the distribution network that delivers them, so it captures both the product margin and the logistics margin on the same shipment. At $29.1B of revenue with 45,000 employees it is one of a small number of participants with national scale in the United States, and hospital supply relationships are sticky — they involve contracted formularies, integrated ordering systems and clinician familiarity.

The evidence the moat prices: revenue growth of 8-11% annually for four consecutive years at a $21-29B scale, which requires taking share.

The evidence the moat is under pressure: gross margin fell 220 basis points year-over-year in the most recent quarter, and operating margin fell 316 basis points (8.90% to 5.74%). A business with genuine pricing power does not surrender 220 basis points of gross margin while growing revenue 10.7%. The most likely readings are input-cost inflation on globally sourced product, or procurement pressure from consolidating health systems — and the empty segment disclosure prevents distinguishing them.

Moat: real in scale and integration, currently not visible in the margin. That combination — a structural advantage that is not showing up in the numbers — is exactly the situation in which a full multiple is dangerous.
