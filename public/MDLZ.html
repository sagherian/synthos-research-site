The Overview

Mondelez makes Oreo biscuits, Cadbury chocolate, Toblerone and Trident gum, and sells them in about 150 countries.

For two years it has been hit by an extraordinary rise in the price of cocoa. It responded by putting up prices, which worked on revenue and hurt volumes — especially chocolate in Europe, where people simply bought less.

The results published on 28 July look, at first glance, spectacular: profit per share more than doubled. That is not real. The company buys financial contracts to fix the price of cocoa and currencies, and accounting rules require it to revalue those contracts every quarter. This quarter the revaluation went its way. Strip it out — as the company itself does, and as every analyst does — and profit per share was exactly flat, and slightly down once you allow for currency movements. The profit margin on that honest basis fell, from 14.3% to 13.1%.

Two things tell you what management actually thinks.

They raised the dividend 4%. And they almost stopped buying back their own shares: about $210 million in the first six months of this year, against $2,385 million in the whole of last year — a cut of roughly 91%, with $6.5 billion of permission still unused. Companies that believe their shares are cheap and their earnings are about to recover do not do that.

Analysts expect profits of about $3.05 per share this year. The company has earned $1.40 in the first half. Analysts think the third quarter will be slightly worse than last year. That leaves the fourth quarter needing to be about 29% better than last year's — all at once. It could happen: the cocoa hedges the company bought at high prices are gradually expiring and being replaced at lower ones. But it is a lot to ask.

At $62.08 the shares cost about 20 times this year's expected earnings and pay a 3.35% dividend. Our estimate of fair value is $65, about 5% above the price. Analysts average $69.89.

A note on our own data: every one of the six regulatory filings we hold for this company came through with the financial tables stripped out. We could still check the capital spending, because the company writes those numbers into the text, and they were exactly right. We could not check the balance sheet, and we say so rather than guess.

  • Growth Quality 4/10. Reported growth is price and currency. Adjusted profit per share is falling.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. Biscuits and chocolate. No mechanism, and a live argument that the category shrinks.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Meanwhile the dividend went the other way. From the 10-Q: "On July 28, 2026, the Audit Committee, with authorization delegated from our Board of Directors, declared a quarterly cash dividend of $0.52 per share of Class A Common Stock, an increase of 4%." Annualised $2.08, a 3.35% yield.

The honest reading, stated as a question rather than an accusation. Management raised the payout it must sustain and cut the one it can vary, while the stock traded between $51.51 and $64.99. A company that believed its own consensus — a 29% fourth-quarter earnings increase and a 2027 number 15.8% above 2025 — would be buying stock at $60.69, not stopping. The alternative explanation is balance-sheet discipline: net debt of $20,278 million at roughly 3.0x adjusted EBITDA is high for a staple, and preserving it while cocoa is unresolved is defensible. 10. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Hold.

The arithmetic. At $62.08 Mondelez trades at 20.4x the 2026 consensus of $3.048 and 18.5x 2027's $3.350, with a 3.35% dividend just raised 4% and a buyback cut by roughly 91%. Our base of $65 is 4.7% above spot with 1.21:1 asymmetry, and total shareholder yield is approximately 3.9% — down from approximately 6.1% on the fiscal-2025 combination. At an unchanged multiple the twelve-month return is the yield and nothing else.

Why this is not a Buy. The 2026 consensus requires a fourth quarter roughly 29% above last year's, after a first half in which adjusted earnings per share FELL 4.8% (8.8% on a constant-currency basis) and against a third-quarter estimate that is itself a year-on-year decline. The mechanism that would deliver it — cocoa hedges rolling off at lower contracted prices — is described in the filing and is not dated, and the same filing says cocoa "is expected to remain elevated compared to historical levels in the near- and medium-term." Adjusted operating margin fell 120 basis points in the quarter. Developed-market organic growth is 0.8%. European chocolate volumes are declining more slowly rather than recovering. And management cut the buyback by 91% while the stock sat 20% below its high, which is the clearest available statement of its own confidence.

Why this is not an Avoid. These are among the most durable consumer brands in existence, in a business with genuine pricing power that lifted revenue 6.2% in the half on essentially flat volume. Emerging markets — 33% of revenue at the segment level and rising — grew organic revenue 5.3%. The dividend is covered at 82% of trailing free cash flow and was just raised. The stock has already fallen 2.9% over twelve months against an index up 24.3%, a 27-point deficit, and has had a genuine 20.8% drawdown — the only one in this batch. The bad news is substantially in the price; what is not in the price is a second year of it.
