The Overview

Medtronic makes medical devices: pacemakers and defibrillators for the heart, spinal implants, surgical staplers, brain stimulators, insulin pumps. It is one of the two or three largest companies of its kind, employs about 95,000 people, and sells $36 billion a year.

For roughly a decade its problem has been simple: it did not grow. Sales rose about 3.5% a year — barely above inflation — while it spent very large sums buying other companies. The result is a business where every dollar invested earns about 6 cents a year, which is less than the cost of the money. That, and not anything about the products, is why the shares trade at half the price-to-earnings ratio of its competitors.

In the year to April 2026 something changed. Sales rose 8.4%, and they accelerated as the year went on. More importantly, the company's annual report says exactly where the growth came from, product by product:

  • The heart-rhythm division grew 17%, driven by pulsed-field ablation — a new way of treating irregular heartbeats that uses electrical fields instead of heat, damaging less surrounding tissue. This is a genuine technology change in the field, and it is early.
  • The vascular division grew 5%, helped by renal denervation — a catheter procedure for high blood pressure that resists drugs. Medtronic spent more than a decade getting this approved and adopted.
  • The diabetes division grew 13%.

And the company did something else: on 9 March 2026 it floated the diabetes business separately, as MiniMed Group, selling about 10% and keeping 90%, and it says it intends to dispose of the rest. That is a sensible decision — the one piece of research in our knowledge base that mentions Medtronic argues that continuous glucose monitoring is a two-company market (Dexcom and Abbott) that Medtronic cannot break into. Selling a business you cannot win, while it is growing 13% and therefore worth something, is good management.

The shares cost $86.20. That is about 14.5 times what analysts expect for the year to April 2027 — compared with more than 25 times for similar companies. They are 18% below their high of the last year and 36% below their all-time peak. They pay a 3.31% dividend, which costs about two-thirds of the company's spare cash. Of the 51 analysts who follow it, 31 say buy, 20 say hold, and none says sell.

Our estimate of fair value is $100 — about 16% above the price, and with the dividend a total of roughly 19%. If we are wrong, our downside case is $75, about 13% below.

One important warning about the data. Medtronic's filings in our archive contain no tables — only text — so we could not check its balance sheet against the filed accounts. And the geographic revenue data we were sold reports United States sales of $71.4 billion for a company whose total sales are $36.4 billion. That figure is simply wrong, and we have discarded it.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
