The Overview

3M makes tens of thousands of physical products — sandpaper, sticky tape, masks and respirators, road signs, roofing granules, electrical insulation, Post-it notes — out of a small number of underlying material technologies it has been refining for a century.

For several years it was a mess. In 2023 it lost $12.63 per share, mostly on legal settlements. It then spun off its healthcare business, and in 2024 it hired a chief executive from outside the company for the first time in a long while.

The turnaround is working. Profits per share have beaten analysts' forecasts five quarters in a row. Operating profit is 18.3 cents on every revenue dollar. And the company is returning an enormous amount of cash: it bought back $4.8 billion of its own stock last year — about 5% of the entire company — and pays a dividend. Together that is roughly 6.8% of the share price returned to shareholders in a year, which is very high.

The problem is the price. The shares rose 2.4% today, are up 28% in the last three months, and now sit within half a percent of the highest price they have reached in a year. Analysts on average think they are worth $185. They cost $181.45. Our own estimate is $186. Everybody, including us, thinks the shares are worth roughly what they cost.

Two things to understand before deciding.

The buyback is bigger than the cash the business generates. Last year 3M reported $3.25 billion of profit but only $1.4 billion of free cash after investment, because it is still writing cheques for legal settlements. It spent $4.8 billion on buybacks and about $1.6 billion on dividends. The difference came off the balance sheet. That is fine for a while; it is not a permanent arrangement.

And the legal exposure has no end date. 3M stopped making PFAS chemicals at the end of 2025 and sold the German subsidiary that made them in June 2026 — but its own filing says it remains responsible for claims from before that sale. It also says regulators are setting limits "at progressively lower levels, in some cases approaching limits that exceed current analytical detection capabilities" — that is, thresholds so low that current instruments cannot reliably measure them — and that some jurisdictions are proposing laws to let governments recover public healthcare costs. That is a liability nobody can size, and the most pessimistic analyst covering the stock thinks it is worth $145, about 20% below today's price.

A genuinely improving business, returning a lot of cash, at a fair price, over a legal exposure nobody can measure. That is a Hold. If you own it, the 6.8% return of capital is a good reason to keep it. If you do not, our number is $150.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

> "In December 2022, the Company announced its intent to exit all PFAS manufacturing and to work toward discontinuing PFAS use across its product portfolio by the end of 2025. The Company completed its exit from PFAS manufacturing by the end of 2025."

> "In June 2026, the Company's German subsidiary, 3M Deutschland GmbH... completed the divestiture of its subsidiary, Dyneon GmbH... Under the terms of the transaction, 3M Deutschland remains responsible for PFAS-related claims arising from activities conducted prior to the divestiture of Dyneon, and is coordinating the response to such claims."

The liability did not go with the business. That is the fact the June 2026 divestiture does and does not change.

And the exposure is described as widening, not closing:

> "Regulatory and legislative activities relating to PFAS continue to expand in the United States, Canada, Europe, Asia, Latin America, and other jurisdictions... Regulatory limits for PFAS in emissions and in environmental media (including soil, groundwater, surface water, and drinking water) are being established at progressively lower levels, in some cases approaching limits that exceed current analytical detection capabilities."

> "Regulatory focus has also broadened to include a wider range of PFAS compounds, including substances previously manufactured by the Company prior to its exit from PFAS manufacturing, contained in third-party materials used in the Company's current products, or associated with the Company's legacy manufacturing activities. In certain jurisdictions, proposed legislation may authorize recovery of healthcare or related public costs allegedly associated with PFAS exposure."

> "...as a result of the CERCLA designation of PFOA and PFOS as hazardous substances in 2024... the Company may be required to undertake additional investigative, compliance, and remediation activities... The Company may also face additional litigation for contribution claims sought by other potentially responsible parties."

Two specific open matters with named locations:

  • Zwijndrecht, Antwerp, Belgium — PFAS manufacturing ceased in 2024; a water-discharge permit confirmed by the Flemish government in March 2025 is under judicial appeal. The company states it "cannot predict the outcome" and that if it cannot meet eventual discharge limits it "could have a significant adverse impact on 3M Belgium's normal operations and the Company's businesses that receive products and other materials from the Zwijndrecht Facility, some of which may not be available or available in similar quantities from the Company's other facilities."
  • Decatur, Alabama — an addendum to the 2020 consent order was agreed with the Alabama Department of Environmental Management in May 2026, reflecting the PFAS exit and requiring "limited additional private well water sampling for certain PFAS within a four-mile radius of the Decatur Facility."

Respirator mask and asbestos litigation, including a separate West Virginia matter, also remains in the file.

The analytical conclusion is uncomfortable and should be stated plainly: there is no defensible way to put a terminal value on this. A company whose regulator is setting limits below what instruments can measure, whose exposure now includes substances in third-party materials used in its current products, and which faces proposed legislation authorising public-healthcare cost recovery, has a liability whose distribution is not merely wide but unbounded at one end. The fiscal-2023 loss of $12.63 per share is what one round of that looked like. Every fair value in this dive is conditional on no further round, and that condition is stated rather than assumed.
