The Overview

Monster makes energy drinks. Coca-Cola's trucks deliver them. That arrangement means Monster gets a global distribution network without owning any of it, which is why a company with $8.3 billion of sales employs only 6,332 people and spends about $132 million a year on equipment.

The economics are exceptional. Monster keeps about 56 cents of gross profit on every dollar of sales and about 23 cents of net profit. It has no debt at all, and roughly $3.25 billion of cash and investments sitting on the balance sheet. It earns about 25% a year on shareholders' money.

And it just got faster, not slower. In the March quarter, sales rose 26.9% — 22.1% if you strip out currency moves. That is remarkable for a company this size in a category this old, and it is the reason the stock is up 60% over the past year.

So what is the problem? Price. You pay about 45 times last year's profit and about 36 times what analysts expect for 2027. Analysts expect earnings to grow about 13% a year. Paying 36 times for 13% growth means you are paying nearly three years of growth for one year of it. If everything goes right, you get a decent return. If growth slows back toward the 12% it has averaged over five years, you probably do not.

Two housekeeping items you should know about. First, the company reports results in two days, on August 6. Second, it is splitting its stock two-for-one on August 10 — so on August 11, the price on your screen will be about $47 instead of $94, and you will own twice as many shares. That is a cosmetic change and worth nothing, but it will be confusing if you are not expecting it. Neither event is anywhere in the data feed this analysis is built from; both were found by reading the filings.

Our estimate of value is $95 against a $94 price. That is not a call. It is an admission that we cannot find an edge here, and that saying so is more useful than manufacturing one.

Four observations.

First, the inflection is genuine and it is five quarters old, not one. The year-on-year line runs −2.4%, +11.1%, +16.8%, +17.6%, +26.9%. That is a monotonic acceleration across five consecutive quarters, which is a much stronger pattern than a single surprising print.

Second, the comparison base flatters the headline and we say so. Q1 2025's $1,854.6M was itself down 2.4% from Q1 2024's $1,899.5M. The two-year stack from Q1 2024 to Q1 2026 is +23.9%, or 11.3% annualised — essentially the company's five-year compound rate of 12.5%, not 27%. The currency-adjusted +22.1% is the honest figure for the year, and it is still excellent; the reported +26.9% is not the run rate.
