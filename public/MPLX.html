The Overview

MPLX moves hydrocarbons around. It gathers natural gas from wells in Appalachia and the Midwest, processes it, separates out the valuable liquids, and pipes or barges crude oil and refined fuels around the country. It is owned and controlled by Marathon Petroleum, one of the biggest US refiners, which is both its parent and one of its biggest customers.

It is a very well-run version of this business. It generates $5.9 billion of cash a year and only needs to spend $1.8 billion of that on maintaining and expanding its assets, which leaves $4.1 billion for owners. It pays out $4.19 per unit a year — a 7.11% yield — and last year that payout was just barely covered by cash flow (1.02 times). It carries $24 billion of debt, which sounds enormous but is about three and a half years of cash earnings, and is meaningfully less than its main rival Energy Transfer carries.

The problem is not the business. The problem is the price. The units are within half a percent of their highest level in a year, they have barely fallen at all over the past twelve months, and Wall Street's twenty-eight analysts collectively see it going to about $60 — roughly 2% above where it trades. On the measure that matters for this kind of company, MPLX costs about 65% more than Energy Transfer does. You are paying that premium for safety, which is a real thing to pay for, but not an infinite one.

There is also a specific near-term wrinkle: analysts expect MPLX to earn less this year than last year — $4.29 per unit versus $4.82 — because last year it bought $4 billion of assets using $4 billion of borrowed money, and the interest bill arrives before the new assets' profits do.

Practical thing to know: like Energy Transfer, this is a partnership. You own units, you get a K-1 tax form instead of a 1099, and holding it inside a retirement account can create odd tax consequences. Know that before you buy.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

Two things to take from this table.

First, the capex discipline is remarkable and is the core of the quality case. The acquisitions have to earn their cost of capital, and this dataset does not tell us what was bought.

Three observations that drive the verdict.

First, the FY26 down-year. Consensus EPS of $4.28792 for FY26E is 11.1% below FY25's actual $4.82. 5. The distribution — thin cover, real cover

The distribution: $4.186 per unit trailing-twelve-month, a 7.11% yield at $58.91 — the highest yield in this batch. The GAAP dividend payout ratio is 108.9% and the dividendPaidAndCapexCoverageRatio is 0.98x.

The coverage history on post-capex free cash flow: 1.44x (FY22), 1.40x (FY23), 1.37x (FY24), 1.02x (FY25).

Why FY25 compressed: capex rose 71% from $1.056B to $1.808B while distributions rose 12.9% to $4.018B and operating cash flow was flat at $5.909B. The compression is capex, not deterioration in the underlying business.

The honest read. MPLX's distribution is covered, but only just — and 1.02x is not a comfortable number. What makes it defensible rather than alarming is the shape of the spending: FY25 capex was only 30.6% of operating cash flow (17.0% on the TTM measure) against ET's 62.1%, so there is a large discretionary buffer if the partnership chooses to slow down. What makes it worth watching is that MPLX simultaneously spent $4.031B on acquisitions and repurchased $400M of units in the same year it took coverage to 1.02x. That is three competing claims on the same cash, and the distribution is the one with no flexibility.

Compare to the batch. MPLX 1.02x versus ET 0.81x. MPLX covers, ET does not. That single fact is worth a meaningful part of the 65% valuation premium — and it is why, if you hold only one midstream partnership for income, it should probably be this one.

Partnership mechanics you must know before buying: MPLX issues a Schedule K-1, not a 1099, arriving later than standard tax forms and complicating filing. Distributions are largely return of capital, deferring tax until sale and reducing cost basis. Holding units inside an IRA or other tax-advantaged account can generate unrelated business taxable income (UBTI). The TTM effective tax rate of 0.17% is the structural flip side — the partnership pays essentially no entity-level tax, so the holder does. Diluted unit count has been stable to slightly declining (1,051M in FY20, 1,017M in FY25, 1,017M in Q1'26), helped by the buyback programme — a genuine positive against ET's 11.8% unit growth over a similar span.

8. Moat and competitive position

The moat is physical, regulated and genuinely wide — and it is the same moat every large US midstream operator enjoys. Gathering systems, processing plants, fractionators, pipelines and storage caverns cannot be replicated at scale in the current permitting environment. MPLX's $22.213B of net property, plant and equipment would cost substantially more to rebuild than it is carried for, and the rights-of-way underneath it are functionally irreplaceable.

Two things sharpen MPLX's version of that moat and one thing weakens it.

Sharpening it: the inland marine franchise. MPLX owns and charters boats and barges on the Ohio River and Gulf Coast and runs its own marine repair facility. River logistics is a niche with high barriers, few operators, and demand that does not disappear. It is a genuinely differentiated asset within midstream, and it barely gets discussed.

Sharpening it: the sponsor relationship. Marathon Petroleum as general partner gives MPLX a stable anchor customer, a captive volume base tied to refinery throughput, and a natural acquisition pipeline (dropdowns).

Weakening it: that same sponsor relationship is a concentration. MPC is simultaneously MPLX's controlling general partner and a major customer. This dataset does not disclose what share of MPLX's revenue comes from MPC, so the exposure cannot be sized here — and the inability to size it is itself the risk. A partnership whose largest customer also controls its board has structurally weaker negotiating leverage on tariffs than an independent operator, and the governance protection an outside unitholder has is limited. This is the single most under-quantified item in the dive and is named as the biggest risk accordingly.
