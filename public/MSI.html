The Overview

When a police officer presses the button on a shoulder radio, the odds are Motorola built the radio, the network it talks to, and the software that logs the call. That is the business. It is not the phone company Motorola — that split in two in 2011 — it is the part that sells to governments.

It is a very good business for an unglamorous reason: interoperability. If a county buys Motorola radios, every neighbouring agency it needs to talk to has a strong reason to buy Motorola too, and switching means replacing everything at once. Our knowledge base contains eight separate observations about this company from one detailed podcast episode, and the central one is that Motorola has roughly 80% of that market and sells networks first, then radios forever afterwards, at high margins.

The filings back that up. Last year Motorola made $2,988 million of operating profit on $11,682 million of sales — a 25.6% margin. It has $9.5 billion of work already contracted but not yet delivered, about $4.1 billion of which arrives in the next year. That is close to a guaranteed revenue floor.

Two things have changed since that podcast, and neither is in the data file we were given.

In August last year Motorola paid $4.4 billion in cash for a company called Silvus, which makes radio networks that work without any fixed infrastructure — useful for military and emergency deployments. To put $4.4 billion in perspective: Motorola's own auditor states that Silvus was 2% of last year's revenue. That is a very full price, it was paid in cash, and it roughly doubled Motorola's debt.

The result is visible in the most recent quarter. Sales rose 7.4%. "Adjusted" earnings per share rose 6.0%. And actual, accounting earnings per share fell 13.8% — from $2.53 to $2.18 — because of the interest on the new debt and the accounting charges for writing down what was bought. The gap between the two numbers is now 55% of the real one, up from 26% a year ago.

Two more things you should know. The shares cost $442.85 and are up 1.4% over the past year while the market rose 24.3% — so this has quietly been getting cheaper relative to its earnings. And Motorola reports its next results tomorrow, on a technical reading (RSI 74) that says the stock has run hard into the print.

Analysts expect $18.54 of earnings in 2027, so you are paying about 24 times. Our estimate of fair value is $445 — about the price. The average analyst says $506, and notably the most pessimistic of the 34 analysts says $470, which is above today's price. Nobody covering this stock has a downside case, which we treat as a warning rather than a comfort.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Government customers and a $9.5bn backlog — against net debt of $8.1bn and tangible book value of minus $44 a share.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. 8% revenue growth, radio at 6.8%, video and software at 10-15%.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. Real adjacencies, growing at one-and-a-half times a core that is 74% of the company.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $445 against a current price of $483.04 — a premium price for a business we still like.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

2. Silvus — $4.4 billion for 2% of revenue, and what it did to the earnings

This is the most consequential fact in the file and it appears in no vendor field except a single cash-flow line reading acquisitionsNet: −$4,899,000,000.

From the 10-K:

> "On August 6, 2025, the Company acquired Silvus Technologies Holdings Inc. ('Silvus') from Silvus Technologies Group LLC... Silvus designs and develops software-defined high-speed MANET technology that enables highly secure data, video and voice communications without the need for fixed infrastructure... The purchase price of $4.4 billion consisted of cash payments of $4.4 billion, net of cash acquired and customary purchase price adjustments, and contingent earnout consideration that had an estimated fair value as of the acquisition date of $38 million."

The purchase-price allocation, from the auditor's critical audit matter: "Of the acquired identifiable intangible assets, $820 million of customer relationships and $920 million of developed technology were recorded" — valued respectively by the excess-earnings and relief-from-royalty methods, both flagged as requiring "significant judgment by management."

And the sizing, from the auditor's internal-control report: "management has excluded Silvus Technologies Holdings Inc. ('Silvus') from its assessment of internal control over financial reporting as of December 31, 2025... Silvus is a wholly owned subsidiary whose total assets and total revenues excluded from management's assessment and our audit of internal control over financial reporting represent 1% and 2%, respectively, of the related consolidated financial statement amounts."

$4.4 billion in cash for a business representing 2% of FY2025 revenues — roughly $234 million over the five months from 6 August, or something in the order of $560 million annualised. That is approximately 7.9x annualised revenue, paid in cash, at the same time the knowledge base's highest-conviction capital-allocation claim praised "opportunistic M&A into adjacencies." We are not saying the price was wrong — MANET technology into defence and public safety may well earn it — we are saying the payload contains no trace of it and the knowledge base's claim predates it by ten months.

Motorola also acquired Theatro and RapidDeploy in 2025, and renamed its Land Mobile Radio Communications product line "Mission Critical Networks" to accommodate Silvus.

Single biggest risk: the $4.4 billion Silvus acquisition. The offsetting consideration is real: mobile ad-hoc network technology sold into public safety and defence is a genuine capability rather than a bolt-on, $920 million of the price was allocated to developed technology, and Motorola has a long record of integrating acquisitions into its single sales channel — which is the fifth of the eight knowledge-base claims and the one with the best evidence behind it. But $4.4 billion is 6.0% of the market capitalisation spent on 2% of the revenue, and until Motorola discloses what Silvus earns, nobody outside the company can say whether it was worth it.
