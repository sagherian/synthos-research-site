The Overview

Netflix sells streaming subscriptions. That is essentially the whole business — the company reports as a single segment, and its own quarterly filing says revenue from anything other than monthly membership fees "was not a material component of revenues."

The shares have fallen from about $126 to $73.55 in a year, a drop of 42 percent, while the wider market rose 24 percent. That is a very large gap, and the reason is a story with a clear beginning, middle and end.

In December 2025 Netflix agreed to buy the streaming and studio businesses of Warner Bros. Discovery — the film and TV studios, plus HBO and HBO Max. Investors did not like it. Buying a large, old-fashioned Hollywood studio is expensive, slow, and brings union negotiations and competition-authority scrutiny. The share price fell.

Then in February 2026 Warner Bros. Discovery walked away, choosing to merge with a different buyer instead. Under the contract it had to compensate Netflix, and Netflix received $2.8 billion for the broken deal. Netflix had spent nothing, borrowed nothing, and was handed nearly three billion dollars.

That should have been good news. The shares have fallen another 20 percent since.

So what is actually wrong? Three things, and they are real but modest. First, sales are still growing but more slowly — from about 18 percent a year to about 13 percent. Second, in the most recent quarter the company's profit margin slipped slightly compared with a year earlier, the first time that has happened in this stretch, because it spent more on technology staff and marketing than its sales grew. Third, the cost of the shows and films it makes is rising fast enough that cash coming in from operations actually fell compared with last year.

Against that: profit margins are still around 33 percent, the company has authorisation to buy back $27 billion of its own shares — about nine percent of the whole company — and analysts expect earnings to grow more than 20 percent a year. At today's price you pay about 19 times what the company is expected to earn in 2027. For a business of this quality that is not expensive.

One number deserves care. Netflix's most recent twelve months of profit look better than they are, because they include that $2.8 billion break fee — worth about 53 cents a share. Strip it out and the real figure is $2.65 a share, not the $3.18 the screens show.

And one warning. The balance sheet appears to show almost no debt — about $5.4 billion net. But the company has separately committed to roughly $18 billion of future content payments that do not appear on the balance sheet at all, plus another $1-4 billion of commitments it cannot yet size. Its real fixed obligations are four to five times what the headline suggests.

Our estimate of fair value is $84 against $73.55. But the share price is still falling, sits well below its own averages, and every analyst covering it still has a target above today's price — which means the downgrades have not happened yet. So this is a purchase to build in pieces, not all at once.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

4. Content accounting — where the real leverage lives

Netflix capitalises the cost of content and amortises it through cost of revenues. That makes content amortization the largest single expense line, and the 10-Q names it as one of only two "significant items subject to… estimates and assumptions" in the entire financial statements (the other being income taxes). It also means the balance sheet dramatically understates the obligations.

So the real fixed-obligation stack is roughly four to five times what the net-debt line implies, and the 10-K's own risk factor is blunt about the consequence: "Given the multiple-year duration and largely fixed cost nature of content commitments, if business performance does not meet our expectations, our margins may be adversely impacted. 11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Buy — Tactical.

A good price attached to a broken trend. That combination is a staged purchase with a defined exit, not a core holding.

The case for buying. A 42 percent drawdown whose principal trigger — a definitive agreement to buy legacy studio assets — no longer exists, was exited without a dollar borrowed, and produced a $2.8 billion payment to Netflix. A multiple that has fallen from roughly 50x trailing at the high to 27.8x trailing underlying, and 19.3x FY2027 consensus, on a business whose first-half operating margin was 32.9 percent and whose consensus earnings compound above 22 percent a year to FY2028. A base fair value of $84, +14.2 percent, with roughly 2.8:1 asymmetry — and reachable on earnings growth alone with no re-rating ($4.571 of FY2028 earnings at an unchanged 19.3x is $88). $27.1 billion of buyback authorisation, 8.8 percent of the company, now buying 21 percent more stock per dollar than six months ago. And a knowledge-base lane whose sharpest voice called the exit in January, circled the lows in February, and holds the stock as of 2026-07-03.

The case for staging rather than committing. The stock is below both moving averages with the 200-day 23.5 percent overhead and both declining. The most recent quarter was the worst relative quarter (−19.2 percent against SPY +7.6 percent). The street has not marked down at all — 63 buys and a lowest target of $75, above spot — so the downgrades are ahead of us, not behind. Revenue growth is genuinely decelerating (17.6 → 16.2 → 13.4 percent), Q2 operating margin fell year on year for the first time in the visible series, and operating cash flow fell $679 million year on year. Management repurchased at $88.81 and the stock is 17 percent lower. There is no print for 77 days. And the panel's competitive concern — that streaming has become "flawless and undifferentiated" while a creator-led, advertising-funded rival takes living-room share — cannot be resolved from any filing.

How to build it. Three tranches. One now at $73.55. One on either a reclaim of the 50-day moving average at $76.50 (trend confirmation) or a print of the Q3 result on 2026-10-20 showing content-amortization growth in the mid-to-high single digits with year-on-year margin expansion (thesis confirmation) — whichever comes first. One on weakness into $65-68, at or below the 52-week low, which would be an upsize and not a reconsideration. Defined exit below $60 on the criteria immediately following.

Pre-registered KILL criteria — what would break the thesis:

  • Revenue growth printing below 11 percent in Q3'26, which would be a fourth consecutive quarter of deceleration and would put the FY2027 consensus revenue of $56.789 billion out of reach.
  • A second consecutive year-on-year operating-margin decline, particularly if the 10-Q again attributes it to technology-and-development and sales-and-marketing outrunning revenue. One quarter is a spending phase; two is an operating-leverage problem.
  • Content amortization growth failing to decelerate to mid-to-high single digits in the second half, contrary to the 2026-04-16 guidance. This is the most precisely testable claim in the thesis and it resolves on 2026-10-20.
  • Operating cash flow falling year on year for a second consecutive quarter on rising content payments — the cash-content-to-amortization ratio moving materially above the stated ~1.1x would mean reported margins are increasingly not cash-real.
  • Off-balance-sheet content obligations growing faster than revenue from the $18.4 billion disclosed at year-end 2025. The 10-K itself warns these cannot be reduced in the near term if cash flows fall.
  • Another large acquisition attempt. The WBD episode is a live, dated demonstration of exactly how the market prices this management's appetite for legacy media assets.
  • A decisive break below $60 on company-specific news rather than market beta.

Pre-registered FLIP TO CORE (upsize):

  • Q3'26 delivering content-amortization deceleration AND year-on-year operating-margin expansion in the same release. That single combination would falsify the deceleration narrative and is the cleanest possible upgrade trigger.
  • Advertising disclosed as a separate revenue line at or above the $3 billion annual pace. The market is currently paying nothing for a stream management says will double, because the filings call it immaterial. Disclosure alone would be a re-rating mechanism.
  • A buyback quarter materially above the first-half run-rate of roughly $2.95 billion at prices below $80, signalling management shares the valuation view at a level well beneath where it last bought.
  • A reclaim and hold of the 200-day moving average at $90.83, which would confirm the trend has turned and would still leave the stock 28 percent below its 52-week high.
  • The first analyst target cut below spot followed by price stability — a sentiment-clearing event that removes the overhang of an un-marked-down street.

Single biggest risk: that the growth deceleration is structural rather than a spending phase. Revenue growth went 17.2 → 17.6 → 16.2 → 13.4 percent across four quarters; operating margin fell year on year in Q2 for the first time in the visible series; operating cash flow fell $679 million year on year; and consensus itself models a glide to 7.3 percent revenue growth by FY2029. If 13.4 percent is a waypoint rather than a floor, the FY2027 consensus of $3.816 is wrong and a 19.3x multiple on it is not cheap.

Most fragile assumption in the price: that advertising never becomes separately material. Management guided to roughly $3 billion of 2026 advertising revenue, doubling year on year, and the company is visibly hiring advertising sales headcount — the Q2 10-Q names that as a driver of higher sales and marketing costs. The same filing states that non-membership revenue "was not a material component of revenues." Both statements cannot remain true for long. This is the rare fragile assumption that is fragile in the buyer's favour, and it is the single cleanest catalyst available on the name.
