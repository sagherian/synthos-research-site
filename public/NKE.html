The Overview

NIKE has been a disaster for shareholders. The shares cost $41.53. A year ago they were near $79. Over four years they have fallen roughly 77% from their peak. Over the last twelve months the stock market rose 24% and NIKE fell 44%.

So why would anyone buy it?

Start with what did not break. NIKE still keeps about 43 cents of gross profit on every revenue dollar — the same as before the decline. That matters enormously. A brand that has genuinely lost its grip has to discount, and discounting shows up immediately in that number. NIKE's held.

Then look at the most recent three months. In the quarter ending in May, gross margin was 49.2% against 40.3% a year earlier, and operating profit was $1,314 million against $321 million. Profit per share came in at $0.72 when analysts expected $0.11. That is not a small beat.

And every single quarter last year beat expectations — by 80%, 41%, 20% and 555%. For the full year NIKE earned $2.10 against the $1.50 analysts forecast. The forecasts are stale.

Now the thing that made us look hardest. In April, three people bought NIKE shares with their own money on the open market: the chief executive, and two directors. They paid $42.27, $42.43 and $43.34. The shares today cost $41.53 — less than all three paid.

Company insiders sell for a hundred reasons and buy for one. In this batch of twelve companies, we looked at roughly ninety insider transactions. This is the only cluster of genuine open-market purchases, and it includes the chief executive.

Here is what is still wrong. Sales in China have fallen from $7.5 billion to $5.8 billion in two years and are still falling. The company is paying out more in dividends than it generates in spare cash ($2.41 billion out against $2.18 billion generated), and it has cut its share buyback by 95% to make room. A new finance chief starts in twelve days. And the most reliable bearish analyst we track named NIKE in May as a stock signalling economic weakness — and he has been right ever since.

The dividend pays 3.92% a year while you wait. The company has slightly more cash than debt, so it is in no danger.

Our fair value estimate is $48, about 16% above the price. If we are wrong the downside looks like 28%; if we are right the upside could be 90%. That ratio — roughly three to one — plus a chief executive buying above today's price, is enough for a small position.

Small. Not large. The stock is a hair above its lowest point of the year and nothing on the chart has turned.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
