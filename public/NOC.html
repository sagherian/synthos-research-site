The Overview

Northrop Grumman makes things the American government cannot buy from anyone else: the B-21 stealth bomber, the replacement for the land-based nuclear missile force, satellites, and — with one competitor — the solid rocket motors that go inside missiles.

It has $104.7 billion of orders already signed. That is about two and a half years of sales, contracted. Very few companies anywhere have that kind of visibility.

Unlike almost every other stock in this batch, this one has already fallen. It is 28% below its high of the last year and it is below its long-term average price. The market is down 24% up over the same period. So something is wrong, and it is worth being precise about what.

In the three months to June, sales rose 5%. Operating profit — what the business actually earned before tax and interest — fell 23%. Profit per share nonetheless rose and beat expectations. The reason is tax: the company's tax rate for the first half was 10.4%, against 17.5% a year earlier, helped by $244 million of research credits. About half the profit growth this year is the tax bill, not the business.

The other problem is the bomber. The early production contracts for the B-21 are fixed-price, which means Northrop agreed a price and eats the overrun. It has been eating it: a $157 million charge in the first quarter of this year, $226 million a year before, and it is carrying a $477 million provision. One of the expert claims in our knowledge base says the B-21 is "uniquely on-time and under-budget". The company's own filings say otherwise, and we grade that claim as wrong.

At $551.55 the shares cost about 18 times what analysts expect the company to earn next year, and the company returns about 3.7% a year to shareholders through dividends and buybacks. Our estimate of fair value is $593, about 8% above the price. Analysts average $642.

One thing worth flagging that most screens will get wrong: the data feed we use reports Northrop's debt as $19.7 billion. The company's own balance sheet says $15.2 billion. The $4 billion difference is property leases — counted once inside long-term debt and then, remarkably, counted a second time on a separate line.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Two and a half years of contracted revenue; almost no tangible equity; fixed-price losses.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Contracted growth, but this year's profit growth is tax, not operations.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. Irreplaceable capabilities sold on terms that keep producing losses.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $593 against a current price of $551.55 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. The business, and the backlog that defines it

Northrop reports four segments — Aeronautics Systems, Defense Systems, Mission Systems and Space Systems. Chief executive Kathy J. Warden; approximately 95,000 employees; NYSE.

From the 10-Q: "Company backlog as of June 30, 2026 was $104.7 billion. Of our June 30, 2026 backlog, we expect to recognize approximately 35 percent as revenue over the next 12 months."

That single sentence is the strongest fact in this file and it appears in no vendor field. $104.7 billion is 2.50 times fiscal-2025 revenue of $41,954 million. 35% conversion implies roughly $36.6 billion of the next twelve months' revenue is already contracted, against a consensus of $44,056 million for 2026 — i.e. approximately 83% of the current-year forecast is in hand. This is why the FY2027 consensus EPS range is only 7.7% wide, the second-narrowest in this batch: analysts are scheduling rather than forecasting.

The estimate-at-completion adjustments — the recurring cost of fixed-price work

From the 10-Q, verbatim:

> 2026 · Stand-in Attack Weapon (SiAW) — "During the second quarter of 2026, the company recorded a $68 million unfavorable EAC adjustment on the SiAW program at Defense Systems driven by increases in the projected costs to support the design and qualification of system software and hardware architecture for the U.S. Air Force."

> 2026 · B-21 — "During the first quarter of 2026, the company reached an agreement with the U.S. Air Force to expand production capacity and increase the aircraft production rate for the B-21 program at Aeronautics Systems. We made no significant changes to the previously recognized loss on the program; however, impacts from the agreement and higher estimated production costs resulted in a $157 million net unfavorable EAC adjustment on the first four LRIP lots, which was offset by a net reduction in the loss contingency accrual on the remainder of the program."

> 2025 · B-21 — "During the first quarter of 2025, the company recorded a $226 million unfavorable EAC adjustment on the first and second LRIP lots of the B-21 program at Aeronautics Systems."

And the cash flow statement carries a B-21 loss provision of $477 million in the first half of 2026.

Read together: the B-21 low-rate initial production lots are fixed-price, the company has been absorbing the overrun for three years, and the March-2026 agreement to expand production capacity produced a further $157 million of unfavourable adjustment on the first four lots even as the loss accrual on the remainder was reduced. The programme is being expanded and it is still losing money on the contracted lots. This is the single most important operating fact in the file, and it is the direct refutation of one of the knowledge base's own claims (Section 6).
