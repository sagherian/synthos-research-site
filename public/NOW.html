The Overview

ServiceNow makes software that large companies use to organise work. When an employee's laptop breaks, when a new hire needs six systems set up, when a security alert needs investigating, when a customer complaint needs routing — ServiceNow is the system that tracks who does what, in what order, with what approvals. It is the market leader in this, sits inside the IT department of most large corporations, and is very hard to remove once installed.

By every measure the company publishes, it is doing well. Sales in the three months to June grew 24%. The value of contracts signed but not yet delivered reached $29 billion, up 21%. 98% of customers renewed — exactly the same as last year. Sales have grown 24% a year for five years without a single down year.

And the shares have fallen 35% in a year while the stock market rose 24%. They are half their peak price.

The reason is an argument, not a number. A large group of serious investors believes that artificial intelligence will make software like ServiceNow's unnecessary — that once a company can ask an AI to do the work directly, it no longer needs to pay for a platform that organises humans doing it. One well-known investor argues that agents are now cheap enough that companies will build their own alternatives and cut what they pay ServiceNow by half or more. An equally serious group argues the opposite: that AI agents need tools to act with, that recreating an enterprise platform with an AI model actually costs about ten times more once you count the computing bills, and that ServiceNow — which connects to over 800 other business systems — becomes more valuable, not less, as the control room for all those agents.

Nothing in the company's current results settles this, because the argument is about 2030 and the results are about contracts signed years ago.

Two things did get worse recently, and both are the company's own doing. ServiceNow spent $8.8 billion buying two security companies in the first half of 2026 — one for $7.6 billion in April. To pay for it, it borrowed $4 billion in bonds plus another $2 billion short-term. A company that had more cash than debt in December now has more debt than cash. And the accounting cost of writing off those purchases has crushed the reported profit margin, from 11% down to 4%.

The shares cost $118.14. That is about 23.5 times what analysts expect the company to earn in 2027 — for a business growing 24% a year. Historically these shares cost 45 to 60 times. Our estimate of fair value is $135, about 14% above the price. The average analyst says $138.

But look at the range of analyst opinions: the most optimistic says $236 and the most pessimistic says $85. That gap — one nearly three times the other — is the honest measure of how unresolved this is.

  • Downside Risk 7/10. The business is not the risk; a debate about its future, and a newly borrowed $6 billion, are.
  • Growth Quality 8/10. Excellent and durable on every published measure — with the acquired portion undisclosed.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. A genuine platform. The same technology wave is both its opportunity and its threat.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
