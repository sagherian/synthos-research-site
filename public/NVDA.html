The Overview

NVIDIA makes the chips and the systems that artificial-intelligence computing runs on. That is now almost the whole company: of the $81.6 billion it took in over the three months to April, $75.2 billion — about nine dollars in every ten — came from selling data-centre computing. Sales were up 85% on the same three months a year earlier.

Three things have changed recently and they are the reason this is worth reading carefully.

First, the customer list got shorter. Three customers accounted for 21%, 17% and 16% of everything NVIDIA sold last quarter. A year earlier the top two were 16% and 14%. So more than half the revenue now comes from three companies. Those same three owe NVIDIA nearly two-thirds of its unpaid bills.

Second, China is gone. US export rules mean NVIDIA cannot sell competitive data-centre chips into China at all — the company says so in plain language in its own filing. China was worth $19.7 billion of sales in the last full year. It is now zero for data centres, and a licence programme granted in February has produced no revenue at all so far.

Third, and most unusual, NVIDIA has started funding its own customers. In that same three months it put $18.6 billion into private companies and infrastructure funds — more than it invested in the entire preceding year — and has promised another $27 billion. It has also guaranteed $3.5 billion of other companies' building leases. When the value of those stakes goes up, the gain is counted as profit. Last quarter $16 billion of NVIDIA's $70 billion of pre-tax profit was exactly that: paper gains on investments, most of which it is contractually barred from selling until at least December 2027. Strip that out and profit per share was $1.87, not $2.39.

There is a fourth item that follows from all this. In June, NVIDIA borrowed $25 billion in the bond market, with some of it not due until 2056. A company earning over $100 billion a year in cash does not normally need to borrow. It needed to because it is buying back roughly $80 billion of its own shares a year, has just raised its dividend twenty-five-fold, and is putting nearly $19 billion a quarter into other people's companies. Together that is more than the cash it generates.

None of that makes the business bad. It is the most profitable large company in the world: it keeps 75 cents of gross profit on every dollar of sales, and every dollar of capital it employs earns 63 cents a year. And it is not expensive against what analysts expect — about 17 times the earnings 40 of them think it will make in the year to January 2028.

What it does mean is that the risks are no longer just "will people buy chips". They are: will three customers keep buying, will the money those customers are borrowing keep being available, and are the profits real cash or marked-up paper. Several credible people we track are asking exactly the third question, and they were asking it last week, not last year.

The stock has actually gone nowhere much. It is up 22% over a year while the broad market rose 24% and the technology index rose 31%. It sits 10% below its high. Today it rose 2.56% — but that was a day when the whole chip sector jumped, and NVIDIA rose less than any of its peers. That is not the day to buy in size, and results are due in three weeks.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

2. Where the earnings actually come from — the $16.0B of investment marks

This is the single most important analytical issue in this name today, and it is new.

Q1 FY2027 reported GAAP diluted EPS of $2.39 on net income of $58.321B. The non-GAAP figure the street measures against was $1.87. The gap is not stock compensation. It is the investment portfolio.

The 10-Q is explicit about what that $16.367B is: "The change in Other income (expense), net compared to the first quarter of fiscal year 2026, was primarily driven by unrealized gains on investments in publicly-held equity securities of $13.4 billion and non-marketable equity securities of $2.6 billion."

So roughly $16.0B of a $69.9B pretax result — 23% — was mark-to-market on an investment portfolio, and 23.4% of GAAP net income. For scale, interest income in the quarter was $540M and interest expense $102M; the entire net-interest line is rounding error next to the marks.

Three things make this more than an accounting footnote.

First, the positions are illiquid. Of the marketable equity securities, $27.4B was subject to short-term lock-up restrictions at 2026-04-26 (against $10.5B at 2026-01-25), and a further $8.9B is locked up through December 2027 and sits in Other assets. Cumulative gross unrealized gains on non-marketable equity securities were $5.3B, against $396M a year earlier. These are gains NVIDIA cannot monetise on demand.

Second, the portfolio is being built at extraordinary speed, into the customer base. The 10-K discloses $17.5B invested in private companies and infrastructure funds across all of FY2026, describing them as "primarily to support early-stage startups... These investments include AI model makers that purchase our products directly or through CSPs. Many of these investments are illiquid and non-marketable... there can be no assurance that we will realize a return." The 10-Q then discloses $18.6B in Q1 FY2027 alone — more in three months than in the prior twelve — plus $27B of further investment commitments (against $11.4B one quarter earlier), $1.0B of equity-method infrastructure-fund investments with $2.3B of maximum loss exposure, and $3.5B of land, power and shell guarantees provided to early-stage companies, against which partners have placed only $712M in escrow.

Third, our own knowledge base has been naming this dynamic for eight months, on both sides. The bearish framing is specific: "Nvidia's extraordinary margins are manufactured by vendor-financing customers (like Cisco in the late 1990s); if customer revenues fail, future write-downs will reverse those booked margins" (2026-07-08, conviction 72). The bullish framing of the identical facts is equally specific: "Nvidia's GPU backstops let it double-dip — earning loan-margin plus revenue-share above the backstop price — turning one-time hardware sales into recurring-margin, sticky businesses" (2026-07-23, conviction 55).

What this dive does with it. We value NVIDIA on operating earnings and consensus non-GAAP EPS, not on GAAP. Trailing GAAP diluted EPS is $6.53 (32.5x); trailing non-GAAP EPS from the earnings calendar is $5.84 (36.3x). The honest trailing multiple is the higher one, and every screen shows the lower one. Forward estimates are on the non-GAAP basis — consensus FY2026 EPS of $4.69 against a $4.78 non-GAAP actual and a $4.90 GAAP actual confirms it — so the forward multiples in Section 6 are internally consistent.

A gap that must be stated: earnings-call transcripts are not available on this data plan. Management's own framing of the investment programme, of the customer concentration, and of the H200 licence position exists only in the Q&A we cannot see. The 8-K exhibits carry the release, not the call. Nothing in this dive quotes management beyond the filed documents.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Over a twelve-month horizon, expected return decomposes as: earnings growth (+41.7%, FY2027E $9.00 to FY2028E $12.75) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 23.5x to ~20x on the equivalent forward year) + shareholder yield (~2.05%).

Our base case explicitly assumes multiple compression, not expansion, and the entire return comes from earnings. In twelve months the market will be pricing the same relative position on FY2028 that it prices on FY2027 today. Hold the multiple constant at 23.5x and the price is $300. We use 20x and get $255 — a 15% de-rating — because growth is maturing from +82% (FY2027E) to +43% (FY2028E) to +22% (FY2029E), because the company is now 9% of the index and cannot carry a scarcity premium at that weight, and because the composition of earnings has degraded with the arrival of $16B quarterly investment marks.

That is the honest structure of this trade: you are underwriting 40%+ earnings growth and giving back roughly a sixth of it in de-rating. If growth comes in and the de-rating does not happen, $300 is available. If growth comes in and the de-rating is worse than we assume — say 17x — the price is $217 and you have made nothing in a year. The fragile leg is not the multiple; it is the earnings, and specifically the three customers who supply 54% of them.

The bull case at $330 (26x FY2028E) requires the market to pay more than it does today for a slower-growing, larger company. That leg is genuinely fragile and the base case does not need it.

  • A decisive break below the 200-DMA at $193.41 on company-specific news rather than sector beta.
