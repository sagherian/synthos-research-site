The Overview

Oracle used to sell database software. Software is a wonderful business: you write it once, sell it many times, and roughly seventy or eighty cents of every dollar of revenue is gross profit. For decades that is what Oracle was.

Over the last two years Oracle has become something else. It is now building enormous data centres — warehouses full of computers — and renting the computing power inside them to a small number of very large customers who need it to run artificial-intelligence systems. That is not a software business. It is closer to a property developer that happens to buy computers instead of bricks.

Building costs money. In the year to 31 May 2026 Oracle spent $55.7 billion on construction and equipment. It only generated $32.0 billion of cash from running the business. The gap — about $23.7 billion — was borrowed, along with the $5.8 billion it paid out in dividends. Oracle now owes $129.5 billion, has promised a further $260 billion of building rents that have not even started yet, and has stopped buying back its own shares.

The reason anyone is willing to fund this is a single number in the annual report: Oracle has $638 billion of signed customer contracts that it has not yet delivered on — up from $138 billion a year ago. That is roughly nine and a half years of its current revenue, already sold. The company says it expects to deliver about 12% of it in the next twelve months and 80% within five years.

So the whole question is: will the customers pay? And here the annual report is honest but unhelpful. It confirms that no single customer was more than 10% of last year's sales. It says nothing about who owes the $638 billion. And in its own list of risks it writes that "some of our customers may be highly leveraged" and that in certain of its cloud offerings it is "more concentrated among a number of large customers."

The bond market is nervous. Oracle had to pay 6.85% to borrow forty-year money in February, and issued a special class of preferred shares paying 6.5%. Insurance against Oracle defaulting has become markedly more expensive. Meanwhile the analysts who cover the stock still have an average target of $249 against a price of $146 — they have not changed their minds, only the share price has.

The shares have fallen 58% from their high. That is a lot of bad news already in the price. It is not, however, a floor: there is no tangible book value to fall back on, no free cash flow, no buyback, and the company has registered to sell up to $20 billion of new shares whenever it wants. Our estimate of fair value is $158 against a price of $145.72 — close enough that this is a name to watch, not to buy today.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

We state the $260 billion as an undiscounted stream of future lease payments over fifteen-to-nineteen-year terms — it is not comparable to debt principal and we do not add it to enterprise value. But it is the reason the credit market is charging what it is charging, and it is entirely absent from every screen.

One nuance the bears should note: the off-balance-sheet commitment stopped growing in the fourth quarter. It was $248 billion at 2025-11-30, $261 billion at 2026-02-28 and $260 billion at 2026-05-31. Quarterly capital expenditure likewise stepped down: $20.5 billion in H1, $18.7 billion in Q3, $16.5 billion in Q4. That is either capital discipline or a demand pause, and the data cannot distinguish between them.

Third — and this is the fulcrum — the concentration of the backlog is not disclosed. The 10-K states:

> "No single customer accounted for 10% or more of our total revenues in fiscal 2026, 2025 or 2024."

That is a revenue disclosure. It says nothing about who holds the $638 billion of contracted future revenue. The same filing's risk factors are unusually direct about why that matters:

> "If we overestimate customer demand or any of our key customers are unable to pay or otherwise perform under their contracts with us, we could be locked into multi-year commitments for excess data center space and related capital expenditures, as well as associated financings, without receiving corresponding revenue... In addition, some of our customers may be highly leveraged and subject to their own operating and regulatory risks... This is the interesting part on a name that has already halved. At $145.72 — 18.1x FY2027 consensus, 13.3x FY2028, and 9.4x FY2029 — the price is not paying for the growth at all. Reverse-engineered, it embeds roughly the following falsifiable claims:

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return ≈ EPS growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield − dilution.

  • EPS growth is the weak leg near term and the strong leg later. Consensus non-GAAP EPS grows +5.6% in FY2027 (from $7.63 to $8.06) and +35.6% in FY2028. The near-term contribution to return is therefore almost nil; the case is a FY2028-FY2029 story, which is another way of saying the holding period is long and the intervening path is where the risk lives.
  • Our base case assumes modest multiple EXPANSION, from 18.1x to 19.6x FY2027 consensus, and we flag that as the fragile leg. The justification is not optimism but normalisation: a business with 33% contracted forward revenue growth should not trade below a terminal multiple, and the current level implies active disbelief in disclosed backlog. But we deliberately keep the expansion small, because the offsetting force is real — as the depreciation base scales and free cash flow stays negative, the quality of the earnings deteriorates and a lower multiple can be perfectly rational.
  • Shareholder yield is roughly zero and could be negative. The dividend is $2.00 a share, 1.37%, and it is currently funded by borrowing. The buyback is effectively dead ($93 million, 0.02% of market capitalisation). The $20 billion at-the-market programme is a potential −4.76%, and the mandatory convertible is a further −1.08% at prices below $160.06. Net shareholder yield is plausibly negative, which is unusual for a mega-cap and materially changes the return arithmetic.
  • Putting it together: roughly +5.6% earnings growth, +8% from mild multiple normalisation, +1.4% dividend, less up to −5.8% of potential dilution, gives a central twelve-month expectation in the high single digits — which is exactly where our $158 base sits (+8.4%). Most of the return in the base case is neither growth nor re-rating; it is the absence of further de-rating. That is not a compelling risk-reward against a −41.7% bear case.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch.

The question the brief posed — broken thesis or violent de-rating of an intact one — has a specific answer: the thesis is intact and the company is different. Oracle's demand is not in doubt; $638 billion of contracted remaining performance obligations, up 362% in a year and still adding $85 billion in the final quarter, with 80% converting inside five years, is about as hard as forward evidence gets. What changed is what has to be true for that demand to reach the equity holder. In FY2024 Oracle turned $52.961 billion of revenue into $11.807 billion of free cash flow. In FY2026 it turned $67.358 billion of revenue into negative $23.686 billion, spent 82.6% of revenue on capital expenditure, borrowed its dividend, stopped its buyback, issued 6.85% forty-year paper and a 6.50% mandatory convertible, registered a $20 billion equity shelf, and committed to $260 billion of lease payments that are not on its balance sheet. The 57.9% de-rating from $345.72 was not an overreaction; it was a repricing of a genuinely different security.

At $145.72 the price has stopped paying for the growth. Eighteen times FY2027 consensus, thirteen times FY2028 and nine times FY2029 on a business the street models at +33%, +45% and +38% revenue growth — with 85% of the FY2027 revenue number already contracted — is a price that says the schedule will not be met. That is a real, falsifiable disagreement with the filing, and it is why this is not an Avoid.

It is not a Buy for one reason above all others: the single fact that decides the case is not disclosed. Oracle has published a $638 billion asset and has published nothing about its counterparty concentration, while writing in its own risk factors that "some of our customers may be highly leveraged" and that "in certain OCI offerings, we are more concentrated among a number of large customers." Our knowledge base contains a specific claim that roughly $500 billion of it sits with one counterparty. We cannot verify it and we will not pretend to. A $260 billion off-balance-sheet lease obligation against a backlog of undisclosed concentration is an asymmetry a Buy rating cannot survive. Add a base fair value only 8.4% above spot, a knowledge-base lane split 44-45 with the credit side more recent, a stock that has already bounced 27% off its low to RSI 60, and a print 35 days away under a four-month-old chief financial officer — and Watch is the honest verdict.

Pre-registered KILL criteria — what would move this to Avoid:

  • Remaining performance obligations DECLINING sequentially, or the disclosed twelve-month conversion percentage falling below 10% (it was 10% at 2025-11-30, 12% at 2026-02-28 and 12% at 2026-05-31). A rising balance with a falling conversion rate is backlog lengthening, not demand strengthening — and the lease commitments commence regardless.
  • Any drawdown on the $20 billion at-the-market equity programme below ~$160. Issuing 4.8% of the company at a 54% discount to the prior-year high would be an admission that the debt markets have closed, and it is the most likely path to a lower low.
  • A credit-rating downgrade. The 10-K is explicit that this could "affect the terms or availability of certain long-term commitments (including data center leases)... and increase collateral, letter of credit or other credit support requirements under certain contractual arrangements." A downgrade is not a headline here; it is a cash call.
  • Credit-default-swap protection widening decisively beyond 300bps, or a failed or heavily concessionary senior-note issuance.
  • Any disclosed impairment, renegotiation, restructuring or non-payment involving a large cloud counterparty, or a write-down against the property base (the 10-K notes no significant property impairment in FY2026, FY2025 or FY2024 — that streak breaking would be material).
  • Gross margin falling below 60% on a fiscal-year basis (65.20% in FY2026, 70.51% in FY2025), which would mean the incremental infrastructure revenue is being taken at a price that does not carry its capital cost.
  • A dividend cut. It would be the correct capital-allocation decision and a devastating signal, and both facts should be held at once.

Pre-registered FLIP TO BUY (and upsize) conditions:

  • The 2026-09-08 print showing RPO at or above $638 billion WITH the twelve-month conversion percentage at or above 12%. This is the single cleanest flip trigger in the dive: it directly falsifies the central claim embedded in today's price.
  • Operating cash flow of $9 billion or more in Q1 FY2027 (against $31.977 billion for FY2026) with capital-expenditure guidance that peaks rather than continuing to rise.
  • Any disclosure quantifying backlog concentration favourably — for example a stated maximum single-counterparty share of remaining performance obligations below 30%. This alone would move the verdict.
  • A restarted buyback against the $6.3 billion remaining authorisation, which would signal management believes the financing phase is behind it.
  • Credit-default-swap protection back below 150bps, or a senior-note issuance at coupons meaningfully inside February 2026's 6.70%-6.85% long end.
  • A price below ~$110 — through the 52-week low — without any of the kill criteria having triggered. At that level the FY2027 multiple is 13.7x and the FY2028 multiple 10.1x on contracted revenue, and the risk-reward becomes asymmetric enough to act on despite the undisclosed concentration.
