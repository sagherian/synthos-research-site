The Overview

When your car breaks, someone has to have the part. O'Reilly's entire business is being that someone — through a network of stores and warehouses close enough to get the part to a mechanic within hours. It sells both to people fixing their own cars and, increasingly, to professional garages.

It is very good at it. O'Reilly keeps about 52 cents of gross profit on every dollar of parts it sells, and earns roughly 46 cents of operating profit for every dollar of capital employed. Its suppliers effectively finance the inventory: O'Reilly takes about 300 days to pay them.

The company pays no dividend at all. Instead it buys back its own shares, relentlessly. Over five years the share count has fallen 19.5% — which is why profit per share grew 12.5% a year while sales grew 8.9%.

Here is the problem, and it is arithmetic. The cash the company has left after investment has fallen for three years running: $2.58 billion, then $2.03 billion, then $2.03 billion, then $1.59 billion. Meanwhile the money it spends on warehouses and stores has doubled to $1.17 billion.

And it still bought back $2.10 billion of shares last year — 132% of the cash it generated. The difference was borrowed.

That is a deliberate, long-standing policy and it has made shareholders a great deal of money. It also means the company's balance sheet shows negative shareholders' equity of $763 million, and that a third of the earnings growth depends on borrowing continuing to be available and cheap.

Two things about the data itself are worth reporting, because they are unusual.

The company's own insider-transaction file contains a record dated 13 July 2027 — eleven months from now. That cannot have happened. We found the answer in a regulatory filing: the company hired a new supply-chain chief effective 13 July 2026 and granted him options that day. The year in the data feed is wrong by exactly one.

And the data feed contains no breakdown of the business at all — one line covering 100% of sales, and nothing by geography. So we cannot tell you how the professional half is performing against the do-it-yourself half, which is the most important operating question about this company.

The shares cost $91.83, about 25 times what analysts expect for 2027. Our fair value estimate is $95 — about 3.5% higher. Analysts on average say $107.77.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Read the middle two columns together. Capital expenditure has more than doubled in three years, from $563.3 million to $1,168.8 million — a 107.5% increase — while operating cash flow FELL 12.3% from $3,148.3 million to $2,762.0 million. Free cash flow has therefore declined in each of the last three years, by a cumulative 38.4%.

And the buyback has continued regardless, at 102% to 155% of free cash flow every single year. commonDividendsPaid reads exactly $0 in all four years — O'Reilly has never paid one. Total debt rose from $7,841.0 million (FY2023) to $7,920.9 million to $8,491.5 million (FY2025), which is where the difference came from.

This is a coherent and long-established policy, not a mistake. A business earning 34.3% on invested capital and 46.5% on capital employed should borrow to buy its own shares, and O'Reilly has done so for a decade with excellent results. What this dive records is that the policy is currently consuming more than the business produces, at a moment when the business is producing less.

The capital-expenditure diagnostic is high but explicable. capexToDepreciationTTM reads 2.107, and the direct fiscal 2025 computation is $1,168.8M / $511.2M = 2.29x. A ratio above 2 means the asset base is growing fast, and the 10-K states the strategy directly: it "requires capital to maintain and enhance our existing stores, invest to open new stores, fund strategic acquisitions, expand distribution infrastructure, develop enhanced information technology systems and tools." No corruption signature is tripped — capex is not zero, not positive-signed, not below 0.15 of depreciation, and the four-year progression from $563.3M to $1,168.8M is smooth and directional. The ratio is genuinely high because the company is genuinely building.

The company's own free cash flow definition differs from ours and is worth noting. The 10-K defines it as "net cash provided by operating activities less capital expenditures, excess tax benefit from share-based compensation payments, and (return of)/investment in tax credit equity investments." This dive uses the standard operating-cash-flow-minus-capital-expenditure construction throughout and states the difference.

Corrected leverage. totalDebt of $8,491.5M is filed long-term debt of $6,016.9M plus $2,474.6M of lease obligations — a 29.1% inclusion, the largest in this batch. On the financial-debt basis, net debt is $5,823.1M, or 1.46x fiscal 2025 EBITDA of $3,986.5M, against the vendor's netDebtToEBITDATTM of 2.311x. Corrected enterprise value is approximately $81,924M against the vendor's $85,426M — a 4.1% overstatement. Interest coverage is 14.4x on either basis.
