The Overview

Every large company runs dozens of separate security products bought from dozens of separate vendors — one for the network, one for laptops, one for the cloud, one for passwords, one for monitoring. They do not talk to each other, and the gaps between them are where breaches happen. Palo Alto Networks' whole strategy is to sell you one platform that does all of it, so there are no gaps and only one bill.

The strategy is working. Sales have gone from $3.4 billion five years ago to $9.2 billion last fiscal year, and more than $10.6 billion over the last twelve months. Four-fifths of that is now subscriptions rather than one-off equipment sales, which means it recurs. The company also tells you how much work it has already sold but not yet delivered — a figure it calls remaining performance obligations — and that number was $18.4 billion at the end of April, up from $15.8 billion nine months earlier. It generates a lot of cash: about $3.5 billion of free cash last year, and it has more cash than debt.

So the business is good. Here is what it costs. The shares are $366.34, and there are 815 million of them, so the whole company is priced at $298.6 billion. Over the last twelve months it made $10.6 billion of sales — so you are paying twenty-eight years of sales. On accounting profits it made $843 million, so you are paying about 354 times what it actually earned. Companies like this also publish a friendlier profit number that leaves out the cost of the shares they give employees and the cost of writing off what they paid for acquisitions; on that friendlier number the shares are still about 97 times earnings.

Two more things a reader should know. First, in February the company paid $21.1 billion for CyberArk, a password-and-access-security company, and $3.0 billion for another business in January. Those deals are so recent that the most recent full balance sheet in our data — dated last July — does not contain them at all. In the three months to April, those acquired businesses added $388 million of sales and lost $523 million, which pushed the whole company into an operating loss for the quarter.

Second, the shares have nearly doubled in three months. They closed on 4 August at the highest price they have ever reached. And the average Wall Street analyst, of whom 66 out of 89 still say "buy", thinks the shares are worth $337.50 — less than they cost today. When the people recommending a stock have a price target below its price, the price has run ahead of the story.

Our own estimate of what the shares are worth is $255. They cost $366.34. That is a 30% gap in the wrong direction, and so the answer is no — not because the company is bad, but because the price already assumes an excellent outcome and then some.

  • Downside Risk 8/10. No debt problem at all — but 28 times sales, an all-time high, an unobserved fiscal fourth quarter, and analyst targets already beneath the price.
  • Growth Quality 8/10. A high-quality recurring revenue base growing in the mid-teens organically, with the recent acceleration bought rather than earned.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. The consolidation-onto-one-platform idea is real and securing AI agents is a genuine new market — but the company is buying that future with its own shares.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $255 against a current price of $353.90 — consistent with our call to stay away or wait for a better setup.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

The 10-K opens plainly: "Palo Alto Networks, Inc. is a global cybersecurity provider and our vision is a world where each day is safer and more secure than the one before. We were incorporated in 2005 and are headquartered in Santa Clara, California." Chief executive Nikesh Arora; 16,068 employees at 31 July 2025, up from 15,289 a year earlier. Listed on NASDAQ since 20 July 2012. CUSIP 697435105, ISIN US6974351057.

Customer concentration: the risk is in the channel, not the customer

This is the most under-appreciated disclosure in the 10-K and the vendor file cannot express it. Palo Alto sells through a two-tier indirect model — to distributors, who sell to resellers, who sell to end customers. The concentration therefore sits with the distributors:

> "For fiscal 2025, three distributors represented 10% or more of our total revenue, representing 18.8%, 14.4%, and 11.0% respectively. No single end-customer accounted for more than 10% of our total revenue in fiscal 2025, 2024, or 2023."

> "As of July 31, 2025, three distributors individually represented 10% or more of our gross accounts receivable, and in the aggregate represented 44.8% of our gross accounts receivable."

Three intermediaries carry 44.2% of revenue and 44.8% of receivables. The end-customer base is genuinely diversified — the 10-Q says customers in over 180 countries, "almost all of the Fortune 100 companies and a majority of the Global 2000" — so this is a working-capital and channel-relationship concentration rather than a demand concentration. It is still 44% of the company through three counterparties, and it is why trailing days-sales-outstanding is 118.5 days, extraordinarily high for a software business.

Competitors, per the 10-K's own four-category framing

  • Large companies embedding security in their products: Cisco Systems, Microsoft, Alphabet.
  • Independent security vendors: Check Point, Fortinet, CrowdStrike, Zscaler, Wiz.
  • Startups and point-product vendors across various security areas.
  • Public cloud vendors and startups offering private, public and hybrid cloud security.

Note what that list implies. Palo Alto's stated competitive threat is not one rival; it is simultaneously the hyperscale platforms bundling security for free and hundreds of venture-funded point vendors undercutting on features. The consolidation thesis in Section 7's knowledge base is a bet that the middle — the independent multi-product platform — wins that squeeze.

2. The single most important thing to understand about these numbers: there are three different companies in this file, and only one of them is priced

Palo Alto's reported numbers cannot be read on a single basis, and the three bases disagree by an order of magnitude.

Basis one — GAAP, as filed. Trailing twelve months to 30 April 2026: revenue $10,606.3M, gross profit $7,630.3M (71.94%), operating income $1,023.2M (9.65%), net income $842.8M (7.95%). Basis three — the historical GAAP record, which is corrupted by a one-off and must be repaired before any trend can be read.

This is the defect the brief flagged and it is live. Palo Alto's fiscal-2024 GAAP net income was $2,577.6 million on pre-tax income of $988.3 million — net income 2.6 times larger than pre-tax income, because the tax line was a BENEFIT of $1,589.3 million. The 10-K states why:

> "For the year ended July 31, 2024, our benefit from income taxes was $1.6 billion, primarily due to the release of our valuation allowance on U.S. federal, U.S. states other than California, and United Kingdom ('U.K.') deferred tax assets."

And, confirming the size of the swing:

> "Our provision for income taxes in fiscal 2025 was $461.8 million, a net change of $2.1 billion compared to a benefit from income taxes of $1.6 billion in fiscal 2024, primarily due to the release of our valuation allowance in fiscal 2024."

A valuation-allowance release is an accounting recognition that past losses will now shelter future profits. It is not earnings. It happened once and cannot happen again.

Stripping it. Our arithmetic, labelled as ours: fiscal 2025's effective tax rate was 28.94% ($461.8M on $1,595.7M of pre-tax income). Applying that rate to fiscal 2024's pre-tax income of $988.3 million gives a normalised tax expense of $286.0 million and normalised net income of $702.3 million — against the $2,577.6 million reported. That is a 72.8% overstatement. Normalised diluted earnings per share become $0.99 against the $3.64 reported. Using the 21% US statutory rate instead gives $780.8 million and $1.10 — the conclusion is unchanged either way.
