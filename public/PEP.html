The Overview

PepsiCo makes two kinds of thing. About half the company sells salty snacks — Lay's crisps, Doritos, Cheetos, Quaker oats — and about half sells drinks: Pepsi, Mountain Dew, Gatorade, bottled water. It does this in more than 200 countries, and it is enormous: roughly $97 billion of sales in the last twelve months and 306,000 employees.

For most of the last thirty years this was a very reliable machine. It sold a little more each year, raised prices a little each year, and handed the profits back to shareholders as a dividend that went up every year. That is what people bought it for.

Three things have changed, and all three are in the company's own filings.

The first is that people are buying fewer units. Not less money's worth — the company keeps putting prices up — but fewer actual bags and bottles. In the six months to mid-June 2026, the North American drinks business sold 3% fewer units than a year earlier, and fizzy drinks specifically were down 3%. The snack business sold 1% more. When a company's sales go up because prices went up while the number of things sold goes down, that is not the same as growth, and it cannot continue forever.

The second is that the reported sales growth is flattered. Sales in the first half of 2026 were up 7.3% — which sounds good. But the company itself says 3 percentage points of that came from currency movements (mostly the Mexican peso and Russian rouble strengthening against the dollar), and most of the rest came from two businesses it bought in 2025, a soda brand called poppi and a Mexican-American food maker called Siete. Strip those out and the underlying business is growing at low single digits at best.

The third is the dividend, which is the reason most people own this stock. It pays $1.48 every three months, $5.92 a year, which at today's $139.10 share price is a 4.26% yield. That is a lot, and it is real. The problem is how much of the company's spare cash it eats. Last year PepsiCo generated $7.672 billion of spare cash after paying for its factories, and paid out $7.638 billion in dividends — 99.6% of it. It also bought back $1 billion of shares, which it had to borrow to do. Company management's plan for 2026 is to hand back about $8.9 billion, against about $9.3 billion of spare cash. There is no room in that. The dividend is not in danger tomorrow, but it cannot grow much faster than profits do, and profits are barely growing.

There is one more thing worth understanding, because it is genuinely uncertain and nobody knows the answer. Weight-loss drugs like Ozempic work by suppressing appetite. PepsiCo's own annual report now lists "increasing use of weight-loss drugs, such as GLP-1 medications" as a risk to demand for its products. Independent voices in our research base estimate these drugs cut roughly 800 calories a day from a user's intake and suppress the desire for both food and alcohol. Today only a small fraction of people take them. If that fraction becomes large, it is a direct problem for a company that sells snacks and sugary drinks. If it stays small, it is noise. We do not know which, and neither does anyone else.

So what is it worth? Analysts expect the company to earn about $8.97 a share in 2027. At $139.10 you are paying about 15.5 times that. Our estimate of fair value is $148, about 6% above today's price; the average analyst says $155.64, about 12% above. When our own number is below the crowd's on a business whose volumes are shrinking, the honest answer is that this is a reasonable thing to keep owning for the income and not a compelling thing to buy today.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. A low-volatility, high-yield asset with an income bid under it — attached to a payout with no spare cash in it and a debt pile that is growing rather than shrinking.
  • Growth Quality 3/10. Sales growth of +0.4% and +2.3% in the last two full years, and the 2026 acceleration is currency and acquisitions, not units.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. There is none, and the two structural forces in the file — appetite-suppressing drugs and the backlash against processed food — both point downward.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $148 against a current price of $139.10 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

PepsiCo describes itself in the 10-K as "a leading global beverage and convenient food company with a complementary portfolio of brands, including Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos, Gatorade, Pepsi-Cola, Mountain Dew, Quaker and SodaStream." Incorporated in Delaware in 1919, reincorporated in North Carolina in 1986. Chief executive Ramon Luis Laguarta; 306,000 employees; headquartered in Purchase, New York; listed on NASDAQ Global Select; CIK 0000077476.

The six reportable segments — and the vendor is one taxonomy behind

This is the single most important disclosure-structure point in the file, and it is a live defect. The 10-K filed 2026-02-03 states at Note 1: "We are organized into six reportable segments" — and lists them:

1. PepsiCo Foods North America (PFNA) — all convenient food businesses in the US and Canada. Cheetos, Doritos, Fritos, Lay's, Pearl Milling Company, Quaker, Ruffles, Tostitos, plus Sabra (the Strauss Group's 50% acquired December 2024).

2. PepsiCo Beverages North America (PBNA) — all beverage businesses in the US and Canada. Aquafina, Bubly, Gatorade, Mountain Dew, Pepsi, Propel. Also distributes Celsius, Alani Nu and Rockstar for Celsius Holdings, and acts as exclusive small-format/foodservice distributor for Tropicana Beverages Group.

3. International Beverages Franchise (IB Franchise) — international franchise beverage businesses plus SodaStream. 7UP, Aquafina, Gatorade, Mirinda, Mountain Dew, Pepsi, Sting Energy.

4. Europe, Middle East and Africa (EMEA) — convenient foods and company-owned-bottler beverages across Europe, the Middle East and Africa. Chipsy, Sasko, Spekko, Walkers, White Star; plus the Russian dairy brands Agusha, Chudo and Domik v Derevne.

5. Latin America Foods (LatAm Foods) — convenient foods in Latin America. Emperador, Marias Gamesa, Sabritas, Saladitas Gamesa.

6. Asia Pacific Foods — convenient foods in Asia Pacific including China, Australia, New Zealand and India. BaiCaoWei, Kurkure, Smith's.

The vendor's seg_prod block reports FY2025 on this NEW six-segment basis and FY2024 and every prior year on the SUPERSEDED seven-segment basis — with one line carrying the wrong name entirely. The FY2025 line labelled "Africa, Middle East and South Asia" at $18.025B is not AMESA; AMESA was $6.217B the year before. It is the new EMEA segment, and the label is a leftover. The vendor also supplies a line called "International Beverage Franchise" that appears only in FY2025 — correctly, because the segment is new — and a "PepsiCo Foods North America" line that replaces the old Frito-Lay North America and Quaker Foods North America lines.

2. The single most important thing to understand about these numbers: the vendor's operating income is not PepsiCo's operating profit

The vendor reports FY2025 operating income of $13.491B, up 4.7% on FY2024's $12.887B. The 10-K states, in plain words: "Operating profit decreased 11%."

Both statements are internally consistent and one of them is not PepsiCo's income statement. The vendor's operatingIncome is computed as gross profit less selling, general and administrative expenses. PepsiCo's income statement carries a separate "Impairment of intangible assets" line below SG&A and inside operating profit — and the vendor drops it into the residual bucket nonOperatingIncomeExcludingInterest instead.

We rebuilt the line and it reconciles to the filing three separate ways.

The identity operatingIncome − nonOperatingIncomeExcludingInterest = incomeBeforeTax + interestExpense holds in every period, which tells us the residual bucket contains everything between the two. For FY2025 the residual is $2.126B. The 10-K discloses pre-tax impairment charges of $1.9 billion ($1.5 billion after-tax, or $1.11 per share) "in impairment of intangible assets primarily comprised of the Rockstar brand in our PBNA, EMEA, and IB Franchise segments", plus a separately-described Be & Cheery brand impairment in Asia Pacific Foods, and separately states that "Other pension and retiree medical benefits expense increased $111 million." Splitting the residual into an impairment line of ~$2.022B and a pension expense of ~$104M gives:

> Reported FY2025 operating profit = $13.491B − $2.022B = $11.469B

> Reported FY2024 operating profit ≈ $12.858B

> Change = −10.8%, i.e. the 10-K's "decreased 11%."

Check two — the June quarter. The 10-Q states "Operating profit increased 125%" for the 12 weeks. Our rebuild gives Q2 FY2026 operating profit of $4.023B (no impairment in the period) against Q2 FY2025 of $1.789B (a $1.860B impairment inside a vendor operatingIncome of $3.649B). $4.023B ÷ $1.789B = +124.9%.

Check three — the half year. The 10-Q states "Operating profit increased 65%" for the 24 weeks. Our rebuild gives $7.236B against $4.372B = +65.5%.

Three independent confirmations. The rebuild is correct and the vendor's operating-income series is not PepsiCo's operating profit in any year that carries an impairment.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: adjusted EPS growth (+4.8%, from FY2026E $8.554 to FY2027E $8.965) + multiple drift (HOLD at roughly 16.3x forward, 0%) + shareholder yield (+4.78%) ≈ +9.6%.

Our base case assumes the multiple HOLDS, and we should say why — because on a name in a two-year de-rating that is a real assumption, not a neutral one. The stock has fallen from a 52-week high of $170.49 to $139.10 and its twelve-month total price return is −0.13% against SPY's +24.26%. Something has been re-rating this name downward continuously. We assume it stops here for one specific reason: at 15.5x FY2027 with a 4.26% dividend yield and a 4.88% free cash flow yield, PepsiCo is priced at a level where the income buyer becomes the marginal buyer. A staple yielding 4.3% with an investment-grade balance sheet and $10B of undrawn revolvers does not usually de-rate to a 5% yield without a dividend cut or a recession. That is the floor argument, and it is the whole reason this is a Hold rather than an Avoid.

Note carefully what this bridge does NOT contain: any meaningful earnings growth. Consensus adjusted EPS growth is 5.4%, 4.8% and 5.6% for FY2026-FY2028. Roughly half the expected return is the dividend cheque. That is a completely legitimate way to make money and it is the correct way to own this stock — but it means the position is an income position, not a growth position, and it should be sized and judged as one.

Hold.

This is a legitimately ownable income asset trading at a fair price, and it is not a place we would put new money today.

  • Any indication that the dividend is at risk. Concretely: trailing free cash flow falling below $7.9B — the company's own 2026 dividend plan — for two consecutive quarters. That is the whole thesis, and it is a live number, not a hypothetical.
  • PepsiCo Beverages North America unit volume worse than −5% in the 2026-10-08 print, or PepsiCo Foods North America turning negative on units with negative pricing alongside. That is the GLP-1 and whole-foods thesis arriving in the P&L, and this stock has no growth to absorb it.

Single biggest risk: the dividend is the reason to own this and it has no cover. FY2025 dividends of $7.638B against $7.672B of free cash flow is 99.6%. FY2024 was 114.5% including buybacks; FY2023 96.9%; FY2022 136.9%. Four consecutive years of returning more than the business generated, funded with $8.2B of debt issuance in FY2025 alone. Trailing free cash flow has improved to $9.282B and management's 2026 plan of ~$8.9B is 95.9% of it — an improvement, and still no margin. If free cash flow reverts toward the FY2025 level while the dividend runs at $7.9B, PepsiCo is borrowing to pay its shareholders in a fifth consecutive year, at 2.28x leverage, into a rising tax rate. Nothing in this file suggests a cut is imminent, and we are not forecasting one. We are saying that the single asset the equity story rests on has no spare capacity in it, and that is a different and more precise statement than "the yield is safe."

Most fragile assumption in the price: that North American unit volume stops declining. Every other assumption embedded in $139.10 is a price, a currency or a cost assumption, and each is observable and manageable. This one is the actual number of bags and bottles people buy, in the 59.3% of the company that sits in the United States and Canada, and it has fallen for consecutive quarters while the company's own 10-K names "increasing use of weight-loss drugs, such as GLP-1 medications" as a driver of shifting diet. PepsiCo has offset the decline with price for two years, and in the June quarter PFNA's net revenue fell 2% on unfavourable net pricing — the first evidence that the offset has a limit. If units keep falling and pricing can no longer cover it, the consensus path of +2.9% and +3.3% revenue growth in FY2027 and FY2028 does not happen, adjusted EPS stalls near $8.50, and at 14x that is $119 — below our bear case. That is what the $116 anchor is describing.
