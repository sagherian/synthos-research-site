The Overview

Progressive sells car insurance. It is the second-largest personal auto insurer in the United States, it now covers just over 40 million policies, and it is unusually good at what it does: it costs less to run than its competitors and it is better than they are at working out which drivers are risky.

Insurance companies measure themselves with something called the combined ratio. It is the share of every premium dollar that goes out again as claims and expenses. Below 100 means a profit on the insurance itself; above 100 means a loss. Progressive has publicly told investors for years that its goal is to grow as fast as possible while keeping this number at or below 96 — that is, it wants to keep about four cents of every premium dollar as underwriting profit.

In the three months to June it kept 12.7 cents. Its combined ratio was 87.3.

That sounds like unambiguous good news, and in the short run it is. But an insurance market where one company is making three times its target profit does not stay that way. Rivals cut their prices to win customers. State regulators, who must approve rate changes, stop approving increases when insurers are visibly over-earning. And the over-earner has to spend more to keep its own customers.

All three of those things are already visible in Progressive's own filings. The company's growth in new premium slowed from 15% a year ago to 5%. It spent $1.4 billion on advertising in three months, 16% more than last year. Its own commercial division grew by cutting prices. And its combined ratio has begun to move the wrong way — 86.2 a year ago, 87.3 now, and in the single month of June, 90.0 against 86.6 a year earlier, with profit down 31%.

The analysts who follow the company have drawn the conclusion: they expect earnings per share of about $17.49 this year, $16.20 next year and $16.03 the year after — against roughly $19.24 last year. Three years of shrinking profits.

The shares are $210.75. That is about 10.6 times last year's earnings, which sounds cheap — but it is cheap against the best year, and the best year is what is ending. Measured against next year's expected earnings it is 13 times, and the multiple gets higher the further out you look, because the earnings get smaller.

There is a real bull case and it should be stated fairly. The number of policies Progressive covers is still growing 7% a year, it added 2.8 million in twelve months, and its net worth has grown from $20 billion to $34 billion in two and a half years. The shares have already fallen 17% from their high while the wider market rose 24%. At some price this becomes a good investment. Our estimate of that price is $215 — 2% above where it trades.

It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. 1. What the business is, and how the disclosure works

Progressive describes itself as an insurance holding company writing "personal and commercial insurance throughout the United States." Chief executive Susan Patricia Griffith; approximately 70,053 employees per the vendor profile; Mayfield Village, Ohio; NYSE-listed.

Two reportable underwriting segments as at the June 2026 10-Q — and the vendor's seg_prod block is out of date on the structure.

  • Personal Lines — personal vehicles (auto and "special lines": motorcycles, RVs, watercraft) plus personal property (homeowners, renters, umbrella, and flood through the National Flood Insurance Program's Write Your Own arrangement). 88% of companywide net premiums written in the June quarter. Personal auto is approximately 90% of Personal Lines net premiums written.
  • Commercial Lines — commercial auto liability and physical damage, business general liability and commercial property for small businesses, and workers' compensation for the transportation industry. Core commercial auto is roughly 80% of the segment.

seg_prod shows a THREE-segment structure through FY2023 and TWO from FY2024 — Personal Lines, Commercial Lines and a separate Property Segment ($2,551.4M in FY2023, $2,270.0M in FY2022) that disappears. This is not a data error: Progressive folded the Property segment into Personal Lines, and the 10-Q's segment note confirms the current two-segment structure. But the vendor supplies no note saying so, and a reader comparing FY2023's three lines to FY2025's two would conclude a segment vanished — the MTZ Power Delivery failure mode. The FY2021 row is a genuine defect: it shows "Agency Channel" $16,881.0M and "Direct Channel" $18,492.3M, which is a distribution-channel split, not a segment split, and totals $35,373M against FY2021 revenue of $47,676M. That row is discarded.

seg_prod FY2025 sums to $82,855M (Personal Lines $71,838M + Commercial Lines $11,017M) against reported total revenue of $87,637M — a $4,782M gap of 5.5%. That gap is investment income, net realised gains, fees and service revenue, which sit outside the underwriting segments. It is not a missing segment; it is the correct structure of an insurer's disclosure, and it is stated here so no reader treats the shortfall as an omission.

seg_geo is EMPTY. Progressive writes only in the United States, so there is nothing to report — this is the CVS/PDD empty-block pattern occurring for a legitimate reason, and it is recorded as legitimate rather than as a defect.

Why 87.3 against a 96 goal is a problem rather than an achievement. The 10-K states the objective explicitly: "our long-standing goal of growing as fast as we can, at or below a companywide 96 combined ratio on a calendar-year basis", and adds that "our focus on achieving our underwriting profitability goal takes precedence over growth." Progressive is currently 8.7 points inside its own target. In an industry where prices require state regulatory approval and competitors observe each other's published results monthly, that surplus is temporary by construction. The 10-Q documents all three channels of erosion in its own words:

> "We also reported steady year-over-year growth in both premiums and policies in force despite increased competition in the marketplace."

> Commercial Lines growth was "primarily driven by volume growth due to rate decreases in targeted state and BMT combinations, and increased advertising and agent incentive spend."

> "During the second quarter 2026, advertising expense was $1.4 billion, or 16% higher than the second quarter last year."

And the growth deceleration is the confirming evidence. Personal Lines net premiums written grew 5% in Q2 2026 against 15% in Q2 2025; policies in force grew 8% against 16%. The company is spending 16% more on advertising to produce a third of the premium growth. That is what a market normalising from a hard cycle looks like from the inside.

The arithmetic of the risk, stated plainly. At the current run rate of roughly $86B of annual net premiums earned, each point of combined ratio is approximately $860M of pre-tax underwriting profit, or about $1.16 of after-tax EPS on 581 million shares. Moving from 87.3 to the company's own 96 goal would remove roughly $10 of annual EPS — more than half of it. Nobody, including us, expects that to happen in one step. But it establishes the size of the lever, and it is the reason a 10.6x trailing multiple on this company is not the bargain it appears to be.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (NEGATIVE, −7.4% from FY2026E $17.49 to FY2027E $16.20) + multiple drift (EXPANSION, from 12.05x to roughly 13.3x, +10%) + shareholder yield (roughly 1-4%, dominated by a variable dividend that shrinks with the earnings) ≈ +2% to +5%.

This is an unusual bridge and it should be read carefully. For most names the earnings grow and the argument is about the multiple. Here the earnings SHRINK and the base case requires the multiple to EXPAND — from 12.05x on the current year to roughly 13.3x on the next — simply for the price to hold. We assume that expansion because a business whose earnings have troughed and whose unit growth continues normally re-rates on the way through the trough, not on the way into it, and because 3.57x book for a company that will still earn a mid-twenties return on equity after normalisation is not an expensive starting point.

But it means almost none of the expected return is earnings. At a constant 12.05x multiple applied to FY2027E of $16.20, the price would be $195 (−7.4%). At 14x it would be $227 (+7.7%). The base case depends on a re-rating, and that is the fragile leg — stated explicitly, as the format requires.

The distinction that matters. Progressive is not a bad business and this is not a bearish note about its quality — it is the best-run company in its industry and the unit growth proves it. The issue is that the earnings being capitalised at 10.6x are the top of an underwriting cycle, the company's own monthly disclosures show that cycle turning, and its own analysts have already written the decline into their models. A cheap-looking multiple on peak cyclical earnings is the single most reliable value trap in property-casualty insurance, and the arithmetic here — 870 basis points of surplus margin, each point worth $1.16 of EPS — quantifies exactly how much room there is to fall. With our base 2.0% above the price, symmetric risk, and no name-level conviction, Watch is the arithmetic answer.
