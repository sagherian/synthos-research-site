The Overview

Parker-Hannifin makes the unglamorous parts that make machines work: seals that stop leaks, filters that clean fluids and air, hydraulic and pneumatic systems that push and pull, and — increasingly — the control systems inside aircraft. It sells them to factory builders, farm and construction equipment makers, refrigeration and air-conditioning firms, and aeroplane manufacturers. It has paid a dividend for 303 quarters in a row and raised it every year for 70 years.

Two things about it matter right now.

The first is good. Over the last four years the aerospace part of the company has gone from about one-sixth of sales to about one-third, mostly because Parker bought a British aerospace company called Meggitt in 2022. Aerospace parts earn a better margin than industrial parts, especially the spare parts sold years after the aircraft was built, and Parker's aerospace margin has risen from about 20 cents on the dollar to more than 25. The order book behind that business is $8.4 billion and growing 15% a year. Meanwhile the industrial half, which shrank in the year to June 2025, has started growing again.

The second is not in any stock screen. Last November Parker agreed to buy a company called Filtration Group for $9.25 billion in cash. It has arranged the loans but not yet drawn them, and the deal has not closed. When it does, Parker's borrowings roughly double. The company already owes about $9.1 billion; afterwards it will owe closer to $18.4 billion. Parker generates about $3.3 billion of spare cash a year, so this is real money — roughly three years of it. And $5.25 billion of the loan matures in one year, which means Parker will have to refinance it almost immediately.

None of the data feeds we buy contain a single field for that transaction. We found it by reading the quarterly report.

The shares cost $992.65. That is about 29 times what analysts expect the company to earn in the year to June 2027. Our own estimate of what they are worth is $1,000 — less than one percent above the price. The average analyst says $1,062, about 7% above. The most pessimistic analyst says $950, which is below today's price. And the company reports its full-year results in two days.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Sound operations, a 70-year dividend record — attached to a balance sheet that is about to take on $9.25 billion of new debt, with no share-price cushion.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Genuinely re-accelerating, but off a flat two-year base, and concentrated in one of two segments.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. A supplier of components, not a platform. The interesting part is a data-centre and semiconductor adjacency that is real but not separately reported.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $1,000 against a current price of $992.65 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure works

Parker describes itself in the 10-K as "a global leader in motion and control technologies. Leveraging a unique combination of interconnected technologies, we design, manufacture, and provide aftermarket support for highly engineered solutions that create value for customers primarily in aerospace & defense, in-plant & industrial equipment, transportation, off-highway, energy, and HVAC & refrigeration markets around the world." Chief executive Jennifer A. Parmentier; approximately 57,950 employees per the vendor profile; Cleveland, Ohio; NYSE-listed since well before the 1980 date the vendor records as an IPO.

Two reportable segments, and the vendor gets this exactly right.

  • Diversified Industrial — an aggregation of business units selling sealing and shielding, filtration, fluid connectors, hydraulics, pneumatics, engineered materials and thermal management into industrial equipment, transportation, off-highway, energy and HVAC/refrigeration. Sold direct to OEMs and through independent distributors. $13.665B in FY2025, 68.8% of revenue.
  • Aerospace Systems — airframe and engine components and systems for commercial transport, defence fixed wing, business jets, regional transport, helicopters and energy applications. $6.185B in FY2025, 31.2% of revenue.

The seg_prod block reconciles exactly: $13,665M + $6,185M = $19,850M, matching the reported FY2025 revenue of $19,850M to the dollar. No missing segment, no double count.

seg_geo reconciles exactly as well — North America $13,406M + Europe $3,862M + Asia Pacific $2,364M + Latin America $218M = $19,850M. This is one of the cleanest vendor-to-filing reconciliations in the batch, and the home market is present — North America is 67.5% of revenue, which is the specific failure mode (SYK, DHR, BMY) that this check exists to catch. It passes. Note that the FY2017 and FY2018 rows of both blocks contain only North America and are structurally incomplete; they are not used.

This is the most important finding in this dive and it comes entirely from the filings.

> "On November 11, 2025, the Company announced that it has agreed to acquire Filtration Group Corporation ('Filtration Group') from Madison Industries for approximately $9.25 billion in cash. ... The transaction is subject to customary closing conditions, including receipt of applicable regulatory approvals, and is expected to close within 12 months from the announcement date."

> — 10-Q filed 2026-05-01, Note 4

> "On December 10, 2025, we entered into a 364-Day Term Loan Agreement and a Three-Year Term Loan Agreement, which provide for senior unsecured delayed draw term loan facilities in aggregate principal amounts of $5.25 billion and $2.50 billion, respectively. ... Two honest caveats on that table. First, Filtration Group's own revenue and EBITDA are not disclosed in any Parker filing in this archive, so the pro-forma leverage denominator is an assumption, not a datum. The ~$750M figure is what a $9.25B enterprise price implies at roughly 12x, which is a typical multiple for a filtration asset — it is illustrative and labelled as such, and no conclusion in this dive rests on it. Second, the split between new debt and cash is not disclosed; Parker held $476M of cash at 31 March 2026, so the overwhelming majority must be debt.

Why it matters beyond the leverage line. The 364-day tranche is $5.25B — more than half the consideration, maturing one year after first draw. Parker's stated financing preference is different: "We primarily utilize unsecured medium-term notes and senior notes to meet our financing needs." Bridging $5.25B into the bond market inside twelve months is a refinancing event on a scale Parker has not done since Meggitt, and it will happen at whatever rates prevail then.

And there is a naming trap inside the payload itself, worth flagging because it is exactly the class of error this process exists to catch. The insider block records transactions by "Jacobson Matthew A., officer: VP & Pres.-Filtration Grp." That is the head of Parker's own existing Filtration Group business unit, not an executive of the acquisition target Filtration Group Corporation. The two are unrelated entities sharing a name. We checked; they are not the same thing.
