The Overview

Big organisations — armies, hospitals, banks, carmakers — have their information scattered across hundreds of incompatible computer systems that were bought at different times and do not talk to each other. This is the reason most attempts to use artificial intelligence inside a large organisation fail: the machine cannot find, trust, or act on the data.

Palantir's product is a way of solving that. It builds a layer that maps all of an organisation's messy data onto a consistent picture of the real things the organisation cares about — this aircraft, this patient, this shipment, this customer — and then lets software and AI act on that picture. It also sends its own engineers to live inside the customer for months to make it work.

That is unglamorous, expensive, and apparently extremely hard to copy. It is also, right now, growing faster than almost anything of its size in the world. In the three months to June 2026 Palantir's sales were 93% higher than a year earlier. Its sales to American companies — as opposed to the American government, which was its original business — were 149% higher. And unlike most fast-growing software companies, it is genuinely profitable: it kept 47 cents of every sales dollar as operating profit and 63 cents as cash.

The company published those numbers after the market closed on Monday. On Tuesday the shares rose 29.4%, from $125.65 to $162.61, on more than four times the usual amount of trading. That is the highest single-day move of any company in this batch.

Here is the difficulty. At $162.61 the shares are worth about $391 billion, and the company will sell roughly $8.2 billion of software this year. You are therefore paying about 48 times this year's sales. For comparison, most excellent software companies trade at five to ten times sales. Palantir may well be worth it — if sales keep roughly doubling, the number comes down very quickly on its own. But it also means there is no protection if growth slows even to a merely excellent rate.

And this has already happened once. Within the last twelve months the shares fell from $207 to $107 — a 48% fall — while the business itself was getting much bigger. Over the whole twelve months, even after today's 29% jump, the shares are up only 5.4% while the American stock market is up 24.3%. Owning this company has been an unpleasant experience for a year despite everything going right.

One more thing worth knowing: Palantir currently pays almost no tax — an effective rate of 1.4% — because it is still using up losses from its unprofitable years. That flatters the reported profit. If you tax it properly at 21%, the shares are on about 173 times earnings rather than 138.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $168 against a price of $162.61 — a 3.3% gap, which is no gap at all. The average analyst says $172, but every one of those estimates was written before yesterday's results, so they are about to change.

  • Downside Risk 8/10. No debt and $9.4 billion in the bank, so the company is completely safe. The share price is not: it has already halved once in a year while the business doubled.
  • Growth Quality 9/10. Growth that gets faster every year for four years running, at high and rising profit margins, with less dilution than before. Close to the best there is.
  • Exponential Potential 9/10. The thing that makes this unusual is not the growth rate but that the growth rate keeps rising at a size where it should be falling.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $168 against a current price of $162.61 — real upside if our numbers are right.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

Palantir was founded in 2003, listed by direct listing on 2020-09-30, and is led by co-founder and chief executive Alexander C. Karp. Headquarters in Miami. 4,429 full-time employees — against trailing revenue of $6.156B, that is roughly $1.39 million of revenue per employee, a figure worth holding against the rest of this batch.

Two reportable segments, and the 10-Q defines the reporting metric unusually. Palantir reports Government and Commercial and evaluates each on contribution — segment revenue less the related cost of revenue and sales and marketing expense, excluding stock-based compensation, research and development, and general and administrative costs, which are managed at the corporate level. This is not gross profit and not operating profit, and it is the company's own construction. We report it because it is the only segment profitability disclosed, and we label it.
