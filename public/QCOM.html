The Overview

Qualcomm does two things. It designs the chips that run most high-end Android phones — and, increasingly, cars, laptops and connected devices. And it owns a large share of the patents that any mobile phone must use to connect to a network at all, so it collects a royalty on almost every phone sold in the world regardless of whose chip is inside.

The second business is small in revenue and enormous in profit: about 13% of sales and, on one independent estimate, about 30% of operating profit, at gross margins of 85-90%.

The company generates a great deal of cash — nearly $13 billion last year, against capital spending of just $1.2 billion — and gives essentially all of it back. Last year it bought back $8.8 billion of shares and paid $3.8 billion of dividends: about 7.4% of the entire company's value returned in one year.

The problem is that sales are falling. Last quarter revenue was down 4% on a year earlier, and profit from operations was down 41%. Analysts expect earnings to fall this year and again next year before recovering in 2028.

Two things you would not see on a screen. First, Qualcomm's reported profits over the last four quarters are almost meaningless: a change in US tax law forced the company to write off $5.7 billion of tax assets in autumn 2025, and a Treasury notice in spring 2026 let it write them straight back on. One quarter showed a $3.1 billion loss; two quarters later, a $7.4 billion profit on $2.3 billion of operating income. Second, and more important: the company's own filing says one customer is 23% of its revenue and another is 20% — 43% between two buyers — and that the larger one has grown from 18% in a year while a third has shrunk from 13% to under 10%.

The shares are $162.67, having fallen 35% from their high of $251 and bounced 31% off their low of $124. They rose 7.3% today with the whole semiconductor sector. Our estimate of value is $180. Analysts average $204 — but their individual targets run from $120 to $400, which tells you how little anyone agrees.

  • Downside Risk 7/10. Almost no debt and enormous cash generation, against the worst customer concentration here and a shrinking top line.
  • Growth Quality 4/10. Revenue is falling and consensus expects two down years of earnings.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. Cars, connected devices and edge computing are a real option. It has not yet converted.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $180 against a current price of $162.67 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

What we are declining is to buy a shrinking business six days after a miss, ninety-two days before a print consensus already expects to be 25% down, with the entry including a 7% gap. Most fragile assumption in the price: that fiscal 2028 revenue reaches $51.709 billion. Which, at 7.37% a year, is not nothing — and that is the honest reason this is a Watch rather than an Avoid.
