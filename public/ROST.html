The Overview

Ross Stores runs two chains of discount clothing shops — Ross Dress for Less and dd's DISCOUNTS — with 2,282 locations, all in the United States. It buys brand-name clothing and homeware cheaply, usually because someone else over-ordered it, and sells it at 20% to 60% below department-store prices. It has done this for decades and it is very good at it.

In the three months to early May 2026 it had the best quarter it has ever had. Sales at stores open more than a year rose 17%. Most of that was more people walking in the door, not people spending more — which is the healthier of the two. Profit per share went from $1.47 to $2.02.

The stock has noticed. It is up 83% in a year while the broad market is up 24%. It is within three quarters of one percent of its highest price ever, and — unusually — it has not had a single meaningful dip in twelve months.

Here is the arithmetic problem. At $251.11 you are paying about 32 times what analysts think the company will earn in the year ending January 2027, and about 29 times the January 2028 figure. Those same analysts think profit growth slows to about 10% a year after this one. Historically this company has traded well below 30 times earnings. So the price now needs the extraordinary quarter to become the ordinary quarter.

Analysts covering the stock have an average price target of $259.89 — about 3.5% above today's price. They do not see much left either.

Our estimate of fair value is $233, roughly 7% below the current price. If the growth normalises and the multiple returns to a more typical level, we get $170. If the company keeps compounding at this rate and holds a high multiple, we get $295. That is a worse-than-even bet.

The company reports again on 20 August, sixteen days from now. That report answers the only question that matters.

One more thing worth saying plainly: our expert knowledge base — 52,021 recorded claims from investors, economists and analysts — contains not one claim about Ross Stores. We are not going to pretend otherwise.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Net cash, no debt problem, a counter-cyclical model — attached to a stock with no drawdown and an RSI of 79.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. A 17% comparable-sales quarter driven by traffic is the real thing. One quarter is one quarter.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. The growth engine is building about 110 more shops a year. That is linear and it is fine.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $233 against a current price of $251.11 — a premium price for a business we still like.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Four observations.

First, the comp is traffic-led and that is the version that matters. The 10-Q: "The 17% increase in comp store sales was primarily driven by an approximately 11% increase in traffic and 6% increase in basket." A basket-led comp can be inflation or mix. An 11% increase in the number of transactions at a 2,282-store chain is people choosing to come, and it is the signature of share capture from failing competitors — the exact mechanism that makes off-price counter-cyclical.

Second, the margin came from merchandise, not from cutting. Of the 145 basis points of cost-of-goods improvement, 85 basis points was merchandise margin, 60 was occupancy leverage, 15 distribution and 10 domestic freight, partly offset by 25 basis points of higher buying costs from incentive compensation. Merchandise margin expanding while traffic rises 11% means the goods were bought well, not marked down to move.

Third, the base was soft. Q1 FY2025 comps were +3% and sales growth +3%. The 21% is measured against the weakest quarter in the recent series. That does not make it less real; it makes it less extrapolable.

Fourth, operating cash flow doubled — and roughly half of it is working capital timing. Accounts payable leverage (payables ÷ inventory) rose to 89% from 81%, which the company attributes to "the timing of inventory receipts and related payments." Payables contributed $262,115k of the increase. That is a genuine cash benefit and it does not repeat.

3. The tariff position — an unrecognised asset and a live exposure

Two filings address it, and the company has taken the conservative side of both.

From the 10-K (subsequent events): "On February 20, 2026, the United States Supreme Court issued a decision that tariffs imposed in 2025 under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act were not authorized under the statute. The Company is currently evaluating the impact of this ruling..."

From the 10-Q, ten weeks later: "U.S. Customs and Border Protection has initiated a process for claiming refunds of tariffs paid under IEEPA, and the Company has submitted claims for IEEPA tariffs paid... As of May 2, 2026, the Company has not recognized a receivable or gain relating to tariff refunds because realization was not assured."

So: a $0.16 per share fiscal-2025 cost, a Supreme Court decision voiding the statutory basis, claims filed, and nothing whatever booked. That is unquantified upside sitting outside every estimate in this file. It is also a reminder of the standing exposure — the 10-K states that "more than half of the goods we sell originate from China", and that Ross "directly import[s] only a small portion", meaning most tariff cost arrives embedded in domestic vendors' prices and is neither controllable nor precisely measurable by the company.
