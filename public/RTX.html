The Overview

RTX is three big businesses stapled together.

The first, Collins Aerospace, makes the parts of an aircraft that are not the engine or the airframe: the cockpit avionics, the landing gear, the wheels and brakes, the cabin interiors, the oxygen and galley systems, the electrical power. If you have flown, you have sat inside Collins equipment.

The second, Pratt & Whitney, makes engines. Two matter. The geared turbofan powers a large share of the world's newest single-aisle airliners — the Airbus A320neo family, the A220 and the Embraer E2 — more than 2,600 aircraft in service with over 90 airlines. The F135 is the only engine used on the F-35 fighter, every one of them, everywhere.

The third, Raytheon, makes missiles and the radars that aim them: Patriot, AMRAAM, the Standard Missile family, SM-3, NASAMS, Stinger, Tomahawk, StormBreaker, SPY-6. This is the business that has been in the news for three years.

Two things make this company unusual to analyse, and both are good.

First, you can see the future revenue. Because aircraft and weapons are sold years before they are delivered, RTX discloses a "backlog" — the value of firm orders already signed but not yet delivered. At the end of June 2026 that was $289 billion. The company's whole revenue for the last twelve months was $93.5 billion. So the order book is more than three years of sales, and it grew by $21 billion in the six months of this year alone. The company says about a quarter of it turns into revenue within twelve months. Very few businesses can show you that.

Second, the growth is accelerating while the analysts expect it to slow. Revenue in the June quarter was up 14.5% on a year earlier — the best in the two years of quarterly data we hold — and the company has beaten profit forecasts five quarters in a row by an average of 13%. Yet the consensus forecast has revenue growing only about 7% a year from here.

So why not simply buy it?

Because of the price, and because of what the profit is made of. The shares are $217.93 and the company earned $5.68 a share over the last twelve months on the official accounting basis. That is 38 times earnings for a company growing profits about 10% a year.

Worse, $0.67 of that $5.68 is not operating profit at all. It is "non-service pension income" — an accounting credit that arises because RTX's very large pension fund is expected to earn more on its investments than the cost of the benefits accruing that year. Accounting rules require it to be reported below the operating profit line precisely because it is not part of running the business. Strip it out and the company earned $5.01 a share, which is 43 times the share price.

Two other things a buyer should know. RTX has effectively stopped buying back its own shares — $12.9 billion in 2023, $50 million in all of 2025 — and the number of shares outstanding is therefore slowly rising, which dilutes each holder by about 0.8% a year. And a U.S. Executive Order issued in January 2026, which the company discloses in its own annual report, allows the Secretary of War to try to stop RTX paying dividends or buying back stock if the government decides it has underperformed on its contracts. That is an unusual risk and it is real enough that the company wrote it down.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $227. They cost $217.93. That is a 4% gap, and the average analyst says $240 — higher than we do. The shares are also within a whisker of their highest closing price of the past year and the momentum indicator is at 80 out of 100, which is about as stretched as it gets. When our own number is barely above the price, sits below the crowd's, and the chart is extended, the correct answer is to wait.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Three years of orders already signed, a low-volatility share, and improving credit ratings — attached to a full valuation, negative tangible net worth, and a pension credit propping up a ninth of the earnings.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. Revenue up 9.7% last year and 14.5% in the latest quarter, with an order book growing faster than sales. Marked down because analysts expect it to slow to 7% and because part of the profit is not operating profit.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. RTX grows when governments appropriate money and when people fly. Both are real and durable. Neither compounds.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $227 against a current price of $217.93 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: adjusted EPS growth (+10.8%, from 2027E $7.892 to 2028E $8.747) + multiple drift (mild COMPRESSION, from 27.6x to 26x on the respective forward year, −5.8%) + shareholder yield (+1.34% dividend, less roughly 0.8% dilution, net ≈ +0.5%) ≈ +4% to +6%.

Our base case assumes modest multiple COMPRESSION, and it is not a rolldown artefact. Today the market pays 27.6x on the next calendar year's estimate. If in twelve months it pays 26x on the then-next year's estimate, that is a genuine de-rating of about 6%, not the mechanical effect of the calendar advancing. We assume it for three reasons, all arithmetic rather than atmospheric. First, consensus growth itself decelerates on its own path: revenue +8.5%, +7.4%, +7.2%, +5.1%, +3.5% across 2026–2030. Second, the quality of the earnings is deteriorating at the margin — a rising share of a shrinking pension credit is arithmetically impossible, so as the $1.1B line decays it becomes a drag on reported growth that operations must offset. Third, RTX re-rated 26% in three months to arrive here, and multiples that arrive that fast without a change in the growth algorithm tend not to be sticky.

Almost all of the expected return is earnings growth, and the multiple is working against it. If the multiple simply HELD at 27.6x on 2028E the price would be $241 (+10.8%); if it compressed to 22x the price would be $192 (−11.7%) — which is, not coincidentally, the 50-day moving average. The entire spread between a good and a mediocre outcome sits in a multiple we do not control and have no edge on.

The bull case at $273 requires modest expansion — 29x applied to the 2028 consensus high of $9.407 — and is therefore the least conservative of our three anchors, deliberately, because the backlog gives a mechanism for the upgrade. The bear case at $163 requires no operational disaster at all: 20x the 2028 consensus low of $8.171. A 20x multiple is not a crisis multiple for an industrial growing 7%; it is a normal one. That asymmetry — a bear case that needs only a normal multiple and a bull case that needs an above-average one — is the core of the Watch.
