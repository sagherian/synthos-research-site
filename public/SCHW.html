The Overview

Schwab looks after other people's money. Eleven trillion, seven hundred and seventy billion dollars of it, belonging to individual investors and to the independent financial advisers who use Schwab to hold their clients' accounts. It makes money three ways: charging a fee on funds it manages, charging for trades, and — this is the big one — taking the cash sitting idle in customer accounts, paying almost nothing on it, and lending or investing it at a much higher rate.

That third business is why the shares have recovered. Two years ago Schwab was paying a lot for that cash, because interest rates had risen and customers moved money to accounts that paid more. Today it pays 0.20% on $243 billion of deposits, against 0.72% a year ago. At the same time, a large portfolio of bonds bought years ago at very low yields — $132 billion earning 1.72% — is gradually maturing and being reinvested at today's much higher rates. Both of those are automatic. Neither requires anyone to do anything clever. Together they took the company's lending margin from 2.53% to 2.88%.

The result is that profits are climbing fast. Analysts expect earnings per share to go from $4.66 last year to about $9.12 in 2028, and the company is buying back its own shares at a rate of nearly $10 billion a year — about 5% of the whole company annually.

Three things you will not find on a stock screen. First, the "revenue" figure most data feeds report for Schwab, about $27.7 billion, is not a number Schwab publishes; the company's actual top line is $23.9 billion, and any "price to sales" you see is calculated on the wrong figure. Second, the reported book value includes $6.8 billion of preferred shares that rank ahead of ordinary shareholders. Third, and most important: Schwab holds bonds it intends to keep until maturity, which it therefore records at cost. If it had to sell them today it would lose $9.9 billion. That loss is real, it is disclosed, and it appears in no ratio anywhere.

The shares are $106.35, within 1% of their highest price in a year. The founder, Charles Schwab, sold 143,450 shares six days ago at almost exactly today's price. Our estimate of value is $120; the average analyst says $126.50. Everything about the business is improving, and everything about the entry point is unattractive.

  • Growth Quality 7/10. Strong, and substantially a rate mechanic that has a finite life.

1. What the business is, and the revenue line the vendor invents

Chief executive Richard Andrew Wurster; 33,000 employees; NYSE-listed; CIK 0000316709; reporting in USD. Charles Schwab remains co-chairman and a director.

The most important structural finding in this file, and it affects every screen. The vendor reports fiscal-2025 revenue of $27.675B and grossProfit of $23.921B, with interestExpense of $3.754B bridging them. Schwab does not report $27.675B as revenue anywhere. Its stated top line is "total net revenues", which is revenue after interest expense — and that is the vendor's grossProfit line. Three independent confirmations:

1. est.revenueAvg for fiscal 2025 is $23.955B (13 analysts, range $23.764B–$24.176B) — within 0.1% of grossProfit, and 13.4% below revenue.

2. earn_cal for 2026-07-21 reports revenueActual of $7,072M, which equals the vendor's June-quarter grossProfit of $7.072B exactly and not its revenue of $8.518B.

3. The seg_prod block sums to $23,921M for fiscal 2025 — Investor Services $18,999M plus Advisor Services $4,922M — matching grossProfit to the dollar.

Two mechanics, both one-way and both finite.

First, the funding cost collapsed. Deposits cost 0.20% against 0.72% — on a $242.7 billion balance that is roughly $1.26 billion of annualised saving. The 10-Q attributes the margin expansion to "reduced aggregate use of wholesale funding and lower rates paid on funding sources" more than offsetting "lower yields on floating" assets. Second, the legacy securities book is repricing. Held-to-maturity balances fell from $144,401M to $132,192M in twelve months — roughly $12.2 billion of runoff a year out of a portfolio yielding 1.72%, which is less than half a current coupon. This one has years left in it and it is the more durable half of the story. It is also the source of the $9,911M of unrecognised losses discussed in Section 3: the same low coupon that makes the runoff valuable is what makes the book worth less than cost today.

3. Capital, and the $9,911 million that is in no vendor field

Correction 1 — the vendor's equity includes preferred stock. The vendor reports totalStockholdersEquity of $49,425M at 2025-12-31 and priceToBookRatioTTM of 3.699x. The available-for-sale loss is already in equity — accumulated other comprehensive loss is $10,757M at 2026-03-31, improved from $10,983M at year-end. The held-to-maturity loss of $9,911M is NOT. Held-to-maturity securities are carried at amortised cost, so the difference between $131,671M of cost and $123,051M of fair value never touches the balance sheet unless the securities are sold.

Marking it:

> Tangible common equity $23,478M

> − unrecognised HTM losses $9,911M

> = adjusted tangible common equity $13,567M → $7.80 per share → price / adjusted tangible book 13.6x

This is the off-balance-sheet item class in its purest form: a $9.9 billion obligation-equivalent with no vendor field, no ratio, and no screen that shows it. It is also the item the knowledge base's oldest bearish claim on this name is about — a 2023 claim that "Schwab is technically insolvent — losses on long-duration assets exceed capital". We can adjudicate that claim directly and the answer is no: $9,911M of unrecognised loss against $23,478M of tangible common equity and $42,662M of common equity is a large haircut, not insolvency. It was a far closer call in 2023. It is not one now — but the item is still there and it is still not on any screen.

5b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over roughly two years decomposes as: adjusted EPS growth (+40.3%, from FY2026E $6.501 to FY2028E $9.120) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 16.4x to 13x on the respective forward year, −21%) + shareholder yield (+6.4% a year, ~13% compounded) ≈ +25% to +30% total, or roughly +12% annualised.

Our base assumes meaningful multiple compression and the reason is the source of the earnings. Today the market pays 16.4x on a year in which EPS grows 39.5%. The growth is a rate mechanic with a visible end: deposit costs at 0.20% cannot fall further, and the securities book has a finite runoff. Consensus itself shows growth decelerating to 21.3% and then 15.7%. A multiple appropriate to 40% growth is not appropriate to 15% growth, particularly when the 15% is half buyback.

Note that shareholder yield is doing unusually heavy lifting here — 6.4% a year is the highest in this batch, and roughly half the expected return. That is a genuine strength and it is also a dependency: it requires the Tier 1 leverage ratio to tolerate a $9-10 billion annual buyback, and that ratio has fallen a full point in a year.

If the multiple simply HELD at 16.4x on FY2028E the price would be $150 (+41%); if it compressed to 11x the price would be $100 (−6%). The bull case at $140 is 16.4x the FY2027 consensus HIGH of $8.539 — today's multiple held against an earnings beat, requiring no re-rating. The bear case at $87 is 12.5x the FY2027 consensus LOW of $6.936, which is a modest de-rating against a modest shortfall and lands almost exactly at the 52-week low of $85.35.
