The Overview

Snowflake sells a place to keep and query corporate data. Companies load their information into it, run questions against it, and pay for the computing power used. It is one of two serious independent companies doing this; the other, Databricks, is private and therefore has no share price to compare against.

The business is doing well. Sales grew 29% last year to $4.7 billion, and in the three months to April they grew 33.5% — faster, not slower, which is unusual at this size. The company has beaten analysts' forecasts five quarters in a row, by between 12% and 31%. Its losses are shrinking: it lost about 43 cents on every dollar of sales a year ago and about 23 cents now.

The problem is what the shares cost. At $316.77 you are paying:

  • about 22 times annual sales — not profits, sales;
  • about 164 times what analysts expect the company to earn in the year to January 2027;
  • about 94 times the spare cash it generates — a cash return of 1.07% a year;
  • and even five years out, on the January 2031 forecasts, about 53 times.

There is a further problem with the "earnings" in those figures. Snowflake pays its staff heavily in shares. Last year that cost $1.6 billion — 34% of all revenue, and more than the $1.1 billion of spare cash the company generated. The profit figures analysts quote exclude that cost entirely. Under normal accounting rules Snowflake lost $3.95 per share last year. And despite spending $874 million buying its own shares back, the total number of shares went up, from 337 million to 347 million — so existing owners were diluted anyway.

The shares have risen 119.7% in three months — more than four times the next-largest move among the twelve companies in this batch — and now stand at their highest closing price in our data. Of the 52 analysts who follow the company, the average target price is $285.20 — about 10% BELOW where the shares trade. The most pessimistic says $177.

And in the last week of July, two insiders sold about $23 million of stock: the head of product management sold roughly 30,000 shares at $300, and a co-founder who sits on the board sold 50,000 shares at $280.

Our own estimate of fair value is $270, about 15% below the price. The company reports again in 22 days.

  • Growth Quality 8/10. Genuinely excellent — accelerating revenue and narrowing losses.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. A real platform in a genuinely expanding market, contested by a strong private rival.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.
