The Overview

Snowflake stores and organises companies' data in the cloud so they can ask questions of it. For a while, investors worried that the AI boom would pass it by, because AI seemed to care about messy things — documents, images, transcripts — rather than the neat tables Snowflake specialises in. That worry has faded, because the numbers got better rather than worse: revenue grew 33.5% in the most recent quarter, faster than the 29% it grew for the year before. The company now throws off about $1.1 billion of spare cash a year.

So this is a good business that is getting better. The catch is the price. The stock has more than doubled in three months and sits at its highest price ever. Analysts who cover it — 42 of whom rate it a Buy — have an average target of $285, which is below today's $307.53. In other words, even the enthusiasts think it has run past them. And in the last week of July, two senior insiders including a co-founder sold about $23 million of stock between $280 and $300; nobody bought.

There is one more thing worth understanding. Snowflake reports two versions of its profit. The version Wall Street quotes — about $1.93 per share expected this year — leaves out the cost of the stock the company hands to its employees, which last year came to $1.6 billion, or about a third of all revenue. On the official accounting, the company lost $1.33 billion. It is generating real cash, so this is not a solvency worry. But it does mean the "159 times earnings" you are paying is measured against a generously defined earnings number.

Hold it if you own it. If you're building a position, wait for a pullback — the stock's own 50-day average sits around $250.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. No solvency risk at all; the risk is that a very high multiple meets one soft quarter.
  • Growth Quality 8/10. Accelerating growth at a $4.7bn revenue base is genuinely rare and genuinely good.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. The AI data layer is a real S-curve, with one credible unanswered objection.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $280 against a current price of $307.53 — a premium price for a business we still like.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

Founded 2012, headquartered in Bozeman, Montana. CEO Sridhar Ramaswamy. 9,060 full-time employees. Listed 2020-09-16.

3. The balance sheet and the dilution problem

Balance sheet (2026-01-31):

  • Cash and short-term investments $4.030B; total debt $2.741B; net cash $87M.
  • Deferred revenue $3.347B (from $2.580B, +29.7%) — a real forward-booking signal.
  • Total equity $1.924B (down from $3.000B a year earlier, on buybacks and losses); accumulated deficit -$9.494B.
  • Current ratio 1.00x. Goodwill and intangibles $1.441B, so tangible book value per share is negative (-$0.14).
  • Debt-to-equity 1.43x; the debt is convertible in nature (total debt jumped from $288M in FY24 to $2.685B in FY25 on a $2.073B issuance).

The dilution arithmetic deserves its own paragraph, because it is the least-discussed risk in the name. Diluted share count: 328.0M (FY24) → 332.7M (FY25) → 337.5M (FY26) → 345.4M (Q1 FY27). That is a 5.3% increase over the period despite the company spending $1.932B on buybacks in FY25 and $873.5M in FY26 — $2.8B of shareholder capital deployed, and the count still went up. The Q1 FY27 jump alone is +2.3% in a single quarter. Stock-based compensation at 34.1% of revenue is issuing shares faster than $2.8B of repurchases can retire them.

What this means for the multiple: the non-GAAP EPS series the market quotes (FY27E $1.932) adds back that $1.60B expense. If you instead charge it, the company loses money. This dive uses the non-GAAP series because that is what the market prices on — but names it clearly as a generous measure, and it is a material reason the Downside Risk score is 6 rather than 4.

6a. What today's price assumes (the inversion)

At $307.53 — 159.2x FY27E non-GAAP EPS, 21.3x trailing EV/sales, 91x trailing free cash flow — the price makes these falsifiable claims. Sources labeled.

  • That revenue compounds at 25%+ for three more years. Consensus: $6.098B (FY27E) → $7.669B (FY28E) → $9.629B (FY29E), i.e. +30.2%, +25.7%, +25.6%. Arithmetic: if growth instead settles at 20%, FY29 revenue is ~$8.09B and the FY29E EV/sales multiple the price implies rises from 11.1x to 13.3x — a ~17% valuation headwind from that assumption alone.
  • That non-GAAP EPS nearly doubles by FY28 and reaches $3.80 by FY29. Consensus: $1.932 → $2.726 (+41%) → $3.796 (+39%). Arithmetic: even hitting FY29's $3.796 exactly leaves the stock at 81x — a multiple that itself assumes no de-rating three years out.
  • That the market keeps paying a premium multiple through the whole path. Arithmetic, and this is the honest framing per house rules: the fall from 159x (FY27E) to 81x (FY29E) at a constant price is mechanical rolldown, not re-rating. It happens automatically if earnings grow. It is not evidence the stock gets cheaper in any economic sense; it is evidence the market is paying today for FY29 earnings.
  • That stock-based compensation is genuinely not a cost. Arithmetic: the entire P/E series above is built on non-GAAP EPS that adds back $1.600B (FY26), or 34.1% of revenue. On GAAP the company lost $1.332B. And the add-back is not cost-free to holders — diluted shares rose from 328.0M to 345.4M even after $2.8B of buybacks across FY25-26. This is the assumption most likely to be quietly wrong.
  • That the sell side is 7% too cautious. From the data file: consensus target $285.20 with 42 Buy, 9 Hold, 1 Sell. The stock trades 7.3% ABOVE the average target of a near-unanimously bullish analyst group. The bulls have been outrun.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return ≈ EPS growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield.

Shareholder yield is negative. No dividend, and net share issuance despite $873.5M of FY26 buybacks — the diluted count rose. So holders are being modestly diluted, not returned to.

EPS growth is the entire return, and it is strong: consensus non-GAAP EPS compounds ~40% a year from $1.932 (FY27E) to $3.796 (FY29E). Our base case explicitly assumes multiple COMPRESSION — from 159x FY27E today to roughly 103x FY28E at our $280 base — and even that compressed number is only defensible for a 25%+ compounder with 67% gross margins and improving cash generation. The reason we assume compression rather than a hold is straightforward: the stock has re-rated 118% in three months without a corresponding change in estimates, so the multiple, not the fundamentals, did the work. Mean reversion in the multiple is the base case; mean reversion in the growth rate is the bear case.

If your thesis requires the multiple to HOLD at 159x, say so — that is the fragile leg, and it means you are underwriting a permanent premium to an already-premium peer group. (For comparison from this data file's peer set: Cloudflare at 44.2x TTM EV/sales, Datadog and Cadence in the same complex, Workday at a fraction of the multiple.)

Scenario arithmetic:

  • Bear ~$175 (-43%). Growth decelerates toward 18-20% and the market re-rates the data-infrastructure complex. Market cap $60.7B, EV $60.6B = 6.3x FY29E revenue ($9.629B) or 7.9x FY28E ($7.669B). Cross-check: $175 is 45% above the $121.11 52-week low and roughly 64x FY28E non-GAAP EPS — still not a cheap stock. This is a de-rating, not a disaster.
  • Base ~$280 (-9%). Market cap $97.1B, EV $97.0B = 10.1x FY29E revenue and 12.6x FY28E revenue; cross-checked at ~103x FY28E non-GAAP EPS and ~74x FY29E.   • Bull ~$420 (+37%). The +33.5% acceleration proves a level rather than a peak, international (currently 22.1% of revenue, growing 33-44%) inflects, and the agentic data layer thesis compounds. Market cap $145.6B, EV $145.5B = 15.1x FY29E revenue, or ~111x FY29E non-GAAP EPS. 7. Moat and competitive position

A real but contested moat. Snowflake's defensibility rests on three things: (1) data gravity — once governed enterprise data lives in the platform, moving it is expensive and risky; (2) the sharing network — secure data sharing across organisations creates a genuine network effect that grows with the customer base; (3) cloud neutrality — spanning multiple cloud providers rather than being owned by one, which No Priors (2025-11-06, conviction 80) argues gives it "durable right to be as large as a CSP."

The evidence for the moat is in the numbers: 67.2% gross margin, stable across five years; 95.5% of revenue from platform product; $3.347B of deferred revenue; and growth ACCELERATING at a $4.7B base, which does not happen to a company losing share.

Where the moat is genuinely contested — and this belongs in an honest dive:

  • The unstructured-data flank. Business Breakdowns (2023-11-07) argued the AI wave favours unstructured analytics, a competitor's strength, and that Snowflake "diverges into commoditized structured infrastructure." The recent acceleration is strong evidence against this, but All-In (2026-02-07, conviction 82) still notes a private competitor "reaccelerating growth (>60% at scale)" — faster than Snowflake's 33.5%.
  • The hyperscalers. Every major cloud provider offers a competing data warehouse and can bundle it. Cloud neutrality is a feature until a hyperscaler decides to price against it.
  • The agentic-bespoke risk. All-In (2026-04-24, conviction 80) is the sharpest version: if agents make building bespoke internal tooling cheap, the seat-and-consumption model of every enterprise software vendor compresses. Snowflake is arguably less exposed than seat-based vertical software — it is closer to infrastructure than to an application — but "less exposed" is not "unexposed."

9. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Hold — do not chase.

Snowflake has won the argument that mattered. Revenue growth accelerated to +33.5% in the most recent quarter; free cash flow reached $1.120B; product revenue is 95.5% of the total; deferred revenue is $3.347B; and the company has beaten consensus EPS in each of its last five prints. The Synthos panel's highest-skill voice called this — "AI-native winners thrown out with the bathwater" — and was right. The company is not the question.

The price is. Hold what you own. Do not add above ~$280.
