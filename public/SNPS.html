The Overview

Every advanced computer chip in the world is designed using software, and essentially all of it comes from two companies: Synopsys and Cadence. That is about as good a competitive position as exists in technology.

In May, Synopsys raised its forecasts for the year — for sales, profit margin, profit per share and cash generation, all four at once. Its most recent quarter beat its own guidance.

And the shares are down 35% over the past year, while the stock market is up 24%. They are 36% below their high and trading below both their three-month and their twelve-month average prices. Almost every other company in this research batch is at or near a record.

So why the fall? Last year Synopsys bought a company called Ansys for about $35 billion. To pay for it, its debt went from $684 million to $14.3 billion in twelve months. The accounting value of everything it now owns is largely goodwill — the premium it paid — so if you subtract that, the company's tangible net worth is negative, by about $43 per share. Its profit before tax barely covers its interest bill on the standard accounting measure. And it pays no dividend and buys back no stock, so an owner is paid nothing while waiting.

There is also a political risk the company writes into its own forecast: the guidance holds only if there are no further US export-control changes affecting sales to China.

In May, the activist investor Elliott Management took a seat on the board.

At $403.75 the shares cost about 27 times the profit management itself expects this year and about 23 times what analysts expect next year. Companies like this have usually cost more than 35 times. Analysts' average price target is $557 — 38% above the price — and, unusually, their lowest target of $455 is still 13% above it. Not one of the twenty-nine analysts covering it thinks the stock goes down.

Our estimate of fair value is $468, about 16% above the price, with roughly twice as much upside as downside.

That is the most attractive arithmetic in this batch of twelve. We are still calling it Watch rather than Buy, and we say why: an owner earns nothing while waiting, the balance sheet has no tangible asset behind it, and a $35 billion integration is still in progress. Results come on 26 August and management has scheduled an investor day on 30 September to explain its long-term plan — which is exactly the event that would resolve this.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.
