The Overview

Southern Company is an old-fashioned electric utility. It builds power plants and wires, a state government tells it what it may charge, and that price is set so Southern earns an approved profit on the money it has invested. The more it invests, the more it earns — provided the regulator agrees the investment was needed.

For about twenty years, Americans did not use much more electricity each year. That made this a slow, safe, boring business.

That has changed, and the reason is data centres. Last quarter, electricity sold to commercial customers in Southern's territory — which is where data centres sit — was up 10.9%. Meanwhile households used slightly less and factories used the same. One kind of customer is doing all the growing.

Southern has signed contracts for about 16 gigawatts of this new demand. To put that in perspective, that is roughly a step-change in the size of the company's system, not an increment to it. And crucially, these contracts have teeth: every one has a minimum bill, so the customer pays whether or not it uses the power, and most of them also require the customer to commit for a minimum period, pay a penalty for leaving early, and post cash or a guarantee up front.

So the growth is real and it is protected. Earnings per share, excluding one-off items, rose 22.8% last quarter even though revenue was flat.

Here is the catch, and it is a big one. Building all this costs more than the company earns. In the first six months, Southern spent $6.6 billion on new plant and equipment while generating $4.3 billion of cash from running the business. The gap is filled by borrowing money and issuing new shares. Debt is up $3.0 billion in six months to $75.6 billion. The number of shares is up 3.3% in a year, which means each existing share owns a slightly smaller slice.

And on the very afternoon this report is dated, Southern sold $2.375 billion of a special kind of bond that turns into shares if the stock rises above roughly $105 or $119. That is more borrowing, and more potential future dilution.

The share price has done nothing for a year: down 1.8%, while the stock market is up 24%. It costs $93.25, about 19 times what analysts think it will earn in 2027, and pays a 3.2% dividend. The analysts mostly say Hold — 21 of them, against 10 buys and 3 sells — with an average target of $100.08.

Our estimate is $98. That is about 5% above the price, plus the dividend. Decent. Not compelling.

It describes itself in the July earnings release as "a leading energy provider serving 9 million customers across the Southeast and beyond... electric operating companies in three states, natural gas distribution companies in four states, a competitive generation company, a leading distributed energy solutions provider with national capabilities, a fiber optics network and telecommunications services."

The Large Load Contracts — quoted in full because the terms are the thesis

From the 10-Q, verbatim:

> "Since 2023, the traditional electric operating companies have contracts with new data centers and other large load customers covering approximately 11 gigawatts of electric load, with each contract individually representing a maximum electric load greater than 100 MWs, that have been reviewed by the applicable state PSC. Additional contracts totaling approximately five gigawatts of electric load have been agreed with customers and are subject to review by the applicable state PSC. The approximately 16 gigawatts of new contracts (Large Load Contracts) fully ramp up over several years after commencement of service. Some of the Large Load Contracts are currently in effect and have commenced service. Service under the remaining Large Load Contracts is expected to begin through 2028."

> "All of the Large Load Contracts contain minimum bill provisions. Large Load Contracts totaling approximately 13 gigawatts of electric load (including substantially all signed after 2024) also contain minimum duration, termination payment requirements, and financial security requiring customers to post collateral or provide an acceptable guarantee based on the customer's credit ratings. Certain Large Load Contracts requiring construction of specifically dedicated assets contain provisions for contribution by the customer to construction costs."

Read that carefully, because it answers the standard objection to every data-centre-utility thesis in the market. The objection is that announced load is not contracted load, and that contracted load can be cancelled when an AI capital-expenditure cycle turns. Southern's answer is four specific protections: a minimum bill on every contract; minimum duration on 13 of 16 gigawatts; termination payments; and posted collateral or a credit-rated guarantee. "Including substantially all signed after 2024" is the most informative clause — it says the terms got tighter as the counterparties got more numerous, which is what a supplier with pricing power does.

Two honest caveats. The 5 gigawatts still "subject to review by the applicable state PSC" is not yet approved, and state commission approval is what converts a contract into recoverable rates. And the filing does not name the counterparties, disclose the concentration among them, or quantify the revenue the 16 gigawatts represents. The economic size is therefore not derivable from this archive and no attempt is made to derive it.

The regulatory model, stated plainly

From the 10-Q: "The traditional electric operating companies are engaged in continuous construction programs to accommodate existing and estimated future loads on their respective systems. Major generation construction projects are subject to state PSC approval in order to be included in retail rates, through which the traditional electric operating companies recover their approved investment and a return on investment."

That sentence is the entire earnings mechanism. Southern invests capital, a commission approves it into the rate base, and Southern earns an allowed return on it. Earnings grow with rate base, not with revenue — which is why adjusted EPS rose 22.8% in a quarter revenue rose 0.1%. It is also why free cash flow is deeply negative and why the share count rises every year: the model requires permanent external financing.

Four things follow and each is a judgement, stated as one.

This is a refinancing at less than half the coupon, achieved by selling equity optionality. The 2024A notes pay 4.50%; the 2026A pay 2.125%. Southern is buying a 237-basis-point saving with a call option struck 12.5% above the market. On $725 million, that is roughly $17 million a year of interest saved against 6.93 million shares of contingent dilution. Whether that is a good trade depends entirely on where the stock goes, and it is an explicit management bet that it does not go above $104.56 quickly.

Southern is now a serial convertible issuer, which is unusual for a regulated utility. The releases disclose three separate series: Series 2024A 4.50% due June 2027, Series 2025A 3.25% due June 2028, and now Series 2026A/2026B. A utility reaching the convertible market three years running is telling you something about the cost of straight equity and the size of the funding requirement.

11. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Hold.

What is genuinely good here, and it is more than the price suggests. Southern has converted the single biggest structural change in its industry in forty years into roughly 16 gigawatts of signed Large Load Contracts with terms that are stronger than any comparable disclosure in this batch: minimum-bill provisions on all of them, and minimum duration, termination payments and posted collateral on approximately 13 gigawatts. Weather-adjusted commercial kilowatt-hour sales grew 10.9% in the June quarter. Adjusted earnings per share grew 22.8% on revenue that grew 0.1%. The dividend yields 3.20% at a 65% payout, the beta is 0.332, and the maximum twelve-month drawdown was 6.5%. The knowledge base's independent electricity-demand lane — fifteen channels, none of them naming a company — is as one-directional as any thematic lane this programme has surfaced.

What stops it being a Buy, stated as four specific reservations.

First, the base case is 5.1% and the payoff ratio is 1.42:1, and our base is BELOW the street's. $98 against a consensus of $100.08 and a median target of $101.50, with the street at Hold by 21 to 10 and three sells. There is no edge here and we are not going to pretend there is.

Property additions of $13.9 billion trailing against $10.7 billion of operating cash flow is a $3.3 billion annual external funding requirement, permanently. The average share count rose 3.3% year on year, which consumed 4.3 points of the June quarter's 27.1% net income growth before it reached a per-share number. Third, the financing has reached the convertible market for the third consecutive year, and $2,375 million of it was priced on the day of this dive. Southern's own filings name "customer affordability matters" among their principal uncertainties, and its chief executive's quoted remarks pre-empt the charge.   • A state commission denying recovery on a material portion of the construction programme, or reducing an allowed return on equity. This is the only event that breaks the model rather than delaying it.
  • Weather-adjusted commercial kilowatt-hour growth below 5%, against 10.9% in the June quarter — the load is the thesis and this is the number that reports it.

The filings list "catastrophic events such as fires, including wildfires, land movement, earthquakes, explosions, floods, high winds, tornadoes, hurricanes and other storms" first among their catastrophic risks, and Southern operates across the US Southeast hurricane corridor.
