The Overview

SpaceX went public on 15 June 2026 at $135 a share, raising $85.7 billion — the largest share sale on record. Fifty days later the shares cost $126.06, which is less than the people who bought at the offering paid. Today they rose 10% because the company reported results that were much better than expected.

The company is really three companies.

The one that makes money is Starlink — internet delivered from satellites. It has 12 million subscribers, twice as many as a year ago, and in the last three months it earned $1.66 billion of operating profit on $4.29 billion of sales. That is an excellent business.

The rocket business loses money. It brought in $962 million and lost $542 million, largely because of what it spends developing the very large rocket. Note something odd and important: most of its launches are for itself — putting up its own satellites — so it is less a business selling launches and more an internal factory.

The artificial-intelligence business is where the money goes. It brought in $2.56 billion, lost $1.26 billion, and in six months it spent $23.55 billion building computers and data centres. That is 83 cents of every dollar the company invested.

Add it up: over six months the company generated $3.5 billion of cash from operations and spent $28.5 billion on equipment. It burned $25 billion in half a year. It can afford to — it holds $100 billion of cash and securities against $39 billion of borrowings, and it owes no significant repayment until 2031.

The whole company is valued at about $1.6 trillion. Last quarter's sales, multiplied by four, are about $31 billion. So buyers are paying roughly 51 times a year of sales. For comparison, most large companies trade at two to eight times sales.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $105 — about 17% below the price. The average of the (only four) analysts covering it is $214. In our research base, one very well-known investor says the company will be worth twenty to thirty trillion dollars; another very well-known investor calls it a classic bubble. They said these things four weeks apart.

One thing we were asked to check and could not confirm. There is a widely repeated claim that a large tranche of previously-locked shares becomes tradeable on 6 August — two days from now — taking the freely-traded portion from about 3% to about 12%. We searched the company's filings and found no such statement. We found the opposite for two named executives, whose share-sale plans do not start until 2027. The claim comes from a commentator, not from a document. We are reporting it as a commentator's claim, which is what it is.

  • Downside Risk 9/10. The highest we have assigned. Fifty-one times sales, minus $25 billion of cash flow in six months.
  • Growth Quality 10/10. Revenue up 92% in a quarter. Subscribers doubled. Nothing else in this batch is close.
  • Exponential Potential 9/10. Reusable rockets, satellite broadband, and possibly computers in orbit. This is what the score is for.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $105 against a current price of $130.99 — consistent with our call to stay away or wait for a better setup.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

The honest conclusion, stated as the standing rule requires.
