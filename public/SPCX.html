The Overview

SpaceX went public on 12 June 2026 — 53 days ago — and is now the largest company in this coverage universe by market value, at about $1.5 trillion. It builds and flies rockets, sells satellite internet, does work for the government, and has recently started renting out large amounts of computing power. Last year it took in $18.7 billion of revenue and lost $4.9 billion. More importantly, it spent $20.7 billion building things while its operations only generated $6.8 billion of cash — so it raised $18 billion by selling stock and borrowed another $8.9 billion to cover the difference.

The stock has already fallen about 49% from its high shortly after listing and now trades below the price it went public at. So the obvious question is whether it is cheap. It is not — at $114.53 you are paying roughly eighty times last year's sales. What makes that number tolerable to the bulls is a forecast: Wall Street analysts expect revenue to more than double this year, to about $38.9 billion. The problem is that the most recent quarter the company actually reported grew only 15%. For the analysts to be right, the next three quarters have to average about $11.4 billion each, versus $4.7 billion in the last one. That may happen — the company has been signing very large computing contracts — but it hasn't happened yet, and you are being charged for it today.

The experts we track are more split on this company than on almost anything else. One large fund manager, speaking on a podcast we follow, calls it his biggest position and says it becomes the biggest company in the world. Another well-known investor calls it a must-own. On the other side, a macro publication calls it "a classic bubble stock," and a market commentator we track says it is "a retail sentiment-driven name at 130x price-to-sales" that he "wouldn't chase." When the smartest people on both sides are this far apart, breadth of coverage stops being a signal.

  • Downside Risk 8/10. Losing money, burning $14 billion of cash a year, funded by issuing stock, with a share-unlock schedule ahead.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. Real 33% growth last year, but only 15% in the last quarter, and it costs $20 billion of capex to buy.
  • Exponential Potential 9/10. As close to a real exponential as the public market offers. That is exactly why it is expensive.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $95 against a current price of $114.53 — consistent with our call to stay away or wait for a better setup.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

2. What they actually sell — and what the filings will not tell you

From the company description in the data file: SpaceX "designs, manufactures, and launches rockets and spacecraft, and provides satellite-based broadband services in the United States, Ireland, and Canada," offering launch services to low Earth orbit, the ISS, the Moon and Mars using Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, and Starship; dedicated rideshare for small satellites; reusable vehicles including Dragon and Starship; broadband via Starlink; and Starshield, a satellite-based government offering covering sensing, communications and satellite bus services. 22,000 full-time employees, headquartered in Starbase, Texas. CEO Elon Musk.

And that is where the filings stop. seg_prod and seg_geo are both empty arrays. There is no revenue split between launch, Starlink, Starshield and compute in the data available to this dive. That matters enormously, because the entire bull case for the multiple rests on a specific mix assumption — that a rapidly scaling, high-margin compute and broadband business is inside a $18.7B revenue line — and we cannot verify the mix from the filings. Any dive that gives you a Starlink revenue number is inventing it. This one will not.

What we can observe about mix indirectly:

  • Gross margin is 49.4% (FY25: $9.223B on $18.674B) and 49.1% in Q1'26 ($2.306B on $4.694B). That is a software-inflected margin for a business that also builds and flies rockets — consistent with a large, high-margin services layer inside the total, but it does not size it.
  • Deferred revenue totals $12.116B at FY25 ($6.111B current + $6.005B non-current), up from $10.179B at FY24. A backlog that large and that fast-growing is real contracted work — the single most encouraging line on the balance sheet.
  • R&D is enormous and accelerating: $2.105B (FY23) → $3.464B (FY24) → $8.643B (FY25, 46.3% of revenue) → $3.514B in Q1'26 alone, or 74.9% of that quarter's revenue. Either an extraordinary program ramp or an extraordinary burn, depending on your priors. Both readings are defensible from this data.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return ≈ revenue growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield. Shareholder yield here is negative — there is no dividend, and net share issuance of $18.01B in FY25 against a $1.125B repurchase means holders were diluted, not returned to.

So the return is entirely: revenue growth × multiple drift. And the honest statement is that our base case assumes substantial multiple COMPRESSION and still needs strong growth to avoid a worse outcome. Getting from 80x trailing sales to a defensible long-run multiple requires either the denominator to explode (the consensus ramp) or the numerator to fall (the price). Our base case splits the difference: we assume revenue grows fast but materially slower than consensus — landing FY27 revenue around $40B rather than $74.2B — and that the market pays roughly 31x that number. That produces $1,240B of value, or ~$95 per share.

Cross-check, and this is the useful part: $95 is also ~17.3x the CONSENSUS FY27E revenue of $74.2B. So you arrive at the same base fair value either by (a) trusting consensus revenue and paying a 17x multiple, or (b) halving consensus revenue and paying a 31x multiple. Two very different views of the world converge on the same price, which is the strongest thing we can say for the $95 anchor. What the base case does NOT assume is any multiple expansion. If your thesis needs the market to pay more than ~31x forward sales, that is your fragile leg, and you should say so out loud.

Scenario arithmetic, stated explicitly:

  • Bear ~$50 (-56%). FY27 revenue lands at $30B (a ~26%/yr compound from the Q1'26 annualized run-rate of $18.8B) and the market pays 21.5x → $645B → $49.4. Cross-check: $50 is 8.7x consensus FY27E revenue. This is the "unlock plus growth disappointment" case, and it is roughly consistent with Compound & Friends' "average IPO down 50%" framing.
  • Base ~$95 (-17%). FY27 revenue $40B at 31x → $1,240B → $94.8. Equivalently, consensus FY27E $74.2B at 17.3x.
  • Bull ~$185 (+62%). Consensus FY27E $74.2B at 32.5x → $2,412B → $184.4. Cross-check: consensus FY28E $141.59B at 17.0x → $2,407B → the same number. This requires the compute and broadband ramp to land in full and the market to keep paying a premium multiple through it.

Positive surprises that would force a repricing upward (each maps to a watchable number):

7. Moat and competitive position

The moat is real, wide, and physically expensive to replicate — and it is also the least contested part of this dive. From the panel: Invest Like the Best (2026-04-14, conviction 95) describes the company as "destined to own the entire space launch industry"; All-In (2025-06-04, conviction 80) notes it is "the sole provider of human orbital spaceflight," while adding the honest caveat that "lack of competition lets it charge more — a moat, but one competition should erode."

The mechanism of the moat is cost-per-kilogram and cadence, and Gavin Baker's 2026-07-17 claim quantifies where that goes: 100+ metric tons at under $2M variable cost versus 17 tons at ~$15M — "roughly a 50x change." A competitor does not catch a 50x cost advantage with capital; it catches it with a decade.

But two moat caveats belong in an honest dive:

1. The moat is not priced at a moat multiple; it is priced at a growth multiple. 80x trailing sales is not what the market pays for a durable monopoly generating cash. It is what the market pays for an expected explosion in revenue. The moat protects the business. It does not protect the multiple.

2. The panel names credible competition. Real Vision (2026-06-28) explicitly cites Bezos and Rocket Lab against both Starlink and launch. All-In (2025-09-24, conviction 70) notes that "rockets are an immature art with room for competition." A moat that our own bulls say "competition should erode" is a moat with a clock on it.

10. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch. Do not initiate.

SpaceX is an extraordinary company priced as an extraordinary certainty. At $114.53 you pay roughly 80x FY25 sales for a business that lost $4.94B on a GAAP basis, burned $13.95B of free cash flow, and funded that burn with $18.01B of stock issuance — and whose most recently reported quarter grew revenue 15.4% against a full-year consensus requiring +108%. Our base fair value of $95 is 17% below spot, derived two independent ways that converge (31x our $40B FY27 revenue estimate; 17.3x the $74.2B consensus). Meanwhile the panel — the deepest coverage lane in the entire Synthos base at 218 name-level claims — is split at both tails, with the bears holding the more recent, more dated, more falsifiable claims and the bulls holding a 10-15 year terminal-value story.

The stock has already done a lot of work for the bear case. -49% from the high, below the ~$135 IPO price, RSI 29.7, at its listing-life low. That is why this is Watch and not Avoid: the gap to fair value is 17%, not 60%, and one strong print closes it.

What would make this a Buy (pre-registered flip conditions):

  • A quarterly revenue print at or above ~$11B, confirming the consensus ramp is real — at which point 38x FY26E sales is defensible and the base case moves materially higher.
  • Price into the $50-75 zone on unlock supply with the growth story intact. That is the bear-case band, and it is where the asymmetry genuinely turns. A great business at 25-35x forward sales after a supply flush is a different proposition to the same business at 80x trailing sales before one.
  • A segment disclosure that sizes the compute and broadband lines and validates the mix the multiple assumes.
  • Free cash flow inflecting — any quarter where operating cash flow growth outpaces capex growth.

Pre-registered KILL / stay-away criteria (i.e., what would move this from Watch to Avoid):

  • A second consecutive quarter of sub-20% revenue growth, making the FY26 consensus arithmetically unreachable.
  • Further large equity issuance beyond the FY25 $18.01B, confirming dilution as the funding model.
  • A break of $104.83 with no moving-average support beneath it and unlock supply still to come.
  • Capex accelerating again without a corresponding step-up in operating cash flow — the FY25 ratio of 3.06x is already the outer limit of what a public market will fund.
