The Overview

This company does three separate things, and they have almost nothing to do with each other.

The first is rockets. It builds and launches them, and unusually it lands and reuses them, which is why it can launch far more cheaply than anyone else. This is the original business and the famous one. It is currently losing money — about $542 million last quarter — because almost all the spending is on developing a much larger next-generation vehicle that has not yet flown a paying customer.

The second is satellite internet. Thousands of small satellites in low orbit beam broadband to homes, ships, planes and armies in 167 countries. This is the business that actually works. It made $4.3 billion of revenue last quarter and kept $1.66 billion of that as operating profit — a 38.6% margin — while growing 66%. The number of subscribers doubled. But the average subscriber now pays 22% less than a year ago, because the company is expanding into poorer countries and selling cheaper plans. It is buying growth with price.

The third is artificial intelligence, and this is where the surprise is. In February the company merged with a Musk-owned AI business, which had earlier merged with the social network formerly called Twitter. Accounting rules for deals between companies with the same owner require the past financial statements to be redrawn as if they had always been one company — which is why this "rocket company" now reports $11.6 billion of goodwill and nearly $2 billion of annual interest costs. This AI division loses $1.26 billion a quarter. It is also the fastest-growing part, because it started renting out computing capacity, which added $1.6 billion of revenue in a single quarter. That $1.6 billion is essentially the whole reason the stock jumped 10% today — and the company's own new risk warning says those rental contracts can be cancelled by either side with 90 days' notice, and that only a handful of customers account for them.

Now the price. The company is worth $1.65 trillion on the stock market. It sold $23 billion of goods and services over the last twelve months and lost $8.2 billion. So you pay about 69 times sales. To put that another way: if you want to earn 10% a year for ten years from here, this company's revenue has to grow about 44% every single year for a decade.

There is one more thing you need to know, and it is the most important. The company only listed on 15 June. Almost all of its shares are still locked up — only about 3% of them can be traded. In two days, a large block is expected to become sellable, taking the tradeable portion to about 12%, and by December to about 40%. Experts we track have said this plainly for weeks, including one who expects the price to fall below $100.

So: a remarkable company, a balance sheet with $61.6 billion more cash than debt, one genuinely excellent profitable division, and a share price that already assumes a decade of near-perfection — attached to a flood of supply arriving this week.

  • Downside Risk 9/10. 69x sales, loss-making, no annual report, float quadruples in two days, 15.9% of shares already promised to someone else.
  • Growth Quality 9/10. Revenue up 91.9%; gross margin up 1,133bp; but subscriber pricing is falling 22% and the fastest-growing revenue is cancellable at 90 days.
  • Exponential Potential 9/10. The real thing — reusable launch, a global constellation, spectrum, compute. Almost none of the biggest pieces are proven yet.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $88 against a current price of $126.06 — consistent with our call to stay away or wait for a better setup.

1. What this company actually is — three businesses, one of which earns money

Read the structure before the numbers. Per Note 1 of the 10-Q, the company operates three reportable segments:

  • Space — designs, manufactures and launches reusable rockets. Revenue comes from launch services for third-party payloads (recognised at a point in time, on launch) and from longer-dated launch-and-development contracts for government space programmes (recognised over time, cost-to-cost). Critically, launches of the company's own broadband satellites generate NO inter-segment revenue; those launch costs are capitalised into property, plant and equipment. The segment's reported revenue therefore understates its economic output by design.
  • Connectivity — the broadband constellation: more than 10,200 satellites, serving consumer, enterprise and government customers across 167 countries, territories and other markets, plus a government-focused sensing and communications offering. Revenue is broadband and mobile service plus hardware kits.
  • AI — a vertically integrated artificial-intelligence platform: a frontier language model, consumer and enterprise AI products, the real-time social platform, and AI computational infrastructure rented to third parties.

The corporate history that explains the accounts. On 2026-02-02 the company completed its acquisition of a Musk-controlled artificial-intelligence holding company, which had itself acquired the social platform (formerly Twitter, Inc., taken private by Mr. Musk in October 2022) on 2025-03-28. Both were common-control transactions, and the filing states the historical financial statements have been retrospectively combined. This single fact explains most of what looks anomalous in the data: the $11.6B of goodwill (of which $11,130M is in the AI segment), the $1,945M of FY2025 interest expense, the restructuring charges relating to a 2022 workforce reduction at the social platform, and the FY2023 net loss of $4,628M at a company most observers assumed was self-funding.

Three readings, in order of importance.

First, Connectivity is the entire economic engine and everything else is a cost centre. It produced $2,844M of operating income in the half while the consolidated result was a $2,086M loss. Every dollar of value in this business today, and every dollar of value at any sane multiple, comes from this one segment.

Second, the growth is not where the story says it is. Space — the business the ticker is named after and the reason the knowledge base is bullish — grew 29% in the quarter but shrank 1.9% over the half, on 17 customer launches against 21 a year earlier. The company's own explanation is timing and mix. The engine of reported growth is the AI segment, at +247.5%.

Third, the AI growth is one line item. Of the AI segment's $1,824M quarterly increase, $1,600M was AI-infrastructure revenue from cloud services the company began offering during 2026 — a business that did not exist a year ago. Advertising revenue on the social platform actually fell $59M in the quarter and $160M over the half. Subscription revenue for the model and the platform added $258M. So the headline beat is one new, concentrated, cancellable revenue line. That is Section 2.

Customer concentration. The filing discloses two customers each above 10% of consolidated revenue. Customer A spans all three segments — on the evidence of the business description, a government counterparty. Customer B is in the AI segment. No other customer exceeds 10%.

2. The single most important analytical issue: what today's 10.07% actually bought

The beat was real, it was almost entirely one line, and that line is the least durable revenue in the company.

The vendor's earnings calendar records reported EPS of −$0.09 against an estimate of −$0.22547, on revenue of $7,814M against $6,826.7M expected — a $987M revenue beat, or 14.5%. The stock rose 10.07%.

Reconstructing where that came from, from the filing:

  • $1,600M of the quarterly revenue increase was AI-infrastructure cloud-services revenue, a line the company began recognising in 2026 under a new accounting policy (Note 2: "a stand-ready promise to provide continuous access to reserved compute capacity, with the transaction price consisting of fixed monthly fees").
  • $1,703M came from Connectivity, on subscriber growth of 101.2% partly offset by an average-revenue-per-user decline of 22.4%.
  • $216M came from Space, on one additional customer launch.
  • Advertising revenue declined $59M.

Now read the only new risk factor in the entire filing. Item 1A states: "The risk factor set forth below supplements the risk factors disclosed under the section titled 'Risk Factors' in our Prospectus. Except as set forth below, there have been no material changes." In a first-year filer, the risk factor management chooses to ADD is the loudest signal available. It reads, in relevant part:

> "We generate an increasing portion of our consolidated revenue from our AI segment. This growth is primarily driven by our AI Infrastructure business... a significant portion of our AI infrastructure revenue is concentrated in a small number of customers. Our cloud services agreements generally provide for monthly fees and, after an initial period inclusive of capacity ramp (generally, a number of months), may be terminated by either party upon 90 days' notice. Accordingly, the loss of a significant customer, the termination or non-renewal of one or more of these agreements... could materially adversely affect our AI segment and consolidated revenue, results of operations, and cash flows and future growth."

Restated plainly: the revenue that beat, that the market capitalised at roughly $150B of added market value today, is monthly-fee revenue from a handful of customers who can walk with a quarter's notice — and management has just told you so in the one risk factor it added. This is the single most important thing in the filing and it is not in the vendor payload anywhere.
