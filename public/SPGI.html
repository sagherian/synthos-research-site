The Overview

S&P Global does three main things. It assigns credit ratings to bonds — the letter grades that decide what interest a borrower pays. It owns and licenses the S&P 500 and Dow Jones stock indices, collecting a fee on the trillions of dollars invested in funds that track them. And it sells financial and energy market data by subscription.

The first two are among the best businesses that exist. Essentially every large bond issue must be rated, and there are only two firms that matter. Every index fund tracking the S&P 500 pays a fee to S&P Global for the right to use the name and the data. Both are close to unavoidable, and both are extremely profitable: for every dollar of ratings revenue the company keeps 64 cents as operating profit, and for every dollar of index revenue, 69 cents.

On 1 July this year — five weeks ago — the company gave away its fourth business. It separated S&P Global Mobility, which sells data about cars, and handed shareholders one share of the new company for each S&P Global share they held. This was a good decision: Mobility was the least profitable of the five divisions, keeping only 22 cents of each dollar.

But it makes the numbers temporarily impossible to read, and this dive found five separate places where the financial data we buy contradicts itself as a result. The quarterly revenue figure is reported in one place as $4,146 million and in another as $3,678 million — the difference being Mobility. Analyst forecasts for this year's revenue are lower than last year's actual revenue, purely because they cover a smaller company. One entire division — the energy data business, worth $2.3 billion of revenue — has vanished from the segment table we were sold. And the average analyst price target of $541 may have been calculated when the company still owned Mobility, in which case comparing it to today's $412.53 price is comparing two different things.

Underneath all of that, the business is doing well. Revenue in the last quarter grew 10.4%, with every division up. The index business grew fastest, at nearly 20%, because more money keeps flowing into index funds. The company spends almost nothing on equipment — about 1.3% of revenue — and uses the cash to buy back roughly 3% of its own shares every year.

The shares cost $412.53, down 27% from their high, and by one common technical measure they are the most oversold in this batch of twelve companies. Every one of the 28 analysts who follows the company thinks they are worth more; the most cautious says $498. Our own estimate is $470, about 14% up.

We are not buying today, and the reason is specific: on 29 October the company will republish all its historical figures on a clean basis, and that is the first date on which anyone can actually measure it.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. A very safe franchise on a balance sheet that is 83% goodwill and intangibles, currently unmeasurable.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Genuinely good — 10.4% in the last quarter with everything growing — but the forward series is unusable.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. One structurally compounding asset (the index business); the rest are tolls.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $470 against a current price of $429.53 — real upside if our numbers are right.

It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. 1. The separation, and the five basis defects it created

On 1 July 2026, S&P Global completed the separation of Mobility Global Inc. The 8-K filed 2026-07-02 states it plainly:

> "...at 12:01 a.m. New York City time, the previously-announced separation (the 'Separation') of Mobility Global Inc. ('Mobility Global') from S&P Global Inc. ('S&P Global') became effective. The separation of Mobility Global, which comprises ... vehicle history data for the automotive sector, which operated under the S&P Global Mobility division (the 'Spin Business'), was achieved through S&P Global's distribution ... of 100% of the shares of Mobility Global common [stock] ... S&P Global stockholders of record received one share of Mobility Global common stock for every share of S&P Global common stock. Following the Distribution, Mobility Global became an independent, publicly-traded company with its common stock listed under the symbol 'MBGL' on the New York Stock Exchange, and S&P Global retains no ownership interest in Mobility Global."

And the 10-Q filed 2026-07-28 states the accounting consequence:

> "Effective July 1, 2026, our operations consist of four reportable segments: S&P Global Ratings ('Ratings'), S&P Dow Jones Indices ('Indices'), S&P Global Energy ('Energy') and S&P Global Market Intelligence ('Market Intelligence'). The results of Mobility are included through June 30, 2026. Beginning with the third quarter of 2026, the historical financial results of Mobility through June 30, 2026 will be reflected in our consolidated financial statements as discontinued operations in accordance with U.S. GAAP for all periods."

> "Additionally, beginning with the third quarter of 2026, results will reflect product transfers of 451 Research and Maritime & Trade from Market Intelligence to Energy ... and a small portion of expenses associated with the transfer of Credit Analytics products from Market Intelligence to Ratings."

So as of this dive, S&P Global has: separated a segment one day after the quarter close, not yet restated its history, renamed another segment, and announced product transfers between two more. Every one of the five defects below follows from that.

4b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Our base assumes modest multiple EXPANSION, and it is the fragile leg — stated explicitly. The argument for it is that the post-separation company is structurally better (a 45.8% segment margin against 43.0%, having shed the 21.6%-margin division), that the current 20.2x is below where a ratings-and-indices duopolist has historically traded, and that a restated, clean reporting basis on 2026-10-29 removes a discount that exists partly because the numbers are currently confusing. The argument against it is that the market may be pricing a genuine structural threat to the data-services half of the company rather than a temporary reporting fog, in which case the multiple stays where it is or falls.

Notably, an unusually large share of the expected return here is shareholder yield: roughly 5.0%, of which 4.1% is buyback.
