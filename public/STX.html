The Overview

Every photograph, video, backup and log file that a large company keeps has to sit on something physical. There are two choices. Flash memory — the same technology as the storage in your phone — is fast and expensive. Hard disk drives are spinning metal platters with a magnetic head reading them, which is slow but much cheaper per unit of space. For data you have to keep but hardly ever look at, the cheap slow option wins, and that is almost all of Seagate's business.

There are only three companies left in the world that make these drives, and none of them has built a new factory in years. Meanwhile the amount of data the world wants to keep has jumped, partly because of artificial intelligence. When demand rises and nobody can make more, the price goes up.

That is exactly what has happened, and the numbers are dramatic. Two years ago Seagate kept about 23 cents of every sales dollar after manufacturing costs. This past year it kept about 46 cents, and in the final three months it kept 52 cents. Sales went from $9.1 billion to $12.2 billion in a year. Profit went from $1.5 billion to $3.2 billion. The company used the cash to pay down $1.4 billion of debt, and for the first time in years the company is worth more than it owes on paper.

So why not just buy it? Because of what happened three years ago. In the year to June 2023 this same company's sales fell 37%, and it lost money — it spent more making drives than it earned selling them. That is not ancient history and nothing about the business has changed to make it impossible again. If you take a step back and look at five years rather than one, sales grew about 2.7% a year. The line is flat; it just wobbles violently.

The shares cost $845. Last year the company earned about $15.53 a share on the adjusted measure analysts use, so you are paying about 54 times what it actually made. Analysts expect about $35 next year and about $57 the year after, which would make it 24 times and then 15 times — numbers that sound cheap. But to earn $35 a share, Seagate would have to sell $18.3 billion of drives, which is 57% more than it has ever sold in any year in its history, and keep 44 cents of every dollar as profit, when the best three months it has ever had kept 36 cents.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $885. They cost $845. That is a gap of under 5%, and the average analyst thinks $1,130, which is a lot higher than we do. When our own number is barely above the price and far below the crowd's, the answer is to wait.

One more thing about today. The whole group of memory and storage shares jumped: one rose 12.8%, another 10.8%, another 7.6%. Seagate opened 5.6% higher and then drifted down all day to close only 1.7% up — four and a half percent below where it started trading. On the one day everything like it went up, this one was being sold.

  • Downside Risk 8/10. Much better finances than a year ago, attached to a business that lost money three years ago and a share price that has risen 446% in twelve months.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Enormous acceleration, about half of it from raising prices, on top of five years of essentially flat sales.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. A genuine cost-per-terabyte advantage, on a technology whose main competitors make the thing that could replace it.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $885 against a current price of $845.35 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

Seagate describes itself in the 10-K as a supplier of mass capacity and legacy data storage solutions. The corporate structure matters more than usual here, so it is worth stating precisely from the filing: Seagate Technology Holdings plc is "a public limited company organized under the laws of Ireland", with principal executive offices at 121 Woodlands Avenue 5, Singapore — the 10-K notes that "The Company established Singapore as its principal executive offices in fiscal year 2024." The vendor profile field records the country as SG. The debt is issued out of two subsidiaries, Seagate HDD Cayman and Seagate Data Storage Technology Pte. Ltd., with cross-guarantees. Chief executive William David Mosley; chief financial officer Gianluca Romano; approximately 30,000 employees (vendor profile field). Founded 1978; listed on NASDAQ; ISIN IE00BKVD2N49, CUSIP G7997R103 — both Irish-issuer identifiers.

One reportable segment, and the vendor gives you nothing

This is the most important disclosure-structure fact in the file, and the vendor payload handles it badly.

The 10-K, Note 15, states: "The Company has determined that its Chief Operating Decision Maker ('CODM'), the Chief Executive Officer, evaluates performance of the Company and makes decisions regarding investments in the Company's technology platforms and manufacturing infrastructure based on the Company's consolidated results, including net income reported on the Consolidated Statements of Operations. As a result, the Company has concluded that its manufacture and distribution of storage solutions constitutes one operating segment."

The vendor's seg_prod block is EMPTY — an empty array. There is no product revenue disaggregation in the payload at all. That is not a defect so much as a consequence: with one reportable segment, the vendor has nothing to populate. But it means the entire mass-capacity versus legacy split — the single most important mix question about this company — is unavailable from the vendor and must come from the filings. The 10-K describes the markets, not the dollars:

  • Mass Capacity Storage — "supports scalable, high capacity, cost efficient storage applications, including nearline cloud, nearline enterprise, video and image applications ('VIA') and network-attached storage ('NAS') and edge-to-cloud data storage infrastructures." This is enterprise nearline HDDs, enterprise SSDs, VIA and NAS drives, systems, and the Lyve edge-to-cloud platform.
  • Legacy Markets — "include consumer, client and mission critical applications. We continue to sell to these markets but do not plan significant additional investment." Seagate is explicitly harvesting this.

The dollar split between them is not in this archive. The 10-Q says the company changed its end-market classification during FY2026 and "reflected these changes to our revenue and HDD exabytes shipped by end market retrospectively to the earliest period presented", adding that "The change had no impact on our previously reported consolidated net revenue." The table itself carries the numbers and the numbers are stripped from the extracted text. We state this as a gap rather than estimating around it.

Customer concentration — lower than the industry reputation, and disclosed annually only

The 10-K states: "In fiscal year 2025, one customer accounted for approximately 10% of consolidated revenue. In fiscal year 2024, no customers accounted for more than 10% of consolidated revenue. In fiscal year 2023, one customer accounted for approximately 10% of consolidated revenue." Separately, under Concentrations: "The Company's customer base is concentrated with a small number of customers... One customer accounted for more than 10% of the Company's accounts receivable as of June 27, 2025 and June 28, 2024."

That is materially less concentrated than the semiconductor capital-equipment names in this batch, where single customers run to 19% of revenue. It is still concentrated in a small number of hyperscale buyers — the risk factors name "large hyperscale data center companies and CSPs" explicitly — but the disclosed 10% threshold is a fact and we report it as one. The caveat is timing: this disclosure is annual, the FY2026 10-K is not in this archive, and FY2026 revenue grew 34% on hyperscale nearline demand. The concentration figure we are quoting is thirteen months old and the direction of travel is almost certainly upward.

Competitors, and what the list tells you

The 10-K's principal-competitor list, verbatim in order: Kioxia Holdings Corporation; Micron Technology, Inc.; Samsung Electronics; Sandisk Corporation; SK hynix, Inc.; Toshiba Corporation; and Western Digital Corporation.

Only two of those seven — Toshiba and Western Digital — make hard disk drives. The other five make flash and memory. Seagate's own annual report is telling you that its principal competitive threat is substitution rather than rivalry. Hold that thought; it recurs in Sections 6c and 11, and it is the single best reason the exponential score is a 5.

First, the amplitude is extraordinary in both directions. Revenue fell 43.8% peak to trough between FY2022 and FY2024. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+61.0%, from FY2027E $35.393 to FY2028E $56.990) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 23.9x to roughly 15.5x on the respective forward year, −35%) + shareholder yield (+0.44%) ≈ +5% to +15%, with an enormous variance around it.

Our base case assumes very substantial multiple compression, and it is important to be clear that this is NOT the rolldown artefact the addendum warns about. Today the market pays 23.9x the next fiscal year's estimate. Our base assumes that in twelve months it pays roughly 15.5x the then-next fiscal year's estimate. That is a genuine de-rating of 35%, and we assume it for a specific, defensible reason: this is what cyclicals do at the top of a cycle. A commodity manufacturer earning peak margins trades at a low multiple of those peak earnings precisely because the market does not believe they persist. Seagate at 24x forward is currently priced like a secular grower. Our base prices it like the cyclical the knowledge base's one claim insists it is.

Nearly all of the expected return in our base is earnings growth, and the multiple is fighting it hard. If the multiple simply HELD at 23.9x on FY2028E, the price would be $1,362 (+61.1%) — which is roughly where our bull case lands, and roughly where the street's $1,130 sits between. If it compressed to 12x, the price would be $684 (−19.1%). The entire spread between a good and a bad outcome sits in a multiple we do not control and have no edge on, applied to an estimate whose implied margin has no historical precedent.
