The Overview

Sunbelt rents out construction and industrial equipment — diggers, lifts, generators, pumps, power tools, temporary climate control — from a large network of branches in North America and Britain. It sells about $11 billion a year and employs 25,000 people. It was separated from its former parent and listed on the stock market in March 2026, five months ago.

At first glance the shares look cheap. The data suggests the company throws off $3.4 billion of surplus cash a year against a $30.8 billion market value — an 11% cash yield, which would be remarkable.

It does not. The figure is wrong, and the error is easy to demonstrate. A rental company's business is its fleet of machines, and those machines wear out — the accounts charge about $2.3 billion a year of depreciation against them. To keep renting, you have to keep buying replacements. But the data records only $352 million of equipment purchases for the year. Buying $352 million of machines while writing off $2.3 billion is not a business, it is a liquidation. The fleet purchases are simply recorded somewhere the "capital expenditure" line does not capture. Correct for it and the real surplus cash is roughly $1.5 billion — a yield of about 4.8%, respectable but ordinary.

With that removed, the picture is of a business past its peak: sales grew 21% three years ago, then 12%, then fell 1%, then grew 3%. Profits have fallen three years running, from $1.62 billion to $1.33 billion. The profit margin on sales has dropped from 24% to 20%.

There are genuine positives — the debt is manageable, the company bought back nearly 5% of its shares last year, and its interest bill fell 27%. But only four analysts follow it, the data contains no breakdown of sales by business unit or by country (even though it has a British division), there is no list of comparable companies, and the shares have traded for five months — too short for the usual trend measures. Our estimate of fair value is $76. The price is $75.07.

  • Downside Risk 7/10. A misreported cash line, three years of falling profits, and almost nothing to check the numbers against.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. Data integrity — the free-cash-flow artifact governs this dive

This file contains the single most consequential vendor error in this batch, because it materially overstates the metric most investors would use to judge the stock. Set out in full:

REJECTED — every free-cash-flow metric in the file

cf_a reports for fiscal 2026 (year ended 2026-04-30):

  • netCashProvidedByOperatingActivities: $3,784M
  • capitalExpenditure: −$352M
  • freeCashFlow: $3,432M

And km_ttm / ratios_ttm derive from it:

  • freeCashFlowYieldTTM: 11.15%
  • priceToFreeCashFlowRatioTTM: 8.97x
  • evToFreeCashFlowTTM: 11.92x
  • capexToRevenueTTM: 3.16%
  • capexToDepreciationTTM: 0.1524

The last figure is the proof. A capex-to-depreciation ratio of 0.15 means the company spent fifteen cents of capital for every dollar of depreciation charged. For an equipment-rental company that is impossible while the business is growing — the depreciating asset is the revenue-producing asset, and a fleet not replaced is a fleet shrinking toward zero.

Independent confirmation from the income statement: FY26 EBITDA of $4,497M less EBIT of $2,181M implies depreciation and amortisation of roughly $2,316M — 20.8% of revenue, exactly what one expects from a rental fleet. Against that, $352M of recorded capital expenditure is 15.2%. The rental-fleet purchases are not in the capitalExpenditure field.

Our corrected estimate, labelled as our computation: in a low-growth year, replacement fleet capital expenditure approximates depreciation. Taking operating cash flow of $3,784M less roughly $2,300M of true fleet-plus-property capital expenditure gives free cash flow of approximately $1,480M, a free-cash-flow yield of roughly 4.8% on the $30.77B market capitalisation and enterprise value to free cash flow of roughly 28x. We use these corrected figures throughout and we discard the vendor's entirely.

This single correction is the difference between a stock that looks obviously cheap and one that looks fairly valued, and it is the reason this dive exists in the form it does.

2. The business — and what we are not told about it

Per the profile: an equipment-leasing firm supplying tools, machinery and engineered solutions to construction, industrial and specialty customers, organised into three core units — North American general tool services, North American specialty services, and a United Kingdom division. 25,428 employees. Chief executive Brendan Christopher Horgan. Headquartered in Fort Mill, South Carolina. The entity was established on 2025-02-12 and listed on 2026-03-02, so the pre-listing financials are carve-out accounts.

We are told the three units exist and we are told nothing about their size. seg_prod and seg_geo are both empty (§1). In equipment rental, the specialty business (climate control, power, pumps, trench safety, remediation) typically carries materially higher margins and lower cyclicality than general tool, and a UK business carries separate currency, cycle and cost dynamics. The mix between them is the single most important structural fact about this company, and it is absent.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over an eighteen-to-twenty-four-month horizon: EBITDA growth (+17% cumulative to FY28 on consensus) + deleveraging transfer + multiple drift + shareholder yield (1.00% dividend plus ~4.7% buyback).

Our base case assumes multiple COMPRESSION: we apply 8.0x FY27E EBITDA against the 7.8x the market pays for FY27E today — nominally near-flat, but the important point is that we do not assume any expansion, and we haircut nothing from the consensus EBITDA figure while noting in §6a that the 410 basis-point margin recovery it embeds is unsupported. The de-rating in our base case is therefore implicit: if consensus EBITDA proves too high, 8.0x on a lower number is what produces the bear case.

The deleveraging transfer is a genuine and quantifiable leg here. At an enterprise value of $40.91B with $10.13B of net debt, equity is 75.2% of the capital structure. Corrected free cash flow of ~$1,480M, if applied to debt, transfers roughly $1,480M — about $3.54 per share, or 4.7% — to equity annually at a constant multiple. But the company is spending $1,432M of it on buybacks instead, which is an equivalent-value transfer through a different route (shares down 4.4% in FY26). Either way it is real, and it is the single largest component of the expected return.

So the base-case return of roughly +1% decomposes as: ~8% of consensus EPS growth, plus ~5.7% of combined dividend and buyback yield, offset by a ~12% implicit de-rate arising from our scepticism about the embedded margin recovery and by a starting price that already reflects the consensus case. State it plainly: on the consensus numbers this stock is worth more than $75; our base case is at $75 because we do not believe the FY27 margin assumption.

The bull case at $95 requires 9.5x FY27E EBITDA and the consensus margin recovery to land — two things at once. That is the fragile leg.

9. Moat and competitive position

Equipment rental at national scale has a genuine, well-understood structural advantage: branch density, fleet breadth and utilisation. A contractor needs a specific machine in a specific place tomorrow; the operator with the densest network and the widest fleet wins the booking and, critically, achieves higher utilisation on the same asset. Scale also confers purchasing power on the fleet itself. Sunbelt, at $11.2B of revenue and 25,428 employees, is one of a small number of participants with that density.

The evidence the advantage exists: a 40.3% EBITDA margin (FY26) is not achievable in a fragmented, price-taking business.

The evidence it is under pressure: operating margin fell 650 basis points from the FY23 peak and fell again in the most recent quarter while revenue accelerated 8.9%. A business with intact pricing power does not lose 27% of its quarterly earnings in a quarter when volume grows 9%. The most likely candidates are rental-rate compression, fleet-cost inflation, or a mix shift toward lower-margin general tool — and the empty segment disclosure (§1) prevents distinguishing them.

Moat: real in structure, currently not visible in the trend. That combination is precisely when a low multiple is a value trap rather than a bargain, and it is why the corrected free-cash-flow figure matters so much: at 11.2% the stock would be cheap enough to accept the uncertainty; at 4.8% it is not.
