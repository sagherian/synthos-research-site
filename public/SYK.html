The Overview

Stryker makes the things used in surgery. Artificial knees and hips. The power tools surgeons use to cut and drill bone. Hospital beds and stretchers. Devices for treating strokes. And Mako, a robotic arm that helps surgeons position an implant precisely.

It is a good business for a simple reason: surgeons learn on a particular system and hospitals build their operating theatres around it, so nobody switches to save money. Demand also does not depend on the economy — people need new knees at the same rate in a recession.

The company has grown steadily for years. Sales rose 75% over five years, and it keeps about 65 cents of every sales dollar as gross profit.

Then, earlier this year, Stryker was hit by a cyber incident. The details are not in the filings we hold, but the effect is visible: in the January-to-March quarter, sales grew only 2.6% instead of the usual 9%, and profit margins fell sharply. Analysts' forecasts were missed by 13%.

Five days before this was written, the company reported the next quarter, and the recovery is clear. Sales grew 9.4%. The underlying growth rate — stripping out acquisitions and currency — was 9.0%. Profit margins were back above 27%. Earnings per share rose 17.9%. And the company narrowed its forecast for the year rather than cutting it.

The share price has not caught up. The shares are down 11% over the last twelve months while the overall market rose 24%, and they are 15% below their high. They cost $335.96, against expected earnings of about $16.77 per share in 2027 — about 20 times, which is cheaper than Stryker has usually been.

Two things to keep in mind. First, the company has bought a lot of businesses, most recently Inari Medical for $4.8 billion. That means it carries $12.3 billion of net debt and, once you subtract goodwill, its accounting net assets are slightly negative. That is common for acquisitive companies and is not a crisis while cash flow is strong.

Second, and more important for the long run: the growth is entirely in units sold, not prices. In the last quarter, orthopaedic sales grew 8.6% and none of it came from raising prices. A company that cannot raise prices depends on making things more efficiently to widen its margins — and the March quarter showed how quickly that can go the other way.

Our estimate of fair value is $369, about 10% above the price. The average analyst says $383, and 38 of 51 analysts rate it a buy with none rating it a sell.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Recession-proof demand and 8.3x interest cover, against $12.3 billion of net debt, negative tangible net assets and a newly demonstrated cyber exposure.
  • Growth Quality 7/10. Nine percent organic growth with margin expansion — interrupted once this year, and now resumed.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. Mako robotics is a real compounding platform. Orthopaedics growing 8.6% on volume alone is not an exponential.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $369 against a current price of $337.68 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Driver
"Long-run this is one of the more defensible franchises in this batch and the reason is clinical rather than financial. An orthopaedic surgeon trained on Stryker instruments, operating in a theatre configured around Stryker equipment, with a hospital whose implant inventory is Stryker consignment, does not switch platform on price. Two observations from that table.

First, MedSurg and Neurotechnology is doing the work. It grew 32.2% over two years against Orthopaedics' 9.3% — and it is the larger segment at 62.3%. Stryker is, on the numbers, more a surgical-equipment and neurotechnology company than an orthopaedics company, which is not how it is usually described.

Second, the FY2023 label reads "Orthopaedics and Spine" while FY2024 and FY2025 read "Orthopaedics". Stryker separated its Spine business, so the FY2023 figure is not like-for-like with the years after it, and the 9.3% two-year growth rate above understates the underlying rate by whatever Spine contributed. We flag it rather than computing around it.

Stryker publishes the full bridge — currency and acquisitions separated from organic — every quarter, which almost no other company in this batch does. The composition matters and it is disclosed too: MedSurg's 9.2% organic was "9.1% from increased unit volume and 0.1% from higher prices", and Orthopaedics' 8.6% was "from increased unit volume" with no price at all. This company grows by selling more, not by charging more, and that is the single most important structural fact about it.

Geography — and the vendor block is missing three-quarters of the company. The FY2025 seg_geo entry reads only Asia Pacific $2,164M, EMEA $3,181M and Other foreign countries $765M, summing to $6,110M against reported revenue of $25,116M. The United States line — $16,943M in FY2024, roughly 75% of revenue — has been dropped. The FY2024 and FY2023 entries are complete. No geographic figure in this dive uses the FY2025 block.

The organisational change, from the 8-K filed 2026-06-26, is worth recording because it signals where management thinks the growth is:

> "In the first quarter 2026 Stryker Corporation announced a change in our organizational structure. Our new Ortho Tech business combines the orthopaedic instruments portfolio from our Instruments business with the Mako and enabling technologies portfolio from our Other Orthopaedics business. By bringing Mako, power tools, cutting accessories, enabling technologies and the teams behind these products together under one business, we are simplifying the customer experience and striving to increase our speed to market through focused innovation."

A company that reorganises around a platform is telling you the platform is the strategy. Note also that no Mako placement, utilisation or procedure figure appears anywhere in this archive.

Three conclusions.

First, the recovery is real and fast. One damaged quarter, one clean quarter, guidance narrowed rather than cut. That is a good outcome and it should be credited.

Second, the episode is now a permanent feature of the risk assessment. Nothing in a financial statement predicted a 2.6% quarter from a 9% grower, and nothing will predict the next one. The company's disclosure of it is thin — a chief executive quotation, with no quantification of cost, no separate line item and no risk-factor amendment in the extracted filings — and we note that thinness.

Third, the market's memory is longer than one quarter. The stock is down 11.1% over twelve months and 14.8% from its high, having recovered 15.5% in the last three months. The re-rating from the incident is roughly half-reversed.
