The Overview

When an aircraft component wears out, the airline cannot fit any part it likes — it must fit the certified one. For a great many small, unglamorous parts, TransDigm is the only company certified to make it. The part might cost a few thousand dollars; the aircraft it is grounding earns tens of thousands a day. So the airline pays.

That is the whole business, and you can see it in one number: TransDigm keeps 46 cents of operating profit on every revenue dollar. Manufacturers do not do that. Monopolists do.

The company reported results this morning and they were good — sales up 22.5%, profit per share of $10.87 against the $10.30 expected. The shares opened 5.5% higher, and then fell all day, ending down 0.82%. From the highest price of the day to the lowest was 9.3%. We do not know why: the detailed release and the guidance were published on the company's website, not filed with the regulator, so they are not in the documents we hold. We are telling you that rather than guessing.

Now the part that determines whether this is a good investment rather than a good business.

TransDigm owes $30 billion. Its interest bill was $514 million in the last three months alone — up 29.5% from a year earlier. Its shareholders' equity is negative $9.7 billion, meaning the company's liabilities exceed its assets on paper.

That is not an accident or a warning sign of failure — it is the deliberate design. TransDigm borrows money and pays it out to shareholders as occasional enormous one-off dividends. Last fiscal year it paid $9.6 billion, equal to about 13.5% of the entire company's value today. The year before that: $2.0 billion. Two years before: $38 million.

So when you see a data feed quoting TransDigm's dividend yield as 7.06%, that is describing a one-off event, not an income stream. We do not treat it as one.

Two other things happened recently that standard data feeds have not picked up. On 27 July the company agreed to buy a business called Prince & Izant for about $1.066 billion in cash — roughly three times its expected annual sales. And on 22 July it appointed a new director.

The shares cost $1,275.05, about 27 times what analysts expect the company to earn in the year to September 2027. Our fair value estimate is $1,340, only 5% above. Analysts say $1,488.

The shares have fallen 19.6% over the last year while the stock market rose 24%. Something in the market has decided that a superb business borrowed to the hilt is worth less than it was, and today's reversal did not argue otherwise.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

"Long-run this is one of the best businesses this programme has covered and the knowledge base, thin as it is, identifies why. Roughly 80% of revenue is sole-source: an airline needing a certified replacement part has one supplier, and the part is a small fraction of the cost of the aircraft being repaired but a total constraint on returning it to service. That is the textbook condition for pricing power, and TransDigm has exercised it for two decades — a 46.05% operating margin on manufactured hardware is the proof. The aftermarket is structurally superior to original-equipment sales: it recurs for the thirty-year life of the airframe, it does not require winning a new platform, and it grows with flight hours rather than with aircraft deliveries. What tempers the long view is that the financing model is inseparable from the operating one. TransDigm runs at 6.26x net leverage and returns capital through debt-funded special dividends, which works while credit is available and margins expand, and which has produced negative book equity of $9,686 million. The business would survive a downturn; the equity is a different question."

Read the last two rows together and the mechanism is complete. TransDigm borrows and distributes. Fiscal 2023 paid $38 million of dividends. Fiscal 2024 paid $2,038 million. Fiscal 2025 paid $9,629 million — 13.5% of today's entire market capitalisation, in one year, funded by a $5,131 million increase in total debt. Retained earnings fell from minus $2,621 million to minus $10,606 million across the same period, which is exactly the cumulative distribution.

Negative equity therefore voids six ratios, and they are REJECTED with the mechanism rather than reported: returnOnEquityTTM of −20.44%, priceToBookRatioTTM of −6.07, debtToEquityRatioTTM of −2.74, bookValuePerShareTTM of −$209.83, tangibleBookValuePerShareTTM of −$466.86, and grahamNumberTTM, which the payload itself returns as null because the formula requires positive book value. Printing "return on equity of minus 20%" for a business earning a 21.2% return on capital employed would be arithmetically true and analytically worthless. The measures used instead are return on invested capital (16.0%), return on capital employed (21.2%) and return on assets (8.6%), all computed on positive denominators.
