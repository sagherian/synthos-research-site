The Overview

Atlassian makes the software that software teams use to organise their own work: Jira for tracking tasks and bugs, Confluence for writing things down, and a set of related tools. Roughly a quarter of a million companies pay a per-person monthly fee. It is a very profitable operation at the gross level — about 84 cents of every revenue dollar is left after the cost of running the service — and it generates well over a billion dollars of real cash each year.

The stock has fallen about 44% from its high. The reason is not that business got worse; revenue is still growing about 24% this year. The reason is that investors have started to doubt how durable that growth is. The worry, argued independently by three different experts we track, is that AI coding tools make it much easier to switch away from — or simply do without — the kind of task-tracking software Atlassian sells. Wall Street analysts have partly conceded the point: their own forecasts show growth roughly halving next year, to about 13%.

So at today's price you are paying about 17 times next year's expected profit. That is not expensive for a growing software company, but it is not obviously cheap for one whose growth is expected to halve. One complication worth knowing: on official accounting rules Atlassian loses money, because it pays employees heavily in stock — about $1.36 billion last year, over a quarter of all revenue. It buys back roughly as much stock as it issues, so existing owners are not being diluted, but every "cheap" valuation number quoted for this company quietly excludes that cost.

Our call is to wait. The business is fine, the price is fair, the argument against it has not been settled, and the company reports earnings in two days.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. No solvency question — net cash, real cash flow, low beta. The risk is structural and unresolved.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Excellent margins and self-funded, but consensus has growth halving next year.
  • Exponential Potential 4/10. A genuine AI-data advantage, but no exponent in the revenue model.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $109 against a current price of $175.61 — consistent with our call to stay away or wait for a better setup.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

3. The three yellow flags in the financials

Honesty requires flagging these before the valuation section, because two of them cut against the bull case.

(a) TTM free cash flow appears to be going backwards. FY25 free cash flow was $1.416B. The vendor's TTM price-to-free-cash-flow ratio of 22.61x against a $27.242B market cap implies TTM free cash flow of ~$1.205B — roughly 15% below FY25, during a year in which revenue grew 24%. The file contains no quarterly cash-flow statements, so I cannot decompose this (timing of collections, tax payments, the FY25 comparison base). I flag it as unexplained and material. Growing revenue 24% while free cash flow declines 15% is the kind of divergence that either resolves benignly in one quarter or turns out to be the story.

(b) The Q4 consensus implies a sequential revenue decline. Q3 FY26 revenue was $1.787B. The Q4 consensus is $1.663B — a 7.0% sequential drop. Subscription businesses do not normally do that. The most likely benign explanation is that Q3 contained lumpy upfront term-license revenue (Atlassian's Data Center product recognises a portion of multi-year term licences at the point of sale, which makes quarters uneven); note that Q3's +31.7% year-over-year growth was itself well above the Q1/Q2 pace of +20.6%/+23.3%, which is consistent with that reading. But this is a hypothesis, not a fact in the file, and I label it as such. The alternative reading — that the Q4 estimate simply has not been refreshed — is equally available. Either way, do not treat Q3's 31.7% as the run-rate.

(c) One historical estimate row is internally inconsistent. The FY23 (2023-06-29) estimate row carries epsAvg of +$1.758 alongside netIncomeAvg of -$504.9M. Positive EPS with negative net income is impossible on a single basis; the row mixes GAAP and non-GAAP fields. It does not affect any forward number used here, but it is a reminder that the estimate series is not internally uniform.

What I did NOT find corrupted, having checked: the most recent quarter (inc_q[0], Q3 FY26) is clean — net income -$98.4M against 260.965M shares gives -$0.377, matching the reported -$0.38 EPS; interest expense and share counts do not duplicate the prior quarter; the earnings-calendar actuals ($1.04, $1.22, $1.75) reconcile to the FY26 consensus of $5.48 once the $1.49 Q4 estimate is added ($5.50). The GAAP-negative / non-GAAP-positive split here is a real accounting fact, not a data error.

5. Competitive position and moat

Atlassian's moat is the classic bottoms-up software moat: the product is adopted by individual engineering teams without a procurement process, spreads laterally through an organisation, and then becomes the system of record for how work is tracked. Once a company's entire project history, sprint structure, permissions model and integration surface live in Jira and Confluence, the cost of moving is organisational rather than financial.

The evidence in this file that the moat is still working: 83.9% TTM gross margin (up, not down — FY25 gross margin was 82.8%), a negative 22.4-day cash-conversion cycle (customers pay before Atlassian pays its own bills, the hallmark of pricing power in subscription software), $2.243B of deferred revenue at FY25 (+22.9% year over year, growing slightly faster than revenue), and broad-based geographic growth.

The evidence the moat is being tested: the bear lane above, and the plain fact that the market has removed 44% of the equity value.

Our assessment: the moat is real but narrowing at the edges. Switching costs built on "all our history is in here" are strong against a competing tool and weak against a change in how work itself is organised. The a16z formulation — that AI collapses switching costs specifically for software that makes customers similar to their peers — is the most intellectually serious version of the bear case, and nothing in this dataset refutes it.

6a. What today's price assumes (the inversion)

At $103.71 — 17.1x FY27E non-GAAP EPS — the price embeds roughly the following falsifiable claims:

  • Revenue compounds at ≥13% through FY27 and ≥15% through FY28 (consensus: +13.4% FY27, +15.9% FY28), i.e. the deceleration from 24% stops at 13% rather than continuing to single digits. Most fragile assumption in the price — see below.
  • Non-GAAP EPS reaches ~$7.18 by FY28 (consensus, 7 analysts), requiring roughly 15% annual EPS growth from the $5.48 FY26 base, which in turn requires operating leverage on a cost base where R&D alone is 51.9% of revenue.
  • Gross margin holds above 82% (FY25: 82.8%; TTM: 83.9%) — i.e. no pricing concession forced by AI-native competitors or by customers renegotiating seat counts downward.
  • Free cash flow re-accelerates from the current ~$1.20B TTM back above the $1.42B FY25 level, since a 14.5x FY28 earnings multiple on a company that converts roughly a quarter of revenue to cash is only defensible if that conversion holds.
  • The market keeps paying ~17x forward — i.e. no further de-rating, despite the fact that the same market has already cut the multiple by 44% in twelve months and three tracked sources argue the process is unfinished.

The single most fragile assumption: that 13.4% is the trough rather than a waypoint. Everything else in the list follows from it. If FY27 revenue growth guides to single digits on 2026-08-06, the 17x multiple is not a floor.

6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the 6-24 month horizon ≈ EPS growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield.

  • EPS growth: consensus +10.6% FY26→FY27 ($5.48 → $6.06), then +18.4% FY27→FY28. Call it ~14% annualised.
  • Multiple drift: our base case assumes the market pays 18x forward at the horizon versus 17.1x today — a marginal expansion, and we want to be explicit that this is the weakest leg of the bridge. We are not forecasting a re-rating back toward the 29x the stock carried on FY25 earnings; we are assuming the de-rating stops. The mechanical rolldown from 18.9x FY26E to 17.1x FY27E at a constant price is not re-rating and we do not count it.
  • Shareholder yield: no dividend. Buybacks ($779M in FY25, 2.9% of market cap) are consumed by offsetting the $1.36B stock-compensation charge, so the net shareholder yield is approximately zero — the buyback keeps the share count flat rather than shrinking it. This matters: it means essentially all of the expected return must come from earnings growth and multiple, with no cushion underneath.

Our base case is therefore almost entirely an earnings-growth story with a flat multiple. $109 = 18x FY27E EPS of $6.06. That is a +5.2% return from $103.71 over the horizon. A thesis that needs multiple expansion to work is a fragile thesis, and we are deliberately not building one.

Scenario bands (stated arithmetic, no DCF):

  • Bear ~$67 — 11x FY27E EPS of $6.06. The de-rating continues because FY27 guidance comes in below 13%, the a16z thesis starts showing up in net revenue retention, and the market applies a mature-software multiple. -35% from spot.
  • Base ~$109 — 18x FY27E EPS of $6.06. Growth stabilises around the consensus 13-16%, free cash flow recovers toward $1.4B, the multiple holds. +5% from spot.
  • Bull ~$158 — 22x FY28E EPS of $7.18. The AI-agent thesis (cognitive_revolution's claim) converts into a visible new revenue line, growth re-accelerates above consensus, and the multiple partially recovers. +52% from spot.

Base sits ~5% above spot and 19% below the $134.89 street consensus. That gap is the dive's actual conclusion: we think the sell-side is paying for a re-rating we do not underwrite.

9. Verdict, kill-criteria and flip conditions

Watch. Atlassian is a high-quality, cash-generative, net-cash software franchise trading at a fair — not cheap — 17.1x FY27E non-GAAP earnings after a 44% de-rating. We decline to buy it today for three specific reasons, each of which is falsifiable:

1. Consensus itself models growth halving from +24.0% (FY26E) to +13.4% (FY27E). Our base fair value of $109 is only 5% above spot precisely because that deceleration is already the shared assumption.

2. The only tracked-expert lane on this name is net bearish, and the bear case (AI collapsing the switching costs of seat-priced workflow software) is structural, recent, argued independently by three sources, and unfalsified by anything in this dataset.

3. TTM free cash flow appears to be running ~15% below FY25 while revenue grew 24% — an unexplained divergence we cannot decompose with the data available, and one we are unwilling to underwrite blind.

Against that: earnings are in two days, and the FY27 guide is exactly the event that resolves point 1 and largely resolves point 2. Buying ahead of it would be paying for information we can have on Thursday for free.

Pre-registered ENTRY TRIGGERS (any one converts Watch → Stage-In, ≤1.5% starter):

  • A separately disclosed AI/agent revenue line of meaningful scale, converting the cognitive_revolution claim from thesis to line item.

Pre-registered AVOID criteria (any one converts Watch → Avoid):

  • FY27 revenue guidance in single digits — the bear case moving from thesis to fact.
  • Net revenue retention disclosed below ~110%, or any commentary indicating seat-count contraction at existing customers.
  • Gross margin below 80% — evidence of pricing concession.
  • The diluted share count resuming its climb (i.e. the buyback no longer absorbing the $1.36B stock-compensation charge), which would convert a manageable expense into genuine dilution.
  • A third consecutive quarter of declining free cash flow.
