The Overview

Truist is a bank. It takes deposits from people and businesses across the American Southeast, lends the money out, and keeps the difference. It also has a growing business advising companies and managing wealthy clients' money.

The three months to June were good. Revenue rose 5.6%. More importantly, the fee side — advising, trading, wealth management — rose 17.4% while costs rose only 2.3%. That combination is the single best thing a bank can report, because it means each extra dollar of revenue arrives with almost no extra cost. Profit for shareholders rose 28.7%, and profit per share rose 36.7% because there are fewer shares.

Loans are being repaid. Truist wrote off 0.50% of its loans as uncollectible — slightly less than a year earlier — and set aside less money for future losses than it did last year.

The shares cost $52.66 and pay a $2.08 dividend, a 3.95% yield. On top of that, Truist bought back $1.2 billion of its own shares in three months alone, which is roughly 7% of the whole company each year. Put the dividend and the buyback together and shareholders are being handed back about 11% of the share price annually.

That is where the caution starts. The company discloses that its total payout ratio was 121% last quarter — it gave shareholders more than it earned. A bank can do that for a while by running down its capital cushion, and Truist's cushion, measured by a ratio regulators watch called CET1, is 10.9%. That is adequate. It is not generous.

And the person who decided all this is leaving. William Rogers retires as chief executive on 1 September, four weeks from now, moving to a chairman role. Whoever replaces him will have to choose: keep handing money back, or rebuild the cushion. That single decision matters more to the share price over the next two years than a year of profits.

There is one more detail we think readers should see. On 20 July, three days after the good results, Mr Rogers sold 13,250 shares at $52.36 — and the filing shows his indirect holding afterwards as zero. Two other insiders also sold in the same fortnight, one of them on 31 July with the paperwork filed on the very day of this report. None of these sales is large, and executives sell for many reasons. But it is a pattern and we report it.

Our estimate of fair value is $60, about 14% above the price. Analysts on average say $54.86 — only 4% above. A good bank at a fair price, with a leadership change four weeks away, is a Hold.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Improving credit, adequate capital, and a payout ratio above 100%.
  • Growth Quality 6/10. Excellent fee growth and operating leverage; the lending half grew 0.9%.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. The lowest in this batch. It is a bank.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $60 against a current price of $50.72 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Three observations, in order of importance.

The operating leverage is genuine and it is the best fact in this dive. Revenue +5.6% against expenses +2.3% — 330 basis points. That is what converts a 5.6% revenue increase into a 20.0% pre-tax increase, and it is the single hardest thing for a bank to produce.

The growth is on the fee side, not the lending side. Noninterest income rose 17.4% to $1,644 million while net interest income rose 0.9% to $3,621 million. Net interest income is 68.8% of total revenue and it is barely moving; the 10-Q attributes the small increase to "higher earning assets and loan growth." The fee growth is concentrated in Wholesale Banking, where the 10-Q reports noninterest income up $217 million "driven by higher income from investment banking and trading activity, wealth management, and lending related revenue, partially offset by decreased income from certain equity and other investments." That is a good mix shift and it is also the most cyclical revenue a bank has.

The buyback contributes eight percentage points of the earnings-per-share growth. Net income to common rose 28.7% and diluted EPS 36.7%. The gap is the share count.

Every credit metric moved the right way, and every one of them moved slightly. Net charge-offs down one basis point, the allowance ratio down two, past-dues down one, the provision down 19%. This is a benign, unremarkable, improving credit environment at this bank, and nothing in the file suggests otherwise.

The capital picture is the interesting one and it needs stating carefully. A CET1 ratio of 10.9% is adequate — comfortably above requirement, and rising. But note the composition of the rise: "current quarter earnings and a reduction in risk-weighted assets, partially offset by capital returned to shareholders." The ratio improved partly because the balance sheet shrank.

And the 121% total payout ratio is the company's own number, not a derivation. Truist returned 121% of its quarterly earnings to shareholders — $1.2 billion of repurchase plus roughly $650 million of common dividends against $1,519 million available to common. Annualised, the buyback alone is approximately $4.8 billion against a $65.61 billion market capitalisation — roughly 7.3% a year — and with the 3.95% dividend the total shareholder yield is approximately 11.3%, the highest in this batch.

Tangible book value per share FELL, from $33.48 to $33.40. A bank returning 121% of earnings while tangible book value declines is converting book value into share count reduction. At 1.58x tangible book that is accretive to per-share value and it is not free — it is the reason CET1 rose only ten basis points on a 15.4% return on tangible common equity.
