The Overview

Target is a big American retailer: about 2,000 large stores selling groceries, clothes, homewares and toys, plus a website whose orders are mostly packed by those same stores.

For four years running, Target sold less than the year before. Not slightly less each year — a slow, steady decline from $109 billion to $105 billion while prices in the economy were rising. Shoppers were going elsewhere, mostly to Walmart.

Then something changed. In the three months to early May, sales rose 6.7%. More importantly, 4.4% more people came through the doors. That distinction matters more than anything else in this report. A shop can make its sales figure look better by raising prices or selling more expensive things. It cannot make more people walk in unless they actually want to.

Profit from operations, as reported, fell 22.9%. That looks alarming and is not. The same quarter a year earlier included a $593 million one-off gain from settling a long-running lawsuit about credit-card fees. Strip that out of the comparison and profit was 29% higher. The company says so itself.

There is also a quietly excellent business hiding inside Target. When a brand wants to advertise to Target's shoppers, it pays Target — a business called Roundel. That advertising revenue went from $649 million to $915 million in two years, a 41% increase, and it costs almost nothing extra to deliver. It is still small — under 1% of sales — but it is the highest-quality thing here.

So why not buy it?

Because the shares have already gone up 48% in a year, and closed less than 1% below their highest price of the last twelve months. More tellingly, the average Wall Street analyst target price is $139.65 — below today's price of $148.09. That is unusual and it is the market's professional observers saying, collectively, that the good news is in.

And three senior Target executives sold shares in May and June, including the chairman, who sold 50,000 shares at around $130. The stock is now $148.

Our estimate of fair value is $155, about 5% above the price, against roughly 26% of downside if the recovery stalls. That is not an attractive trade-off.

One number worth holding onto, though. Target is still 44% below its highest price of the last six years. This is a company recovering from a long slump, not one at an all-time peak. If the next set of results — due 19 August, two weeks away — shows shoppers coming back a second time, this becomes a different conversation.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Four observations, in order of importance.

First, the traffic number is the finding. A comparable-sales increase of 5.6% built on 4.4 points of traffic and 1.1 points of ticket is the good version of that composition. Retailers routinely deliver comps on price and mix while footfall declines; that is a slow liquidation of the customer base. Target's shoppers came back in numbers. After four consecutive fiscal years of falling revenue, that is the first hard evidence against the structural-decline thesis the knowledge base states in Section 6.

Second, the growth was bought. SG&A rose 21.2% against revenue's 6.7% — 261 basis points of margin — and even excluding the $593 million settlement gain from the prior-year base, underlying SG&A rose from $5,184 million to $5,562 million, +7.3%, slightly faster than revenue. Gross margin improved 84 basis points, so the merchandising is working; the operating margin did not, because the company is spending on price, labour and marketing to bring the traffic back. Whether that spend recedes as the traffic sticks is the single most important operating question for the next four quarters.

Third, the adjusted comparison is favourable and it is the company's own. Excluding the settlement, adjusted operating income of $1,135 million against approximately $879 million — +29.1% — on revenue up 6.7%. That is genuine operating leverage in the underlying business and it is disclosed rather than derived.

The finding: advertising revenue grew 41.0% in one year, from $649 million to $915 million. The 10-Q describes it: "Advertising revenue — Primarily represents revenue related to certain advertising services provided via our Roundel digital advertising business offering. Roundel services are classified as either Net Sales or as a reduction of Cost of Sales or Selling, General, and Administrative (SG&A) Expenses, depending on the nature of the advertising arrangement."

Note the accounting: only part of Roundel appears as revenue at all. The rest reduces cost of sales or SG&A, which means the disclosed $915 million understates the business and also means part of the gross-margin improvement in Section 1 may be Roundel rather than merchandising. We cannot separate them from this archive and we say so.

The rest of the "Other" bucket, from the same note: "Includes commissions earned on third-party sales through our Target Plus third-party digital marketplace, Target Circle 360 membership revenue, Shipt membership and service revenues, rental income, and other miscellaneous revenues." Commissions on a marketplace, subscription revenue and advertising are all structurally better businesses than selling merchandise, and they are the reason the exponential score is 4 rather than 2. Together they remain a low single-digit percentage of revenue.

The credit-card arrangement is worth naming because it removes a risk. "We receive payments under a credit card program agreement with TD Bank Group (TD)... TD underwrites, funds, and owns Target Circle credit card receivables, controls risk management policies, and oversees regulatory compliance." Target takes a profit share and carries none of the credit risk — $522 million of FY2025 revenue at essentially no capital cost, declining 9.4% year on year.

Geography — seg_geo is effectively empty. The block contains exactly one row, for fiscal 2013, reporting United States revenue of $71,279 million. There is no geographic data covering any year since. Target is a United States retailer so the economic loss is minimal, but the field is a thirteen-year-old single observation and is reported as such rather than described.
