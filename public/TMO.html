The Overview

If a scientist anywhere in the world wants to do an experiment, someone has to sell them the equipment, the chemicals, the plastic tubes and the machines that measure the result. Thermo Fisher is the biggest company doing that. It also runs factories that manufacture other companies' drugs for them, and it runs the clinical trials those drugs have to pass. It has about 125,000 employees and sells in roughly 70 countries.

That is a very good position to hold. About 84% of what it sells is either a consumable that gets used up and reordered, or a service under contract — only about 16% is a one-time machine sale. It has $29.7 billion of work already under contract and not yet delivered. It is not going away, and the science it supplies is not going to get less important.

Here is the problem. In 2022 the company sold $44.9 billion of goods and services. In 2025 it sold $44.6 billion. Four years, and it is slightly smaller than it was. The profit per share was $17.64 in 2022 and $17.74 in 2025 — essentially identical, and even that only happened because the company bought back enough of its own shares to shrink the count by about 4%.

Why did it stop growing? Two things happened at once. The COVID testing business, which had been enormous, went away — that part of the revenue fell by about $3 billion. And at the same time, drug companies stopped buying new laboratory equipment, universities had their budgets cut, and business in China slowed; equipment sales have fallen in each of the last three years. What filled the hole was a business the company bought at the end of 2021 for running clinical trials and manufacturing other people's drugs. That part of revenue went from $8.9 billion to $18.6 billion.

So the company did not really grow. It bought a large business and used it to replace what it lost. It has kept doing that: over roughly four and a half years it spent about $19.7 billion of cash buying companies, including $8.87 billion in the first six months of this year alone — which is more than it earns in cash in a whole year, so it borrowed to do it. It now owes about $43 billion.

The most recent quarter was good: sales up 10.5% on a year earlier. But a large part of that came from the company it bought in March. The company does publish a figure showing how much it grew without acquisitions — and that figure is in a table that did not survive into the text version of the filing we have, and it is not in our data feed either. So we cannot tell you the single most important number about this business, and we are not going to guess at it.

The shares cost $564.75. Last year the company actually earned $18.59 per share by the official accounting, or $23.83 on the adjusted basis the company and analysts prefer. So you are paying about 30 times official earnings or about 24 times adjusted. Analysts expect about $27.40 next year, which would be about 21 times. Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $575 — about 2% more than they cost. The average analyst says $592. When our own number is barely above the price and below the crowd's, and when we have no independent information that anyone else lacks, the honest answer is that the shares are worth about what they cost.

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. 1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

The 10-K opens with a claim that is close to true: Thermo Fisher is "the world leader in serving science." The company was established in 1956, is headquartered at 168 Third Avenue, Waltham, Massachusetts, listed on the NYSE since 17 March 1980, and is run by chief executive Marc N. Casper. It employed approximately 125,000 colleagues at 31 December 2025 — 59,000 in the Americas, 44,000 in Europe/Middle East/Africa and 22,000 in Asia-Pacific — including approximately 14,000 sales personnel. Its brands are Thermo Scientific, Applied Biosystems, Invitrogen, Fisher Scientific, Unity Lab Services, Patheon and PPD.

The four reportable segments (10-K Item 1 and Note 11)

  • Life Sciences Solutions — reagents, instruments and consumables for biological and medical research and for the production of biologics. Three businesses: biosciences, genetic sciences, bioproduction. Bioproduction is the fastest-growing line the filings name.
  • Analytical Instruments — three businesses: chromatography and mass spectrometry, chemical analysis, electron microscopy. Electron microscopy explicitly serves the semiconductor market as well as life and materials sciences.
  • Specialty Diagnostics — five businesses: clinical diagnostics, immunodiagnostics, microbiology, transplant diagnostics and the healthcare market channel. The microbiology business is being sold (see below).
  • Laboratory Products and Biopharma Services — four businesses: laboratory products, the research and safety market channel, pharma services and clinical research. This is the segment that houses Patheon (contract drug manufacturing), PPD (clinical research) and now Clario.

The 10-Q confirms at Note 11 that the company reports in four segments and that "During 2026, there have been no changes to the company's basis of segmentation."

seg_prod is NOT the segment table — and this is the biggest presentation defect in the file

The vendor's seg_prod block supplies three lines: Consumables, Instruments and Service. Those are not segments. They are the revenue-by-type disaggregation that appears at 10-K Note 6 and 10-Q Note 6 under the heading "Revenues by type are as follows", and the filings are explicit that the two are orthogonal: "Each reportable segment earns revenues from consumables, instruments and services in North America, Europe, Asia-Pacific and other regions."

Read naively, seg_prod would tell a reader that Thermo Fisher has three segments. It has four, and none of them is called Consumables. The four-segment names do appear in the vendor block — but only in the FY2017 and FY2015 rows and earlier, and under a superseded taxonomy: the 2017 row reads "Laboratory Products and Services", which is the name the segment carried before it was renamed Laboratory Products and Biopharma Services. The 2013 row uses "Analytical Technologies", a segment that no longer exists. The 2012, 2011 and 2010 rows contain nothing but "Intersegment Eliminations" and "Excluded from Reportable Segment Operating Income" — line items, not segments.

So the vendor cannot answer the segment question at all, and neither can the filings as extracted, because the Note 11 segment tables are numeric and the extraction strips numeric tables. The four-segment revenue and margin split is not available from any source in this file, and no such figure appears anywhere in this dive.

Our position on that last exclusion is that it is defensible in principle and dangerous in practice for THIS company.
