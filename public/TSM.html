The Overview

TSMC does not design chips. It manufactures them, for other companies, to their designs. Almost every advanced chip in the world — in phones, cars, data centres, laptops — is physically made by this one company, in facilities mostly on one island.

Being the only firm that can do something at the leading edge means you can charge for it. In 2025 TSMC made essentially the same number of wafers it could have made in 2024 — its total capacity barely moved — yet revenue rose 32% and the profit on each dollar of sales rose from 56 cents to 60 cents. That is not a company selling more; it is a company selling harder things for more money. The proportion of its output at the most advanced sizes went from 69% to 74% in a single year.

The growth is still speeding up. Because TSMC is a Taiwanese company listed in the US, it must file monthly revenue figures — something no American company does. Its June 2026 report, filed on 13 July, showed revenue 68% higher than June a year earlier. That is the freshest, hardest number in this entire analysis, and it is better than the annual trend.

There are three catches, and they matter.

The first changes the price you think you are paying. TSMC keeps its books in New Taiwan dollars but the security Americans buy — an ADS — trades in US dollars, and each ADS equals five Taiwanese shares. The data file mixes the two currencies without saying which is which, and it prices the company off the Taiwanese share, which trades meaningfully cheaper. We can prove it from the file: on 28 July 2026 a company vice-president bought both on the same day, and the American version cost about 15% more per unit of ownership. So the "26.9 times earnings" the file reports is what a Taiwanese buyer pays. An American buyer pays about 30 times. Every multiple here has been rebuilt to reflect what you actually pay.

The second is that this company spends almost everything it earns on new factories — about US$41 billion last year, guided to US$52-56 billion this year. That is a good problem, because it is spending in response to demand, but it means the cash left over for shareholders is small relative to the price.

The third has no number attached to it at all. Nearly all of this capacity sits in one place, and that place is the subject of a long-running international dispute. The company's own annual report lists it as a risk; experts we track describe the consequence of a serious disruption as a global depression lasting years. There is no way to calculate the odds, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. What we can do is refuse to pretend it away and size the position accordingly — it is the reason this is not a larger recommendation.

Our estimate of fair value is $470 against a price of $417. The analysts who cover it think $596. We are more cautious than every one of them, and Section 6c explains why.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. Balance sheet is close to flawless — US$66 billion net cash, 285x interest coverage. The 6 is entirely the concentration risk, which cannot be sized.
  • Growth Quality 9/10. +31.6% in FY2025 accelerating to +41.6% consensus for FY2026, with margin expanding and a monthly filing confirming it. Docked one point for weak cash conversion.
  • Exponential Potential 7/10. Consensus triples revenue by FY2029 — but the company deliberately under-expands capacity, and it starts from a US$2.16 trillion base.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $470 against a current price of $415.54 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

2. The foreign-private-issuer problem — the single most important analytical issue on this name

TSM is a foreign private issuer. There is no 10-K and there is no 10-Q. The filings available are a 20-F filed 2026-04-16 (the annual report, covering FY2025) and three 6-Ks dated 2026-07-13, 2026-07-16 and 2026-07-24. That fact has four consequences, and the first three are valuation-breaking if unhandled.

2.1 The disclosure gap — annual, not quarterly

A US filer of this size would have published two 10-Qs since its last annual report, each with a full balance sheet, a cash-flow statement, segment detail and a management discussion. TSMC has published none. The most recent balance sheet and cash-flow statement in this file are dated 2025-12-31, seven months stale, and there is no quarterly balance sheet at all — bal_a contains three annual periods and nothing else. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over 12-24 months decomposes as: earnings-per-share growth (+30.4% consensus FY2027, +26.0% FY2028) + multiple drift + shareholder yield (0.70-0.85% dividend, no meaningful buyback).

Our $470 base assumes modest multiple EXPANSION — from 18.7x to 21.1x FY2027 — and we name that as the fragile leg, but the far more important point is how little of the return depends on it. If the multiple simply holds at today's 18.7x and FY2027 consensus earnings of US$22.32 are delivered, the price is $417 — exactly where it is, because 18.7x FY2027 is what is being paid today. The return over a 12-month horizon therefore comes from the market rolling its anchor forward: at an unchanged 18.7x on FY2028 consensus of US$28.13, the price is $526 (+26%). That rolldown, not a re-rating, is the engine.

We do not underwrite the full $526, for two reasons stated plainly. First, the FY2028 line carries only five analysts and this dive does not rest conclusions on it. Second, the rolldown assumes no widening of the risk discount — and on a name whose dominant risk is unsizeable, assuming the discount stays constant is an assumption, not a neutral base case. Our $470 therefore sits between the no-return case ($417, multiple holds on FY2027) and the full-rolldown case ($526), and represents 21.1x FY2027 or 16.7x FY2028.

The bull case at $610 requires 27.3x FY2027 — roughly where the street already is (its $596 consensus target is 26.7x FY2027) — and that leg genuinely needs the crowd to stop discounting the concentration risk. It is not needed for the base case.

Note the mechanical trap this name sets, per the addendum's guardrail: 24.4x FY2026 becoming 18.7x FY2027 at a constant price is rolldown, not compression. The only honest re-rating statement is relative to what the market pays at each date, and on that basis our base assumes a modest expansion and the bear assumes a sharp compression to 12.1x.

  • Currency basis note — the most important methodological statement in this dive: TSMC reports in New Taiwan dollars and its ADS trades in US dollars. All USD conversions use NT$31.11 to US$1.00, the FY2025 weighted-average rate disclosed in the 20-F, chosen because it is the only rate with an auditable source and because it reconciles trailing TWD earnings per ADS-equivalent share (NT$434.95 → US$13.98) with the sum of the four earn_cal USD actuals (US$13.81) to within 1.2%. A 5% move in the NT dollar shifts every USD multiple in this dive by 5% in the opposite direction. The 20-F confirms that "substantially all of our sales are denominated in U.S. dollars while we publish our financial statements in NT dollars," and that the NT dollar appreciated from NT$32.13 (2024) to NT$31.11 (2025), which had an unfavourable effect on reported revenue.
