The Overview

Uber runs three marketplaces: rides, food and grocery delivery, and freight. It does not own the cars or employ most of the drivers; it matches supply and demand and keeps a share of each transaction.

In the three months to March, 199 million people used it at least monthly — up 17% — taking 3.6 billion trips, up 20%. The total value of everything booked through it was $53.7 billion, up 25% (21% ignoring currency movements). Operating profit rose 57%.

Reported revenue grew only 14%, which looks like a slowdown and is not. The company changed how it accounts for its UK ride business, and states that this reduced reported revenue by exactly $1.0 billion in the quarter. Add that back and revenue grew about 23%, in line with everything else.

Despite all that, the shares are down 17% over the past year while the American stock market rose 24% — a 41-point gap — and sit 28% below their high, only 9% above their low for the year.

There is one reason: self-driving cars. If robot taxis get cheap enough, Uber's advantage — a huge pool of human drivers — becomes worthless. Our research library contains this argument properly on both sides, with 64 separate expert claims about Uber, more than any other company in this group. The bears note that driverless rides may cost 60-80% less than Uber's. The bulls note that self-driving cars already on Uber's network run about 30% busier than the same cars operating alone — which, if it holds, means fleet owners make more money by listing on Uber than by going it alone.

Two things you will not find in the standard data.

Uber's official profit figures are meaningless. Last year it reported $10.1 billion of net income on $5.6 billion of operating profit, because the tax line was negative $4.3 billion — an accounting release, not cash. The year before it was negative $5.8 billion. The published "effective tax rate" reads minus 77%.

And on 16 July, Uber agreed to buy Delivery Hero, the German food-delivery group, for €41.50 per share in cash, completing in late 2027 and funded with cash and borrowing. None of the analyst forecasts in our data include it.

The shares are $71.99, about 16 times what analysts expect next year's earnings to be, and the company generates cash equal to 6.7% of its value every year. Our estimate of fair value is $90, about 25% higher. The average analyst says $102 — and of 61 analysts covering it, not one says sell.

It reports tomorrow. Our own most recent expert claim, from a week ago, describes it as "a dirt-cheap stock with horrendous momentum — exactly the setup... getting slaughtered on any miss."

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

1. What the business is, and the disclosure changes that matter

Uber Technologies, Inc., CIK 0001543151, NYSE-listed since 2019-05-10; chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi; approximately 34,000 employees. Three reportable segments, defined in the 10-Q:

  • Mobility — "products connect consumers with Drivers who provide rides in a variety of vehicles, such as cars, auto rickshaws, motorbikes, minibuses, or taxis. Mobility also includes activity related to our financial partnerships products and advertising."
  • Delivery — "allow consumers to search for and discover local restaurants, order a meal, and either pick-up at the restaurant or have the meal delivered... grocery, alcohol, convenience and retail categories collectively as Grocery & Retail. Delivery also includes advertising."
  • Freight — "connects Carriers with Shippers on our platform."

seg_prod and seg_geo are BOTH EMPTY in the vendor payload. All segment data in this dive comes from the 10-Q.

Two disclosure changes make the historical series discontinuous and neither is visible in the payload.

First, the segment measure changed. From the 10-Q: "Beginning in the first quarter of 2026, we changed our segment operating performance measure from Segment Adjusted EBITDA to Segment Operating Income. Segment Operating Income excludes certain non-cash items or items that management does not believe are reflective of our ongoing operating performance. Segment results for the comparable prior period have been recast to reflect these changes." Any model built on Segment Adjusted EBITDA before 2026 is not comparable to anything published since.

Second, and much larger, the United Kingdom Mobility business model changed. From the 10-Q: "Revenue was $13.2 billion, up 14% year-over-year, primarily attributable to an increase in Gross Bookings of 25%... The increase in revenue was partially offset by Mobility business model changes in the United Kingdom ("UK") that negatively impacted revenue by $1.0 billion." The filing also records the underlying mechanism: since 2022 "Uber UK became a merchant of transportation and is required to remit VAT", under an order "which allows for VAT remittance on a calculated margin, rather than on Gross Bookings."

> Adding the disclosed $1.0 billion back, March-quarter revenue was approximately $14.2 billion against $11.533 billion — roughly 23% growth, not 14%. This single disclosure is the difference between a business decelerating and a business accelerating, and it is invisible in every field of the vendor payload.
