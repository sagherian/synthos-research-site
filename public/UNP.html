The Overview

Union Pacific owns the railroad network across the western two-thirds of the United States. Freight railroads are among the best businesses that exist for a simple physical reason: moving a ton of goods by rail costs about a quarter of what it costs by truck, and nobody is building new railroad lines. Union Pacific keeps about forty cents of profit on every dollar of revenue.

The business has, however, stopped growing. Revenue in 2022 was $24.9 billion. In 2025 it was $24.5 billion. For four years, nothing.

Last July, Union Pacific agreed to buy Norfolk Southern, which owns the eastern network. If regulators allow it, the combined company would be the first railroad able to move a container from a Pacific port to an Atlantic one without handing it to a competitor halfway — "over 50,000 miles across 43 states," in the company's words.

The price is high. Norfolk Southern shareholders receive one Union Pacific share plus $88.82 in cash for each of their shares. That means Union Pacific will issue about 225 million new shares — on top of the 594 million it has, so existing owners are diluted by about 38% — and pay about $20 billion in cash, mostly borrowed. Union Pacific has also stopped buying back its own shares, which it had been doing at nearly $2.7 billion a year.

None of this is in the standard financial data. Worse, the analyst forecasts are internally confused about it: their revenue estimates for 2028 onward clearly assume the merger happened, but their per-share earnings estimates for the same years divide by the old share count.

The regulator, the Surface Transportation Board, received the application in December 2025 and has not ruled. Nothing in the available filings says when it will.

Meanwhile the shares are $296.35, up 35% over the last year against 24% for the market, and within 4% of their highest price in a year. Our estimate of value is $305 — about 3% above the price — and the average analyst says $330.

  • Downside Risk 6/10. A superb asset about to take on $20 billion of debt and issue 38% more shares, subject to a regulator's decision.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. No growth for four years, a genuine acceleration in 2026, and everything after that depends on the merger.
  • Exponential Potential 3/10. Railroads are the opposite of an exponential. That is mostly a compliment.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $305 against a current price of $296.35 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Four consequences, none of which appears in any vendor field:

1. Dilution of 37.9%. 225 million new shares against a June-quarter diluted count of 594.0 million.

2. Leverage. Approximately $20 billion of new debt on top of $31.814 billion existing takes gross debt toward $52 billion — roughly 4x combined EBITDA before synergies, against 2.39x today.

3. The buyback is gone. Union Pacific repurchased $2.679 billion in 2025, $1.505 billion in 2024 and $6.282 billion in 2022. It is now zero, and the diluted share count has stopped falling — 593.2M, 593.5M, 593.6M, 594.0M across the last four quarters, i.e. rising.

4. A $2.5 billion contingent obligation in either direction with no vendor field.

The regulatory timeline is unknowable from this file. The application went in on 2025-12-19 and the 10-Q filed seven months later reports no decision. We do not speculate on a date, and we treat the outcome as genuinely binary.
