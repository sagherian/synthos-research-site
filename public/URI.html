The Overview

United Rentals rents construction equipment — diggers, cherry pickers, generators, pumps, trench shoring — to builders and industrial customers. It is the largest such company in North America.

The business is doing well and the reason is worth understanding. Last quarter its rental revenue rose 12.7%. That came from two things: the fleet got 7.1% bigger, and something the company calls "fleet productivity" rose 3.4%. Fleet productivity combines the rate charged, how much of the time equipment is out on hire, and what mix of equipment. A rental company that adds 7% more machines and still gets 3.4% more out of each one is a company whose prices are not being competed down. That is the whole investment case, and it matches what our knowledge base says about this industry: two claims from an investor describing equipment rental as a market where a few players compete on how much capacity to build rather than on price, so profits stay high.

There are two problems.

The first is the price. The shares closed today at $1,150.13, which is exactly their highest price of the past year — to the cent. They are up 47.6% in six months. Analysts expect $56.44 of earnings next year, so you are paying about 20 times. This company has historically traded at closer to half that, because renting construction equipment is a cyclical business: when building activity falls, both the number of machines out on hire and the price per machine fall together. And this share price moves about 1.8 times as much as the market in either direction.

The second problem is ours, not the company's, and we have to be straight about it. The regulatory filings we were given for United Rentals — the annual report, both quarterly reports and all three current reports — contain no financial tables at all. Only the narrative text was preserved. That means we can read management's discussion of the business, which is detailed and useful, but we cannot check the balance sheet, the cash-flow statement or the debt schedule against a filed document. Everything we say about debt, cash and capital spending comes from the data vendor and is unverified. For a company with $16.5 billion of reported debt that spends $4.5 billion a year buying machines, that is a meaningful gap, and we would rather tell you about it than pretend it is not there.

Our estimate of fair value is $1,101 — about 4% below today's price. The average analyst says $1,233; the median says $1,300; but five of the forty-one covering this stock rate it a sell, which is more than any other company in this batch.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

1. The data constraint, stated first because it shapes everything

All six documents in the United Rentals filing archive are prose-only. A check across the archive returns zero [TABLE] markers in each of the 10-K filed 2026-01-28, the 10-Q filed 2026-04-22, the 10-Q filed 2026-07-22, and the 8-Ks filed 2026-05-08, 2026-06-18 and 2026-07-22.

What that means in practice. The extracted text preserves the management's discussion and analysis, the notes narrative, the legal and covenant descriptions and the 8-K item text — all of which are used extensively below and are genuinely informative. What it does not preserve is any financial statement: no consolidated balance sheet, no statement of cash flows, no debt maturity schedule, no lease table, no segment table.

The consequence for this dive, stated as a rule and applied throughout. Every revenue, growth-rate, fleet-productivity and operational figure in this dive is filing-verified from the management discussion. Every balance-sheet, cash-flow, debt and capital-expenditure figure comes from the vendor payload and is explicitly labelled as unverified. Where the standing rule says the filing wins over the vendor, there is no filing to win with, and the correct response is to say so rather than to present vendor data as though it had been checked.

The checks that therefore could NOT be run on this name, all of which fired on other names in this batch: the lease-inflation test on totalDebt (which found $17.2bn on FedEx, $5.8bn on Digital Realty and $982M on Boston Scientific); the capital-expenditure verification (which found a $3.18 billion zero on Digital Realty and a $22 million error on Simon Property Group); and the enterprise-value net-debt reconciliation.

One thing the prose does confirm about the capital structure, from the 10-Q's guarantor disclosure: "As of June 30, 2026, the indebtedness, net of debt issuance costs, of our non-guarantors was comprised of (i) $1.414 billion of outstanding borrowings by the SPV in connection with the Company's accounts receivable securitization facility, (ii) $147 of outstanding borrowings under the ABL facility by non-guarantor subsidiaries and (iii) $15 of finance leases of our non-guarantor subsidiaries." That is a partial view — non-guarantor subsidiaries only — but it establishes that finance leases at those entities are $15 million, which is a very small number against the $1,124 million the payload records as capitalLeaseObligations for the consolidated group.

And the covenant position is disclosed in prose and is favourable: "Based on our current total indebtedness leverage ratio (as defined in the applicable debt agreements) and usage of the ABL facility as of June 30, 2026, we met the criteria under the applicable debt agreements for these exceptions, and as a result we were not restricted in our ability to make share repurchases and dividend payments."

The company's own explanation, quoted because it is the whole argument: "Equipment rentals increased $434, or 12.7 percent, primarily due to a 7.1 percent increase in average OEC and a 3.4 percent increase in fleet productivity."

Fleet productivity is the metric to watch and the 10-Q explains why: "Fleet productivity is a comprehensive metric that provides greater insight into the decisions made by our managers in support of equipment rental growth and returns. Specifically, we seek to optimize the interplay of rental rates, time utilization and mix to drive rental revenue. Fleet productivity aggregates, in one metric, the impact of changes in rates, utilization and mix on owned equipment rental revenue."

Two readings.

First, the growth is accelerating and it is not just fleet. Revenue grew 7.1% in FY2024, 4.9% in FY2025, and then 9.6% in the first half of 2026 and 11.8% in the June quarter alone. And the acceleration is roughly two-thirds fleet size and one-third productivity — a 7.1% OEC increase plus a 3.4% productivity increase compounding to the 12.7% rental growth. A cyclical business reaccelerating from 4.9% to 11.8% is either early in a new upcycle or benefiting from a specific demand source, and the filings do not disclose which.

Second, fleet productivity improved sequentially within the half — +2.9% for six months and +3.4% for the quarter, implying roughly +2.4% in the March quarter. Rate and utilisation are getting better, not worse, while the fleet grows 7%. That is the Cournot claim in Section 5 tested and passed.

The base-period distortion, disclosed and quantified. From the 10-Q: "In January 2025, we announced that we had signed a merger agreement to acquire H&E Equipment Services, Inc. d/b/a H&E Rentals. In February 2025, following the termination of that merger agreement, we received a break-up fee of $64. Our results for the six months ended June 30, 2025 include a net $39 merger termination benefit... For the six months ended June 30, 2025, the impact of the merger termination was a $29 after-tax benefit, or $0.45 per diluted share, to net income and a $52 benefit to adjusted EBITDA, cash flow from operating activities and free cash flow."

So the first half of 2025 was flattered by $52 million of adjusted EBITDA and $0.45 per share — which means the 2026 comparisons in this file are struck against an inflated base, and the underlying improvement is slightly better than the reported one. It also records a corporate action the payload does not contain: United Rentals attempted to buy H&E Equipment Services in January 2025 and the deal was terminated a month later, with United Rentals collecting a $64 million break-up fee. This is an acquisitive company whose most recent large attempt failed, and that context matters for anyone modelling capital allocation.

One other corporate action, from the 8-K filed 2026-06-18: Amendment No. 18 to the Third Amended and Restated Receivables Purchase Agreement, extending the accounts-receivable securitisation facility to 2027-06-18, further extendable on a 364-day basis by mutual agreement. Advances under the facility are reflected as debt on the balance sheet and stood at $1,414 million at 2026-06-30.

Three observations, each carrying the unverified caveat.

Capital intensity is the defining feature. Capital expenditure of $4,528 million is 28.1% of FY2025 revenue and capexToDepreciationTTM is 1.590 — this business reinvests roughly one and a half times its depreciation charge every year. That is not a defect signature; it is what an equipment rental company does, and a ratio below 1.0 would be the warning sign.

But the vendor's free-cash-flow figure is not the company's, and the difference is large. From the 10-Q: "We define 'free cash flow' as net cash provided by operating activities less payments for purchases of, and PLUS PROCEEDS FROM, equipment and intangible assets." United Rentals adds back the cash it receives from selling used fleet; the vendor's calculation does not. The payload's own seg_prod block shows "Rental Equipment" revenue of $1,413 million in FY2025 — that is the sale of used rental fleet. On the company's definition, free cash flow would therefore be materially higher than the vendor's $662 million — our derivation puts it in the order of $2.0–2.1 billion, a roughly 2.9% yield rather than 0.92%. We label that a derivation rather than a figure because the cash-flow statement that would confirm the proceeds line is not in the archive. Consequently freeCashFlowYieldTTM of 0.883%, priceToFreeCashFlowRatioTTM of 113.3x and evToFreeCashFlowTTM of 137.4x are rejected as understating by roughly a factor of three.

Capital return is substantial and confirmed by the prose covenant disclosure. Repurchases of $1,969 million in FY2025 — 2.75% of the current market capitalisation — plus $464 million of dividends. Weighted diluted shares have fallen from 72,929 thousand in FY2020 to 63,600 thousand in the June 2026 quarter, a 12.8% reduction. Total shareholder yield is approximately 3.4%.
