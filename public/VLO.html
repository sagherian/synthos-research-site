The Overview

Valero buys crude oil and turns it into petrol, diesel and jet fuel. Its profit is the gap between what the fuel sells for and what the crude costs — the refining margin — and it does not set either price.

Right now that gap is enormous, and the company's own filing explains it in one sentence: conflict has disrupted commodity markets and destroyed refining capacity, so there is less ability to make fuel than there is demand for it. In the June quarter Valero earned $3.7 billion. In the same quarter a year earlier it earned $714 million.

The shares have more than doubled in a year — up 132% against the market's 24%.

Two things happened to Valero's own plants during this. In March, a fire shut down the entire Port Arthur refinery, one of its largest; it was back to normal by the end of June, and insurance is expected to cover most of the cost. And separately, the company permanently stopped making fuel at its Benicia refinery in California. So Valero earned a record quarter while one plant burned and another closed.

What management is doing with the money is straightforward: buying the stock. It has retired 28% of its shares in five years, and on 16 July it authorised another $5 billion of buybacks on top of $1.4 billion still available — about 7% of the whole company.

Here is the catch, and it is the same one every refinery investor faces. The shares look cheap: about eight times what analysts expect Valero to earn this year. But those same analysts expect earnings to fall by nearly half over the following two years as the margin normalises. On the 2028 forecast you are paying sixteen times, not eight. Refining shares look cheapest exactly when the cycle is at its best.

The average analyst target is $310.20. The shares closed at $308.73. We think fair value is about $310.

There is no edge here in either direction, and pretending otherwise about a company whose profits depend on a war would be dishonest.

  • Downside Risk 7/10. A cyclical extreme, plus a refinery fire and a refinery closure in one half-year.
  • Growth Quality 4/10. No growth; a cycle and a 28% share-count reduction in five years.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. Refining. Renewable diesel revenue fell 6.2% last year.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $310 against a current price of $345.64 — a premium price for a business we still like.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, We don’t publish a reverse-DCF cross-check for pre-profit companies — negative or missing earnings break that math — so take this number on our modeling alone.

4b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (MINUS 29.2%, from FY2026E $37.660 to FY2027E $26.675) + multiple re-rating (mechanical EXPANSION from 8.2x to 11.6x at a constant price) + shareholder yield (+8.0%).

As with Marathon Petroleum, the forward-multiple rolldown here is MECHANICAL and is not re-rating. 8.2x becoming 11.6x as the estimate year advances at a constant price is arithmetic, and the format guardrail exists precisely for this case. The real question is the multiple the market pays on the then-current year.

Our base of $310 is 11.6x FY2027E — exactly the multiple implied by today's price on next year's number. We assume no re-rating in either direction, and the result is that a 29% consensus earnings decline is almost exactly offset by an 8% shareholder yield and a share count falling ~5% a year.

That equilibrium is the finding: Valero's capital return is large enough to neutralise the consensus earnings decline and not large enough to produce a return. It is the identical arithmetic to Marathon Petroleum in this batch, at a slightly lower absolute yield and a slightly higher forward multiple.
