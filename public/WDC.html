The Overview

Every photograph, video, backup and AI training file in the world has to physically live somewhere. Most of it lives on hard drives — spinning magnetic discs in a sealed box — because they are still by far the cheapest way to store enormous amounts of data that you do not need instantly. Western Digital makes those drives. So does Seagate. So, in smaller volume, does Toshiba. That is essentially the whole industry.

Until February 2025, Western Digital was also one of the world's big makers of flash memory chips, the faster and more expensive kind of storage in your phone. It split that business off into a separate listed company called Sandisk, giving most of the Sandisk shares to its own shareholders and keeping about a fifth of them. This matters enormously for reading the numbers, for two reasons.

The first is that the company's revenue appears to have collapsed — from about $18.8 billion four years ago to about $9.5 billion. It did not collapse. Roughly half the company left. What remains is smaller and, right now, much more profitable.

The second reason matters more. Western Digital kept a slice of Sandisk shares, and Sandisk's share price has gone up enormously. Accounting rules require Western Digital to re-value that leftover stake every quarter and put the change through its profit line. Over the last nine months that produced $4.45 billion of paper profit — money the company has not earned from selling anything, and on which it pays no tax. Its actual hard-drive business earned about $2.9 billion of operating profit over the same period. So when a stock screen tells you Western Digital trades at 32 times earnings, it is dividing the share price by a number that is more than half paper gains on someone else's shares. Divide by what the drive business actually earns and you get something between 66 and 77 times.

The drive business itself is doing very well, and the reasons are in the filings, not in a press release. In the three months to April, it shipped 34% more data capacity than a year earlier and charged 9% more for each unit of it. Its profit on each sales dollar went from about 40 cents to about 50 cents. Cloud customers — the big data-centre operators — are 89% of its sales and are now signing longer agreements, which is a genuinely good sign.

The problems are three. Three customers alone are 43% of sales, so losing one is not a rounding error. About a third of the growth is higher prices rather than more units, and prices in this industry have swung violently up and down for forty years. And the shares have risen 617% in twelve months, which means a great deal of good news is already paid for.

Our estimate of what the shares are worth is $524. They cost $548.62. That is our number sitting below the price, which almost always means wait. The average analyst says $641, but the range of analyst forecasts for next year's profit runs from $11.71 to $22.75 a share — they genuinely do not know. And the company reports its full-year results tomorrow morning.

  • Downside Risk 8/10. No debt problem at all, but a share price up 617% in a year, three customers making up 43% of sales, and a results announcement in one day.
  • Growth Quality 8/10. Real growth, driven mostly by shipping more, not just charging more — and confirmed in the filings rather than asserted.
  • Exponential Potential 5/10. The world's data keeps growing, which helps. But this is a commodity made by three companies, and one credible voice in our research base argues flash will eventually replace hard drives entirely.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $524 against a current price of $468.25 — real upside if our numbers are right.

1. What the business is, and how the disclosure actually works

Western Digital describes itself in the 10-K as operating "in the data storage industry", with strengths in "innovation, areal density and cost leadership". Chief executive Irving Tan; approximately 40,000 employees at the end of fiscal 2025, across 24 countries, with approximately 88% in Asia Pacific, 11% in the Americas and less than 1% in Europe, the Middle East and Africa. Roughly 4,500 active patents.

The single most important structural fact is the separation. From the 10-Q:

> "On February 21, 2025, the Company completed the Separation through a pro rata distribution of 80.1% of the outstanding shares of Sandisk to Western Digital stockholders... the Company's retained ownership interest, which was initially 19.9%... As a result of the Separation, Sandisk became an independent public company and Western Digital no longer consolidates Sandisk into the Company's financial results. The historical results of operations of Sandisk are now reported in the Company's Condensed Consolidated Financial Statements as discontinued operations for all periods prior to the Separation."

Everything that looks like decline in this company's history is that sentence. Revenue of $18.793B in FY2022 and $9.520B in FY2025 are not the same company measured twice.

Competition and technology — and the one distinction that matters most

From the 10-K:

> "Our competitors include HDD competitors such as Seagate Technology Holdings plc, and Toshiba Electronic Devices & Storage Corporation along with NAND flash suppliers that provide and enable alternative storage technologies."

Two direct competitors. And the technology bet is disclosed:

> "Our multi-year product roadmap for high-capacity HDDs, which include ePMR, OptiNAND, UltraSMR and triple stage actuators..."

Heat-assisted magnetic recording is not on that list. Its principal competitor leads with that technology. This file cannot tell you which bet wins, and we will not pretend otherwise — but a reader holding both HDD names should understand they are not the same technology exposure, only the same demand exposure.

The vertical integration is real and is the moat: "We develop and manufacture substantially all of the recording heads and magnetic media used in our HDD products." Assembly and test are concentrated in Prachinburi and Bang Pa-In (Thailand); Penang, Johor Bahru and Kuching (Malaysia); Laguna (Philippines); Shenzhen (China); and San Jose and Fremont, California.

The vendor's arithmetic is not wrong — netProfitMarginTTM of 55.07% is exactly what you get when you divide $6,486M by $11,777M. It is the economics that are wrong. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return over the next twelve months decomposes as: EPS growth (+64.3%, from FY2027E $18.696 to FY2028E $30.717) + multiple drift (COMPRESSION, from 29.3x to roughly 18x on the respective forward year, −38%) + shareholder yield (+1.1%) ≈ −4% to +12%, with enormous variance.

Our base case assumes severe multiple compression, and it is not a rolldown artefact — it is the correct treatment of a cyclical at a cycle high. Today the market pays 29.3x on the next fiscal year. In twelve months, if it pays 18x on the then-next fiscal year, that is a genuine de-rating. We assume it for three reasons, all of which are on the page above: growth decelerates on the consensus path itself (+42.7% then +36.1%); the earnings being capitalised are peak-cycle earnings produced by a pricing environment the company's own disclosure shows accelerating; and the industry has a forty-year record of the market refusing to pay a secular multiple for it. Our knowledge base contains that argument in a single sentence from Josh Brown, and it is the most useful thing in the lane: don't mistake memory names' single-digit price-earnings ratios for cheapness — they are the most cyclical names on the planet with wildly swinging operating margins, so they deserve a discount.

Almost all of the potential return is earnings growth, and the multiple is fighting it hard. If the multiple simply HELD at 29.3x on FY2028E the price would be $900 (+64%); if it compressed to 15x the price would be $461 (−16%). The spread between those two outcomes is more than the entire market capitalisation of most companies, and it sits in a variable we do not control.

The bull case at $773 requires no multiple expansion at all — it is 34x applied to the FY2027 consensus high of $22.748, i.e. an earnings beat plus a mild re-rating. The bear case at $265 is the mirror image: 22.6x applied to the FY2027 consensus low of $11.709.
