The Overview

A bank is a business that borrows money cheaply and lends it out for more. Wells Fargo borrows from ordinary people and companies — that is what a deposit is, a loan from you to the bank — and lends it to homebuyers, car buyers, credit-card users and businesses. The difference between what it pays depositors and what it earns on loans is called net interest income, and it was $12.3 billion in the three months to June 2026. It also charges fees for things like managing investments, running company payments and arranging deals, which brought in another $10.3 billion in the same three months.

This matters for how you value it, and it is the single most important idea in this report. For an ordinary company, borrowing a lot of money is a warning sign — it is a choice the company made, and it makes the company riskier. For a bank, borrowing money is the business. A bank with no borrowings would be a bank with no deposits, which is to say not a bank. So the standard investment measures that add up a company's debts and subtract its cash — "enterprise value", "net debt", "EV/EBITDA" — produce numbers here that mean nothing at all. The data file we work from computes them anyway. It says Wells Fargo has an enterprise value of $523 billion and owes 7.7 times its annual profits. Both figures are arithmetically correct and economically meaningless, and we throw them out rather than printing them.

The measures that do work for a bank are simpler. How much do you pay per dollar of profit? $12.75 for every dollar of last year's profit — cheap by the standards of the stock market as a whole. How much do you pay per dollar of the bank's own net worth, after stripping out the accounting goodwill left over from old takeovers? About $1.92 for every $1 of tangible net worth. How much does the bank earn on that net worth? About 16 cents a year on every dollar, which is good. And how much of every dollar of revenue does it spend running itself? That is the "efficiency ratio", and it was 60 cents in the June quarter, down from 64 cents a year earlier — a genuine improvement, and the clearest evidence in the file that the cost-cutting is working.

The other big thing that has changed is regulatory, and it is worth understanding. In 2018, after a series of scandals, the Federal Reserve did something it had never done to a bank this size: it forbade Wells Fargo from growing. The bank was frozen at roughly $1.95 trillion of assets for seven years while its competitors grew. That cap was lifted on 3 June 2025, and on 5 March 2026 the Federal Reserve cancelled the underlying order entirely. Since then the balance sheet has grown by $133.6 billion in six months. We checked both of those dates in the company's own filings rather than trusting memory, because getting this wrong in either direction would invalidate the whole report.

The last thing to know is that the company is handing back a very large amount of cash. In the first six months of 2026 it bought back 84 million of its own shares for $7.1 billion and it has permission to buy $22.7 billion more. On 28 July it raised the quarterly dividend to 50 cents a share. Between the two, roughly 7.6% of the company's entire stock-market value is being returned to shareholders every year. That is unusual and it is the main reason to own it.

Our estimate of fair value is $99. The shares are $88.39. That is a 12% gap, and the average analyst says $98.14 — almost exactly the same. But here is the honest part: our $99 does not come from expecting the bank to earn more than everyone thinks. It comes from expecting the stock market to pay a slightly higher price for the same earnings, because the regulator's punishment is over. That is a judgement about sentiment, not about the business, and it might simply not happen.

  • Downside Risk 5/10. Capital and liquidity are comfortably above what the regulator requires and credit is currently getting better, not worse — but this is a $2.3 trillion balance sheet, and bank shares fall hard and fast when a credit cycle turns.
  • Growth Quality 5/10. Revenue has crept up 2.4% a year for five years; the real improvement is in costs, and the most recent quarter was the best in two years.
  • Exponential Potential 2/10. It is a 174-year-old bank. There is no exponential here and we are not going to invent one.

Putting a number on it: our fair-value estimate is $99 against a current price of $88.39 — real upside if our numbers are right.

What does “fair value” mean?

Fair value is Synthos’s estimate of what one share is worth today, based on our model of the company’s future cash generation and the risks to it. It is not a price target or a prediction of where the stock trades next quarter — it’s the price where we think risk and reward are balanced. Above it, you’re paying for outcomes better than our base case; below it, the market is offering a margin of safety.

The model’s inputs and weightings are proprietary — they’re the product. What we publish is the output, plus an independent, public-math cross-check further down the page (“Check our number”) so you can judge it for yourself.

Items that flatter the 2026 comparison by depressing the 2025 base:

  • Q1 2025 investment securities repositioning loss. The 10-Q states first-half net gains from debt securities "increased driven by the impact of a repositioning of our investment securities portfolio in first quarter 2025." The size is in a stripped table.

  • H1 2025 favourable mortgage servicing rights valuation adjustments, "including for higher expected escrow balances." Size not disclosed in prose.

Net pre-tax drag of $55 million in the quarter and $117 million in the half. This is a permanent reduction in the revenue base, not a one-off, and we do not add it back — but it means the underlying revenue growth is slightly better than reported.

Items that flatter the 2026 quarter and that we CANNOT size:

  • Net gains from equity securities "increased reflecting improved results from our venture capital investments, including higher realized and UNREALIZED gains, partially offset by higher impairment losses." This is the defect-class-3 candidate and it is precisely the pattern that produced the fake 94% net margin at another name in this programme: unrealised marks on a private portfolio flowing through the income statement as earnings. 6b. The return bridge (why the multiple moves)

Expected return decomposes as: EPS growth + multiple drift + shareholder yield. For Wells Fargo the decomposition is unusually lopsided and we will not disguise it.

Our base fair value of $99 is 12.4x the FY2027 consensus of $7.972, applied to today's estimate. Today the market pays 11.09x the same estimate. So the entire +12.0% base-case gap is multiple re-rating — 11.09x to 12.4x — with zero contribution from estimate revision. Earnings growth accrues separately, as the market's forward year rolls from FY2027 to FY2028, and that rolldown is mechanical and is explicitly NOT counted as re-rating.

Stated as arithmetic over the next twelve months:

  • If the multiple simply HOLDS at 11.09x and the market's forward year rolls to FY2028E of $9.158, the price is $101.6 (+14.9%), plus 2.26% of dividend received. That is the mechanical case and it requires no opinion from us at all.
  • If the multiple holds at 11.09x on the SAME FY2027 estimate, the price is unchanged at $88.39. Flat.
  • Our base assumes a genuine re-rating to 12.4x, which is +11.8% on the same estimate.

Why we assume expansion, and it is the one judgement in this dive that is not arithmetic. Wells Fargo traded at a persistent discount to JPMorgan and Bank of America for seven years for one specific, identifiable, disclosed reason: it was the only large US bank operating under a Federal Reserve order capping its total assets. That order's growth limitation was removed on 2025-06-03 and the order itself was terminated on 2026-03-05, both filing-verified. A discount applied for a reason should narrow when the reason is removed. The balance sheet has already grown $133.6 billion in the six months since, and the operating evidence has arrived alongside it — a 60.39% efficiency ratio, ~17.7% annualised return on tangible common equity, and credit improving on every disclosed measure.

But we name it as the fragile leg, without qualification. A re-rating is a judgement about what other people will pay, not about what the company will earn. If we are wrong about the multiple and right about everything else, the return is the mechanical rolldown — roughly +15% over a year, plus the dividend — which is still acceptable but is not our thesis. If we are wrong about both, the shareholder yield of ~7.6% is the floor under the outcome, and it is a real floor: at the current pace roughly 5.3% of the shares outstanding are being retired every year, which raises per-share earnings mechanically regardless of what the multiple does.

Contrast with the alternative structure: in the highest-quality names in this batch, most of the return is earnings growth and the multiple is fighting it. Here it is the reverse: the earnings are pedestrian and the multiple is the whole trade. That asymmetry is exactly why this is Buy — Tactical rather than Buy — Core.
